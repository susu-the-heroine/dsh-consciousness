## Abstract

Testosterone is an essential endogenous hormone in women, exerting physiological actions in reproductive and non-reproductive tissues. Despite the increased demand for the use of testosterone for an array of menopausal symptoms beyond sexual functions, there is a lack of robust evidence on its impact on cardiovascular and musculoskeletal health and cognition. The aim of this review is to provide an overview of evidence-based indications of testosterone as part of menopausal hormone replacement therapy (HRT) in women and highlight the uncertainties, evidence gaps, and future research priorities. This narrative review synthesised current evidence on the physiological role of testosterone, indications for testosterone as part of HRT, clinical efficacy, formulation and prescribing, and safety profile. Testosterone declines across the adult female lifespan until the late sixth decade, and the menopausal transition itself has no meaningful independent impact on testosterone concentrations. Transdermal preparations maintaining testosterone levels in the physiological range are generally safe, with potential side effects of acne and hair growth. The existing high-quality evidence, including national and international guidelines, recommends that hypoactive sexual desire disorder (HSDD) in postmenopausal women is the only evidence-based indication for use; the evidence does not support use for any other symptom, nor for disease prevention.

## Lay summary

Testosterone, widely known as the male hormone, is also produced naturally in women and plays an important role throughout their lifespan. There has been a growing use of testosterone for a range of symptoms in menopausal women, including low sexual desire, low mood, fatigue, poor memory, and heart and bone health. This review brings together the current evidence and guidelines on testosterone as part of hormone replacement therapy (HRT) in menopausal women, including its impact on reproductive and non-reproductive tissues; when it is indicated to be prescribed and in what forms; and safety and gaps in existing evidence. We found that there is high-quality evidence to support the use of testosterone for low sexual desire where conventional HRT has not helped. Transdermal preparations are recommended with no serious side effects at testosterone levels normally found in women. There is insufficient evidence to support its use for brain, heart, and bone health. This represents an area of focus for future research.

## Introduction

Testosterone is a physiological endogenous female hormone produced by the ovaries and adrenal glands, and from peripheral pre-androgens, exerting effects directly via the androgen receptor and indirectly via aromatisation to oestradiol. There is a growing interest in the role of ‘physiological testosterone’ supplementation as part of hormone replacement therapy in relation to the menopause transition. The menopause transition can have a significant impact on women, with a high burden of symptoms in 3 out of 4 women, severe symptoms in one-quarter, and one-third experience long duration of symptoms beyond 7 years ( Hamoda et al. 2024 ). Unlike oestradiol levels, testosterone declines across the reproductive lifespan in women, and the menopause transition has no meaningful impact on the decline in testosterone concentrations until the sixth decade of life ( Wang et al. 2025 a ). There are compelling data based on high-quality evidence on the role of testosterone in the management of hypoactive sexual desire disorder (HSDD) ( Islam et al. 2019 ). However, there remains a dearth of robust evidence on the role of testosterone therapy in supporting cardiovascular, musculoskeletal health, and cognitive functions ( Wierman et al. 2014 , Davis et al. 2019 , Islam et al. 2019 ).

In addition, there is a growing presence of online information on HRT: two-thirds of it falls outside BNF/NICE recommendations, with conflicts of interest related to private practice, online clinics, and pharmaceutical industry funding or retail products marketed at the menopause ( McCartney et al. 2025 ). This pattern of misinformation has translated into a steep rise in demand and more than a 50% rise in use of testosterone in women aged ≥50 years ( NHS Business Services Authority 2023 ). The widening use of testosterone has extended beyond its evidence-based indication for HSDD to indications including cognition, mood, and musculoskeletal and cardiovascular health, for which there is a lack of robust evidence ( Islam et al. 2019 , Geddes 2024 a , b ). Within this context of rising demand for ‘physiological testosterone supplementation’ worldwide, there is limited availability of licensed female-specific formulations of testosterone.

The aim of this review is to provide an overview of evidence-based indications of testosterone therapy in postmenopausal women and highlight the uncertainties, evidence gaps, and future research priorities.

In this review, the word ‘women’ refers to people assigned female at birth.

## Methods

A systematic search of the literature underpinning a narrative synthesis was performed by one reviewer from inception to 21 June 2026 in the following databases: MEDLINE, EMBASE, and Cochrane Library. English-language publications were searched using a combination of Medical Subject Headings and the following keywords: ‘menopause’, ‘testosterone’, ‘hypoactive sexual desire disorder’, ‘vasomotor symptoms’, ‘mood’, ‘sleep’, ‘cognition’, ‘fatigue’, and ‘musculoskeletal symptoms’. Details of search strategy and database results are outlined in Supplementary Tables 1, 2, 3 (see section on Supplementary materials given at the end of the article). A manual search of the reference lists of selected articles was also performed to identify any missing papers with relevant data not identified by the electronic search. Database searches identified 7,637 records. After title and abstract screening of 5,819 records, 117 full-text articles were obtained that met the study’s eligibility criteria. Nineteen records, including meta-analyses, randomised controlled trials, observational studies, and guidelines examining the role of testosterone as part of hormone replacement therapy (HRT) in menopausal women, were included. Data synthesis was performed as a narrative literature review across five key areas: physiology of testosterone in women, indications for testosterone as part of HRT, clinical efficacy, formulation and prescribing, and safety profile. All screening, selection, and extraction were performed by the same reviewer.

## Physiology of testosterone in women

In females, ovaries and adrenal glands are the main sources of circulating androgens, testosterone, androstenedione, and dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA) ( Davis 1999 b ). In girls aged between 6 and 9 years, maturation of adrenal zona reticularis, resulting in the production of DHEA and DHEAS, leads to the onset of adrenarche and a rise in testosterone ( Davis et al. 2011 , Davis & Wahlin-Jacobsen 2015 ). Throughout the reproductive years, testosterone is produced cyclically with the onset of ovulation by theca cells in the ovary under the influence of luteinising hormone (LH), with peak concentrations in mid-cycle ( Davis & Wahlin-Jacobsen 2015 ). Approximately 50% of total circulating testosterone is derived from peripheral conversion of adrenal pre-androgens ( Davis 1999 b ). Testosterone is further metabolised to the potent dihydrotestosterone by the enzyme 5-alpha reductase (DHT) ( Davis 1999 b ). Most circulating testosterone is bound to sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG) or albumin. It is the free, non-protein-bound fraction that is biologically active, accounting for approximately 1–2% of total circulating testosterone; the bioavailable fraction, comprising free plus albumin-bound testosterone, is substantially larger ( Rannevik et al. 1995 , Davis 1999 b ). After menopause, adrenal (80%) and ovarian (20%) pre-androgens, such as DHEA, are the main sources of testosterone ( Labrie 2015 ). Testosterone levels follow a circadian rhythm with peak concentrations in the morning ( Davis & Wahlin-Jacobsen 2015 ). The production of testosterone in premenopausal women has been estimated to be approximately 0.2–0.25 mg/day with median circulating testosterone levels similar to those of oestradiol and oestrone, ranging from 0.29 to 1.01 nmol/L ( Parish et al. 2021 , Wang et al. 2025 a ).

Throughout the reproductive lifespan, free testosterone and pre-androgen levels decline with age and precede menopause. Testosterone levels decline by almost 25% between ages 18 and 39 years ( Wahlin-Jacobsen et al. 2015 , Skiba et al. 2019 , Wang et al. 2025 a ). The decline in testosterone levels is preceded by a decline in mid-cycle ovarian androgen production ( Mushayandebvu et al. 1996 , Davis 1999 b ).

A recent cross-sectional study has concluded that blood testosterone concentrations measured precisely using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) decline by a further 25% from the age of 40 years until the age of 58–59 years, after which concentrations increase with age, while androstenedione and DHEA decline continuously from the age of 40 years ( Wang et al. 2025 a ). This also highlights that testosterone declines across the adult female lifespan and that the menopause transition has no meaningful impact on testosterone concentrations. In women aged 55–75 years with a history of bilateral oophorectomy, a steep decline in testosterone is noted compared with women without a history of bilateral oophorectomy ( Davison et al. 2005 , Wang et al. 2025 a ). This further attests to ongoing ovarian production of androgens many years beyond the time of natural menopause ( Davison et al. 2005 , Wang et al. 2025 a ). The unexplained rise in testosterone levels in the late sixth decade may reflect a change in intracrine enzymatic conversion of DHEA, governed by peripheral enzyme activity rather than precursor availability.

Physiological actions of testosterone in reproductive and non-reproductive tissues are mediated directly via androgen receptors after conversion to dihydrotestosterone in skin, sweat glands, hair follicles and genital skin and indirectly by aromatisation into oestradiol in brain, bone, adipose tissue, skin, vascular endothelium and smooth muscle, ovary and placenta ( Davis 1999 b , Davis & Wahlin-Jacobsen 2015 ). Tissue sensitivity to androgens varies according to the amount and activity of 5-alpha reductase and aromatase and tissue androgen receptor sensitivity ( Davis 2024 ).

## Sexual function

The peak prevalence of low sexual desire, arousal, orgasm, or sexual responsiveness corresponds to a drop in testosterone until about 58–59 years of age ( Wang et al. 2026 ). Testosterone has a direct, androgen receptor-mediated effect in the hypothalamus and limbic system involved in sexual motivation and reward, and it indirectly modulates dopaminergic neurotransmission to enhance sexual motivation and response to sexual stimuli ( Shifren 2004 , Davis et al. 2018 , Parish et al. 2021 ). Its peripheral actions in female sexual arousal include genital vasocongestion and vaginal lubrication, which result from increased blood flow to the clitoris, vulva, labia, and vagina ( Parish et al. 2021 ).

## Vulvovaginal health

The vaginal mucosa and submucosal vascular endothelial cells express androgen receptors and intracrine enzymes, suggesting a potential role in vaginal smooth muscle relaxation and genital blood flow ( Berman et al. 2003 ). Although the androgen receptor mRNA is significantly less expressed than oestrogen receptor α in the vagina, the human vagina is an androgen-target organ with the ability to synthesise androgens from the precursors ( Cellai et al. 2021 ). The density of androgen receptors in the vaginal mucosa declines with age, menopause, and oral/transdermal oestradiol replacement ( Berman et al. 2003 ).

Although animal studies indicate that oestrogen, rather than androgens, regulates blood flow and vaginal lubrication, human studies have indicated a potential role of testosterone in increasing genital blood flow and improving sexual sensation and desire ( Sarrel 1998 , Sarrel et al. 1998 , Min et al. 2002 , Berman et al. 2003 ). In addition, testosterone may also prevent apoptosis and fibrosis of vaginal smooth muscle, stroma, and vascular endothelium and induce proliferation of vaginal epithelium ( Berman et al. 2003 , Davis & Wahlin-Jacobsen 2015 ).

## Cardiovascular

In postmenopausal women, the mechanistic evidence on the association between testosterone and cardiometabolic markers, with some studies suggesting improved endothelium-dependent vasodilation, whereas others demonstrate enhanced vascular contractile responsiveness after short-term testosterone administration ( Worboys et al. 2001 , Golden et al. 2002 , Montalcini et al. 2007 , Maturana et al. 2008 , Mathews et al. 2019 ).

Both endogenous testosterone levels and exogenous testosterone administration have been positively correlated with improvement in endothelium-dependent (flow-mediated) and endothelium-independent (GTN-mediated) brachial artery vasodilation and lower odds of carotid artery atherosclerosis ( Worboys et al. 2001 , Golden et al. 2002 , Montalcini et al. 2007 ). A more recent, well-designed prospective study concluded that in healthy Australian women aged 70 years and older, having blood testosterone and DHEA concentrations above the lowest quartile appears to confer cardioprotective effects ( Islam et al. 2022 ). In addition, evidence indicates that an abrupt decline in testosterone in those who underwent bilateral oophorectomy was associated with reduced baseline blood flow and reduced endothelium-dependent and endothelium-independent dilatation ( Rech et al. 2016 ). Many studies confirm that DHEA-S has anti-obesity properties by its influence on body fat distribution, metabolic profile, and on expression of specific adipokines ( Montalcini et al. 2012 ).

The supportive mechanistic evidence on the cardiovascular efficacy of testosterone is contradicted by other studies that have found a negative association between endogenous testosterone levels and flow-mediated brachial artery dilation and a proatherogenic profile of higher BMI, waist circumference, endothelial dysfunction, and inflammatory markers (CRP and endothelin 1) ( Maturana et al. 2008 , Mathews et al. 2019 ). Higher testosterone levels in women aged ≥65 years have been associated with insulin resistance, metabolic syndrome, and cardiovascular disease ( Patel et al. 2009 ). In addition, female animal studies remain inconclusive, with one study suggesting testosterone-mediated endothelial-dependent vasodilatation to acetylcholine in atherosclerotic and previously hypertensive female animal models, whereas another indicates enhanced vascular contractile responsiveness after short-term testosterone administration to prostaglandins and potassium chloride ( Adams et al. 1995 , Farhat et al. 1995 , Tatchum-Talom et al. 2002 ).

## Cognition

Menopause transition may have distinctive effects on specific neurofunctional domains of cognition, mood, and sleep, although it can be difficult to distinguish between effects of somatic vs reproductive ageing ( Will & Randolph 2009 , Berent-Spillson et al. 2012 ). Certain cognitive domains, such as verbal fluency, are affected during the menopausal transition independent of age ( Berent-Spillson et al. 2012 ).

Androgens, including testosterone, may have an impact on the regulation of memory, learning, myelination, glial function, neuroprotection, and motor performance ( Rosario et al. 2011 , Melcangi 2024 ). Preclinical studies suggest that these effects involve androgen receptor (AR) signalling, neurotrophic factors, and local aromatisation to oestradiol, supporting regulation of neuronal signalling, synaptic plasticity, increased expression of nerve growth factor and brain-derived neurotrophic factor, neurogenesis, neuroprotection, and repair ( Sarkey et al. 2008 ; Roselli et al. 2009 ; Will & Randolph 2009 ).

Observational studies have found a conflicting correlation between endogenous testosterone levels and cognition, with some studies suggesting an improvement in spatial and mathematical ability tasks, verbal memory and neuroprotective effects, whereas other studies are suggestive of an association between hyperandrogenic state and poorer performance on cognitive tasks of verbal fluency, verbal memory, manual dexterity, and visuospatial working memory ( Schattmann & Sherwin 2007 , Wierman et al. 2014 ). The most recent systematic review did not find an association between testosterone and cognitive performance in postmenopausal women, with assay imprecision and methodological heterogeneity limiting interpretation ( Sultana et al. 2023 ). Declining testosterone over 3 years was not associated with a decline in cognitive function in healthy women aged 70 years and older, although lower levels have been linked to poorer cognition in APOE -ε4 (apolipoprotein E ε4 allele) carriers across ageing women ( Dratva et al. 2024 , Sultana et al. 2026 ).

## Musculoskeletal

Androgens collectively affect skeletal homeostasis throughout life in both men and women, particularly at puberty and during adult life ( Clarke & Khosla 2009 ). The current evidence suggests that oestradiol plays a greater role in maintenance of skeletal health than testosterone ( Clarke & Khosla 2009 ). Testosterone stimulates osteoblast proliferation and differentiation and decreases osteoblast and osteocyte apoptosis, via direct interaction with androgen receptors and indirectly via aromatisation to oestradiol, leading to acquisition and maintenance of bone density ( Clarke & Khosla 2009 ).

Bone loss is significantly associated with lower androgen concentrations in premenopausal women, and with lower oestrogens and androgens in peri- and postmenopausal women ( Slemenda et al. 1996 ). Total and free testosterone have been directly associated with higher BMD at the lumbar spine and hip after adjusting for oestradiol and free testosterone with lean body mass ( Rariy et al. 2011 ). In the Women’s Health Initiative Observational Study, in women not using oestrogen or bone active therapies, high serum sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG) was associated with an increased risk of subsequent hip fracture, whereas high bioavailable testosterone was associated with decreased risk ( Lee et al. 2008 ).

## Indications for testosterone as part of HRT

## Sexual health

Early postmenopausal women aged 55–59 years have two times higher prevalence (24%) of female sexual dysfunction (FSD) compared with premenopausal women aged 40–44 years (17%) ( Wang et al. 2025 b ). The association between testosterone and sexual desire is further supported by the data demonstrating a higher prevalence of hypoactive sexual desire disorder (HSDD) in surgically menopausal women aged 20–49 years (26%) with abrupt loss of ovarian androgen than age-matched premenopausal women (14%) ( Leiblum et al. 2006 ). HSDD is defined as persistent or recurrent deficiency or absence of sexual fantasies and desire for sexual activity with marked distress or interpersonal difficulty not otherwise accounted for by a general medical or psychiatric condition ( Parish et al. 2021 ). In the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5), HSDD and female sexual arousal disorder were merged into a single diagnosis, female sexual interest and arousal disorder ( Parish et al. 2021 ). However, female sexual interest and arousal disorder as a diagnostic entity has been controversial and, to date, has not been used in the pharmacological interventional clinical trials of testosterone in women ( Islam et al. 2019 , Parish et al. 2021 ). The complexity of HSDD also lies in its multifactorial aetiology and various methods of assessment of sexual desire ( Davis & Wahlin-Jacobsen 2015 ).

Assessment of low sexual desire includes the use of the validated self-report screening and diagnostic instrument, the Decreased Sexual Desire Screener, that enables the diagnosis of generalised, acquired HSDD and identification of associated biopsychosocial factors ( Parish et al. 2021 ). In addition, sexual history should include sexual desire, arousal, orgasm, and pain, along with history involving partnered and unpartnered sexual activity and past and current partner relationships and sexual experiences ( Parish et al. 2021 ).

As part of initial clinical assessment of sexual function disorders, one should not perform investigations for testosterone and pre-androgen levels for diagnosis of sexual dysfunction or androgen insufficiency ( Wang et al. 2026 ). The association between endogenous androgen concentrations and sexual function remains uncertain due to limitations in androgen assays and the complexity of androgen metabolism ( Davis et al. 2019 , Parish et al. 2021 ). The associations between sex steroids and orgasm and arousal are complex and vary with menopausal status ( Wang et al. 2026 ).

There is consistent evidence from meta-analyses of RCTs on the effectiveness of testosterone for treatment of HSDD in postmenopausal women ( Achilli et al. 2017 , Islam et al. 2019 ). A significant improvement in sexual function is noted with testosterone across several domains, including satisfying sexual episodes, sexual activity, number of orgasms, sexual desire, pleasure, arousal, responsiveness and self-image, and reduced sexual concerns and distress. Tables 1 and 2 outline the evidence and guideline recommendations on indications and efficacy of testosterone for management of HSDD.

It is recommended that testosterone therapy should only be initiated after a full biopsychosocial evaluation and appropriate management of other conditions that may contribute to diminished desire, such as dyspareunia, fatigue secondary to vasomotor symptoms, anaemia, thyroid disease, anxiety and depression, medication side effects, and relationship issues ( Parish et al. 2021 ). NICE guidelines in addition recommend a trial of conventional HRT before testosterone supplementation is considered ( National Institute for Health & Care Excellence 2024 ). Cessation of testosterone therapy is recommended for women who have not responded to treatment by 6 months ( Wierman et al. 2014 ).

## Vulvovaginal health

Menopausal vulvovaginal symptoms are often included in a broader term, genitourinary syndrome of menopause (GSM). GSM encompasses a spectrum of vulvovaginal, urinary, or sexual symptoms and physical changes resulting from declining oestrogen and androgen concentrations in the genitourinary tract during the menopausal transition ( Kaufman et al. 2025 ). Vulvovaginal symptoms associated with menopause include dryness, burning, and irritation ( Kaufman et al. 2025 ). The vulvovaginal effects are often considered the cause of dyspareunia and bleeding during intercourse, reduced libido, arousal, and orgasm ( Kaufman et al. 2025 ).

There is very low-certainty evidence on the use of vaginal testosterone for improvement in vulvovaginal health in postmenopausal women with genitourinary syndrome. Evidence summary is highlighted in Tables 1 and 2 . Both randomised controlled trials had small sample sizes, a limited period of follow-up, and the meta-analysis has not shown any difference between the interventions, testosterone with or without oestradiol compared with oestradiol alone ( Raghunandan et al. 2010 , Fernandes et al. 2014 , Pitsouni et al. 2018 ). Intravaginal testosterone may offer improvement in sexual wellbeing, vaginal dryness, and dyspareunia in women taking aromatase inhibitors ( Davis et al. 2018 ). The most recent trial which examined the direct effect of testosterone on the vulvovaginal tissues was underpowered with limited duration of follow-up ( Davis et al. 2018 ). Prospective studies with underpowered sample size and short duration of follow-up have reported limited systemic hormonal effects of vaginal testosterone; however, these were not designed to assess breast-cancer recurrence or long-term oncological safety. Hence, there is insufficient evidence to establish safety of vaginal testosterone in women with hormone receptor-positive breast cancer ( Witherby et al. 2011 , Dahir & Travers-Gustafson 2014 , Melisko et al. 2017 ). Overall, intravaginal testosterone to support vulvovaginal health offers potential as a treatment option but needs confirmation by a larger clinical trial.

## Cardiovascular

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains the leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide. Menopause represents a watershed in women’s cardiovascular trajectory. The most recent women-centred global epidemiological data on CVD burden in women aged ≥55 years from 1990 to 2021 with projection of trends to 2040 indicate a sharp rise in CVD burden, with ischaemic heart disease and stroke contributing to the majority of the burden ( Xu et al. 2026 ). Over the last three decades, there has been a 99.64% increase in incidence, with mortality rising from 5.59 million to 8.48 million and a 45% increase in disability-adjusted life years (DALY) attributable to CVD ( Xu et al. 2026 ). Weight loss, regular exercise, blood pressure, and lipid and diabetes control are recommended as key primary prevention measures ( Sturdee & Pines 2011 ).

Though mechanistic studies suggest a cardiovascular protective role of testosterone, there is a scarcity of studies on the impact of testosterone on cardiometabolic markers as a primary endpoint. Heterogeneity in population characteristics (age, hormonal status), vascular outcomes, and hormone measurements across observational studies that use cardiovascular outcomes as ‘surrogate’ markers included in the reviews limits the generalisability of conclusions about the cardiovascular efficacy of testosterone. Well-designed, large, prospective studies and trials with long-term follow-up are required to verify the association between testosterone and cardiometabolic events (stroke, venous thromboembolism, and myocardial infarction) as primary endpoints in postmenopausal women with stratification by age, time since menopause, and pre-existing cardiometabolic morbidities. The existing evidence, including national and international guidelines, does not support the use of testosterone for cardiometabolic health protection. ## Cognition

Existing observational studies and RCTs have mixed conclusions on the impact of androgens on cognition, largely due to the complexity of testing all aspects of human cognition ( Shah et al. 2006 , Kocoska-Maras et al. 2011 , Davis et al. 2014 , Huang et al. 2015 , Glynne et al. 2025 ). There are limited studies which are underpowered, with a narrow range of subjective neurophysiological tests to measure the impact and imprecise effect estimates of short-term and long-term testosterone exposure. Tables 1 and 2 highlight the evidence and guideline recommendations on testosterone and cognition.

The existing preliminary data are insufficient to support the use of testosterone replacement therapy for prevention or treatment of cognition, mood, or sleep. In addition, future research using animal models and specialised brain imaging techniques is required to evaluate the potential effects of androgens on brain function.

## Musculoskeletal

Despite the biological plausibility of anabolic effects of testosterone on bone and skeletal muscle, there is paucity of data on the musculoskeletal effects of testosterone. Of the studies that have reported musculoskeletal outcomes, the number of included participants has been small; all participants were taking concurrent oestrogen therapy, and no studies have been in women with osteoporosis ( Davis et al. 2019 , Islam et al. 2019 ). Tables 1 and 2 highlight the evidence, including up-to-date meta-analyses of RCTs and guidelines. There is a need for well-powered clinical trials with validated endpoints to evaluate the impact of testosterone on musculoskeletal health.

## Formulation and prescribing

There is an increase in demand for testosterone as part of HRT, partly driven by a surge of public awareness through social media ( Wickware 2023 , McCartney et al. 2025 ). Until July 2025, there was a lack of female-specific licensed testosterone products in the UK for postmenopausal women. The only female-specific formulation approved by regulatory authorities in Australia, New Zealand, South Africa, and, more recently, by the Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency (MHRA) in the United Kingdom is 1% transdermal testosterone cream ( Medicines and Healthcare Products Regulatory Agency 2025 , Panay 2022 ).

In the United Kingdom, 1% transdermal testosterone cream is still not uniformly available on the formularies of recommended medicines within individual integrated care boards and health boards. In countries where this female-specific formulation is not licensed, male testosterone preparations titrated to female doses (one-tenth) are prescribed ( Panay 2022 ). Using male-specific products has the potential risk of using the incorrect amount of testosterone, as these are difficult to measure and women need only 5–10% of the male dose. In addition, tibolone, which has weak androgenic, oestrogenic, and progestogenic properties, has demonstrated improvements in sexual function in postmenopausal women, although evidence supporting it as a specific treatment for HSDD is limited ( Nijland et al. 2008 ). Oral androgens are not recommended due to their adverse impact on lipid profile ( Islam et al. 2019 ).

It is recommended to at least try testosterone for a short duration of 3–6 months to fully evaluate the efficacy of treatment, with an annual review of ongoing use ( Panay 2026 ).

Testosterone can also be given as a subcutaneous implant, in which a pellet of crystalline testosterone, usually 50–100 mg, is inserted under local anaesthetic into the fat of the lower abdominal wall or buttock and replaced approximately every six months ( Davis 1999 a ; Panay 2026 ). The dose cannot be adjusted or readily withdrawn once the pellet is in place; serum concentrations can exceed the female physiological range in the weeks after insertion, and testosterone should be measured before each re-implantation to avoid accumulation ( Buckler et al. 1998 , Davis 1999 a , Parish et al. 2021 ). These pellets are usually used in combination with oestradiol pellets, most commonly after a total hysterectomy ( Buckler et al. 1998 , Tamblyn et al. 2023 ). No testosterone implant is licensed in the UK, and we rely on the importation of a 100 mg pellet from a company based in the United States ( Panay 2026 ). Supply of this unlicensed pellet was placed on hold after a US Food and Drug Administration inspection in 2024 identified significant risks at the manufacturing site, and at the time of writing, testosterone implants are, therefore, not routinely available in the UK ( Panay 2026 ). ## Monitoring testosterone

Measurement of serum testosterone levels requires careful interpretation. Liquid or gas chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/GC-MS) is a highly sensitive assay for measuring testosterone and DHT. However, these assays do not provide tissue-level measurement of testosterone or tissue sensitivity and responsiveness ( Davis 2024 ).

Monitoring testosterone levels at baseline before starting treatment and at 3 and 12 months after treatment is recommended to ensure physiological testosterone levels within the laboratory reference range during treatment ( Panay 2026 ). The Endocrine Society Clinical Practice Guideline recommends testing at baseline, after 3–6 weeks of initial treatment to assess patient overuse and every 6 months in ongoing treatment to monitor for excessive use and signs of androgen excess ( Wierman et al. 2014 ). Diagnosing androgen deficiency based on testosterone levels is not recommended ( Wierman et al. 2014 , Davis & Wahlin-Jacobsen 2015 , Davis et al. 2019 ). Despite the weak correlation between levels of testosterone and its impact on sexual desires, cognition, and heart and bone health, using testosterone level to diagnose androgen insufficiency is not recommended because, currently, no widely accepted diagnostic lower limit of any androgen has been identified to characterise a woman as ‘androgen deficient’. In addition, total testosterone should be measured using an accurate method, such as liquid/gas chromatography and tandem mass spectrometry assays ( Davis et al. 2019 , Panay 2026 ).

The free androgen index (FAI) was historically used as a surrogate to estimate free testosterone and assess the adequacy of testosterone therapy ( Parish et al. 2021 ). However, the free androgen index (total testosterone / SHBG × 100) does not account for testosterone bound to albumin and the complexity of SHBG binding and, as a result, does not always reflect free testosterone accurately ( Goldman et al. 2017 , Parish et al. 2021 ). For example, low SHBG would lead to a disproportionate rise in FAI, leading to false overestimation of free testosterone. Hence, FAI should not be used for monitoring and clinical assessment. Most evidence on testosterone efficacy is based on total testosterone levels rather than free testosterone or SHBG to assess treatment response. SHBG may add value in assessing treatment non-response or side effects. High SHBG, particularly with oral oestrogen therapy, reduces the circulating free testosterone and may explain non-response at physiologically normal total testosterone levels ( Parish et al. 2021 , Panay 2026 ). In these cases, consider switching to transdermal oestradiol, and avoid increasing testosterone doses above the premenopausal physiological range to compensate for high SHBG ( Parish et al. 2021 , Panay 2026 ). Conversely, low SHBG may explain androgenic side effects at physiologically normal total testosterone levels ( Parish et al. 2021 , Panay 2026 ).

In conclusion, testosterone efficacy is assessed clinically. Total testosterone levels based on highly sensitive assays are used for monitoring to avoid excessive dosing.

## Safety profile and risks

Testosterone therapy for postmenopausal women is not associated with serious adverse events, in doses that approximate physiological testosterone concentrations for premenopausal women ( Davis et al. 2019 ). However, prescribing testosterone for indications such as cognition, energy, or general wellbeing for which there is no established benefit may expose women to potential harm.

## Short-term risks

These tend to be dose-dependent and may take several months to become evident ( Maclaran & Panay 2012 ). The evidence highlighted below is based on studies with limited follow-up duration and may, therefore, not reflect clinical practice. 
Testosterone is associated with a greater likelihood of acne (RR: 1.46; 95% CI: 1.11–1.92; 11 studies) and hair growth (RR: 1.69; 95% CI: 1.33–2.14; 11 studies) compared with placebo ( Islam et al. 2019 ). In the most recent systematic review and meta-analysis, alopecia, clitoromegaly, or voice change were not noted to be significantly higher in the testosterone arm compared with the comparator ( Achilli et al. 2017 , Islam et al. 2019 ). Though rare, these side effects are apparent at supraphysiological testosterone ranges ( Maclaran & Panay 2012 ).

Testosterone pellets provide long-term delivery but at the cost of supraphysiological early peaks (3.5–8.7 nmol/L; 100–250 ng/dL) and dosing variability ( Pinto da Costa Viana et al. 2025 ). Current guidelines restrict use of testosterone to hypoactive sexual desire disorder (HSDD) with transdermal formulations ( Davis et al. 2019 , Parish et al. 2021 , Panay 2026 ). In addition, there are limited good-quality data on endometrial and breast safety of implants ( Pinto da Costa Viana et al. 2025 ).

Weight gain (mean difference: 0.48 kg; 95% CI: 0.16–0.79; 5 studies) ( Islam et al. 2019 ).

## Long-term risks and safety

Data on long-term safety of testosterone are based on studies with outcomes followed until 24 months ( Wierman et al. 2014 , Davis et al. 2019 ). Due to the short duration of follow-up and limited sample size, there is uncertainty about the long-term safety of testosterone, especially with regard to the risk of cardiometabolic health, breast cancer, and endometrial health. In addition, women at high risk of breast cancer and/or cardiometabolic morbidity have been excluded in these studies, limiting the generalisability of the data on long-term safety. 
Based on pooling of effect estimates of 9 studies in the meta-analysis, oral testosterone is associated with an increase in LDL cholesterol (mean difference: 0.29 mmol/L; 95% CI: 0.04–0.53) and a reduction in total cholesterol (mean difference: −0.32 mmol/L; 95% CI: –0.50 to −0.14), HDL cholesterol (mean difference: −0.40 mmol/L; 95% CI: –0.49 to −0.30), and triglycerides (mean difference: –0.30 mmol/L; 95% CI: –0.49 to −0.12) compared with placebo or a comparator ( Islam et al. 2019 ). Transdermal preparation is not associated with an adverse impact on lipid profile ( Islam et al. 2019 ).

Testosterone was not shown to affect blood glucose or insulin concentrations, blood pressure, or waist-to-hip ratio, although the contributing trials were neither powered nor designed for these outcomes ( Islam et al. 2019 ).

Although there are limited data on the long-term impact of testosterone on breast and endometrial health, testosterone was not associated with change in mammographic breast density; adverse breast health effects such as breast pain, breast tenderness, breast engorgement, breast mass, or breast cancer; and was not associated with an increase in endometrial thickness ( Davis et al. 2019 , Islam et al. 2019 ). In addition, women with a prior diagnosis of breast cancer were excluded from RCTs for HSDD; hence, caution is recommended for testosterone use for HSDD in women with hormone-sensitive breast cancer ( Davis et al. 2019 ).

Testosterone is not associated with more frequent reporting of cardiovascular events (acute myocardial infarction, stroke, deep vein thrombosis, or cardiovascular deaths) ( Islam et al. 2019 ). However, RCTs of testosterone therapy have excluded women at high cardiometabolic disease risk; most have included women taking concurrent oestrogen therapy, and all have been of relatively short duration ( Davis et al. 2019 ). Therefore, recommendations regarding the effect of physiologic doses of testosterone in postmenopausal women on cardiovascular health are not generalisable to a more ‘at-risk’ population or to long-term therapy ( Davis et al. 2019 ). However, evidence supports adverse cardiovascular effects of lifelong androgenic exposure on a selected group of women with androgen receptor gene (CAG) repeat polymorphism and (TAAAA) polymorphism of the SHBG gene promoter, which affects androgen receptor and SHBG gene transcriptional activity, respectively and the severity of coronary artery disease (CAD) ( Alevizaki et al. 2008 ; Saltiki et al. 2011 ).

## Discussion

While the majority of the research in the arena of hormone replacement therapy has primarily focused on oestrogen, a parallel line of evidence has explored the role of testosterone in sexual health, neuroprotection, cognitive performance, cardiometabolic protection, and bone health.

The postmenopausal stage with intact ovaries does not automatically translate into a state of androgen insufficiency. Testosterone declines across the adult female lifespan and starts increasing slightly at age 58–59 years, the menopausal transition having no meaningful impact on testosterone concentrations ( Wang et al. 2025 a ). In addition, there is no universally established definition of ‘androgen deficiency’, and as a result the decision on testosterone replacement therapy should be based on symptoms and signs, as recommended by evidence-based guidelines ( Wierman et al. 2014 , Davis et al. 2019 , Parish et al. 2021 ). Women on testosterone replacement should have baseline testosterone levels checked using an accurate and sensitive assay to ensure it is in the premenopausal physiological range and to avoid the risk of hyperandrogenism with overuse. The existing evidence, including national and international guidelines, does not support the use of testosterone for women for any symptom or condition other than postmenopausal HSDD or for disease prevention.

Although mechanistic and observational studies have found some positive associations between testosterone levels and improvements in cardiovascular and musculoskeletal health, as well as cognitive function, these associations should not be extrapolated to therapeutic benefit of testosterone. As indicated by experimental and mechanistic studies, there is biological plausibility in the role of androgens in the regulation of memory, learning, myelination, glial function and a neuroprotective role, with a reduction in myelin abnormalities and an improvement in motor performance ( Sarkey et al. 2008 , Roselli et al. 2009 , Will & Randolph 2009 , Rosario et al. 2011 , Wierman et al. 2014 , Melcangi 2024 ). Building on this physiological rationale, the most recent prospective data indicated that a physiological fall in testosterone levels in the late menopausal phase in healthy women aged ≥70 years was not associated with deterioration in cognitive function ( Sultana et al. 2026 ). Collectively, these findings, along with most up-to-date RCTs and guidelines, do not support use of testosterone as a preventive measure for cognitive decline in late-life healthy women. The association between higher testosterone levels and improvements in cardiometabolic and musculoskeletal health, as well as cognitive function, does not establish causality and should not be interpreted as an indication for testosterone replacement. In addition, several association studies have methodological limitations and measured testosterone and pre-androgens using immunoassays, which are imprecise and unreliable in the female range. Future studies should use reproducible and accurate measurement techniques using liquid/gas chromatography and tandem mass spectrometry assays (LC/GC-MS/MS).

To date, a limited number of RCTs with small sample sizes and short follow-up periods have not demonstrated a beneficial effect of testosterone on cardiometabolic health, cognition, or musculoskeletal health. The landmark systematic review and meta-analysis published in 2019 concluded that testosterone is effective for postmenopausal women with low sexual desire causing distress ( Islam et al. 2019 ). No effects of testosterone were reported for body composition, musculoskeletal variables, or cognitive measures ( Islam et al. 2019 ). Consistent with this evidence, national and international guidelines recommend that HSDD is the only evidence-based indication for testosterone as part of menopausal hormone therapy and recommend against prescribing it for cognition, cardiovascular and musculoskeletal health.

Prescribing testosterone for cognition, cardiovascular, and musculoskeletal health requires discussion about potential harms along with the absence of established benefits. Acne and hirsutism are some of the short-term risks of testosterone, whereas there is uncertainty about long-term cardiometabolic, breast cancer, and endometrial health, especially in high-risk women. Shared decision-making should explicitly address the above-mentioned risks. Taking the evidence into account, a favourable benefit–risk profile has been established for indication of postmenopausal HSDD only.

There is an unmet need for well-designed studies to evaluate the role of testosterone in cardiovascular, cognitive, and musculoskeletal health. Existing RCTs have limitations of small sample sizes with a limited period of follow-up and determining the outcomes related to cardiovascular events, breast cancer risk, endometrial and ovarian effects, cognitive health, mood, and musculoskeletal health as secondary points. It would be clinically useful to stratify the efficacy and safety data in RCTs by menopausal status, time since menopause, ovarian status (intact vs oophorectomised), concurrent use of conventional HRT (oestrogen with or without progesterone), and baseline morbidities. Future research should examine the role of testosterone in a genetic context in specific subgroups, such as carriers of the APOE-ε4 allele, the strongest common genetic risk factor for late-onset Alzheimer’s disease, in whom lower testosterone concentrations have been associated with poorer cognitive performance, whereas the association is weaker or absent in non-carriers ( Dratva et al. 2024 ).

This review has some limitations. With single reviewer screening and performing data extraction, there is potential for selection and extraction bias. In addition, restriction to English-language publications may have resulted in some relevant evidence being missed. This is a narrative review rather than a systematic synthesis, and no formal risk of bias assessment of included studies was undertaken. Importantly, the conclusions on safety of testosterone should be interpreted with caution. RCT data on safety of testosterone were limited to a follow-up of 24 months or less and women at high risk of cardiometabolic morbidity were excluded. As a result, the available evidence cannot establish the long-term safety of testosterone, particularly in women with cardiometabolic risk factors.

There is a sharp rise in demand and prescribing of testosterone, which is in parallel with the mismatch between online information supporting use of testosterone for non-evidence-based indications and evidence-based national and international guidelines ( NHS Business Services Authority 2023 , Wickware 2023 , Geddes 2024 a , b ). This also risks women making decisions by overestimating the benefits and underestimating the risks of treatment. It is vital that clinicians support ‘shared decision-making’, based on high-quality evidence and discuss the limitations of testosterone menopausal hormone therapy in order to support women to make an informed decision. ## Conclusion

Testosterone declines across the adult female lifespan until the late sixth decade, and the menopausal transition itself has no meaningful independent impact on testosterone concentrations. Transdermal preparations maintaining testosterone levels in the physiological range are generally safe, with potential side effects of acne and hair growth. The existing high-quality evidence, including national and international guidelines, does not support the use of testosterone for women for any symptom or condition other than postmenopausal hypoactive sexual desire disorder (HSDD) or for disease prevention.
