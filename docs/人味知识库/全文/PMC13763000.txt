## Abstract

## Highlights

 What are the main findings? 

Relational wellness is proposed as a dyadic condition of a particular feedback episode: both participants can remain respected and connected while addressing performance truthfully; its dimensions and distinctness require testing.

The proposed SEED dialogue heuristic is a four-move, author-developed prototype for opening brief feedback conversations; it is not yet empirically validated.

 What are the implications of the main findings? 

SEED may help educators connect an observation with its significance and one prioritized action while inviting learner response; urgent safety action takes precedence.

Both learner and educator perspectives, construct development, cultural adaptation, and comparative testing are required before routine implementation

## Abstract

Background: Clinical feedback occurs in hierarchical, time-pressured settings. Objective: This Perspective proposes relational wellness as a dyadic condition of a feedback episode and describes a brief feedback heuristic. Approach: The framework was developed before the focused, non-systematic searches used to check and align specific claims with sources. We compare SEED with established approaches and map its rationale, boundaries, and testable mechanisms. Key Propositions: In a relationally well feedback episode, learner and educator can remain respected and connected while engaging in truthful learning work. The author-developed SEED heuristic comprises See the situation and invite perspective; Explain observed performance and significance; Extend one prioritized next step collaboratively; and Dialogue to check understanding and invite response. Dignity governs all moves. Safety instructions may precede dialogue. SEED overlaps with Ask–Tell–Ask and remains unvalidated. Conclusions: Separate construct development should test the proposed level, dimensions, and discriminant validity of relational wellness using both participants’ perspectives. Co-design, technical and cultural review, feasibility work, and comparative evaluation are required before effectiveness claims.

 Keywords: clinical feedback, dental education, feedback dialogue, feedback literacy, health professions education, medical education, psychological safety, relational wellness, SEED

## 1. Introduction

Clinical education is conducted through relationships as well as curricula. A medical trainee may defend a diagnostic plan during rounds before an unfamiliar team; a dental student may perform an irreversible procedure while a patient, assistant, and faculty member observe. In both settings, feedback must protect patients, identify performance gaps, and support learning within steep hierarchies and limited time. The interpersonal conditions surrounding that feedback influence whether uncertainty is discussed, whether errors are disclosed, and whether learners remain cognitively engaged [ 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 ]. In a longitudinal study of US medical students, mistreatment and perceptions of the learning environment were associated with subsequent burnout, empathy, and career regret [ 6 ]; that study concerns mistreatment and the broader learning environment rather than feedback specifically, and is cited as context only.

The effects of feedback are not uniformly beneficial. Foundational syntheses show that feedback is most useful when it clarifies the task, the current performance, and the next action, whereas feedback that redirects attention toward the self can impair subsequent performance [ 7 , 8 ]. Contemporary health professions scholarship has similarly moved away from a one-way delivery model toward educational alliance, learner agency, feedback literacy, coaching, and dialogue [ 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 ]. The relevant question is therefore not simply whether an educator gives feedback, but what the interaction permits the learner to understand, say, and do next.

We use relational wellness as a proposed lens on small interpersonal moments in clinical feedback. No neurochemical mechanism is inferred from a particular feedback phrase; claims about dopamine responses would require different evidence [ 16 ].

This Perspective has three objectives: (1) to propose a provisional definition of relational wellness and delineate its boundaries with neighboring constructs; (2) to position a proposed brief feedback heuristic against established approaches; and (3) to specify the heuristic’s theoretical warrants, boundaries, risks, and staged research agenda. The principal scope is brief formative feedback in undergraduate and postgraduate medical and dental clinical education. Patient counseling, peer support, mental-health intervention, and formal remediation are intentionally excluded as applications requiring separate evidence and validation.

## 2. Article Type and Approach to Concept Development

This Perspective presents a framework and heuristic developed by the authors before the literature searches reported here. Focused, non-systematic searches on 15–16 September 2026, undertaken after the framework and heuristic had been developed, were used to verify and align sources with pre-existing claims, identify countervailing evidence and comparators, and refine wording; they did not generate the framework. Three separate principal PubMed expressions were used: (“medical education” OR “dental education” OR “health professions education”) AND (feedback OR “feedback culture” OR “feedback literacy” OR coaching OR “educational alliance”); (“medical education” OR “dental education” OR “health professions education”) AND (“psychological safety” OR shame OR “relational well-being” OR “relational wellbeing” OR “learning environment”); and (“medical education” OR “dental education” OR “health professions education”) AND (culture OR “power distance” OR language OR linguistic OR bias OR equity). The principal ERIC expression was (“medical education” OR “dental education” OR “health professions education”) AND (feedback OR “feedback literacy” OR coaching OR “educational alliance” OR “psychological safety” OR culture OR bias OR equity). These are the principal expressions reported by the authors; database filters, result counts, and exact search logs were not retained and are therefore not reconstructed here.

This was a claim-driven source-selection process, with a risk of confirmation bias. Sources were retained when they informed a principal claim, construct boundary, comparator, risk, or hypothesized mechanism. Backward citation chaining examined references in selected foundational and relevant papers, and forward chaining sought later papers citing them; neither was logged as a comprehensive search. A final check on 28 September 2026 aligned citations with the revised wording, rather than serving as a fresh systematic search. The four authors discussed construct boundaries and revised the moves iteratively; the literature-identification method was led by R.K. No protocol, formal screening flow, or risk-of-bias appraisal was performed, and no learner, patient, or external expert participated in development. Every proposed mechanism and script remains an unvalidated hypothesis or illustration.

## 3. Relational Wellness: Definition, Level, and Boundaries

We propose relational wellness as a dyadic condition of a particular clinical feedback episode, in which the learner and educator can remain respected and connected while addressing observed performance truthfully and identifying a safe next action. The episode is the unit of analysis; each participant may perceive it differently. Repeated episodes, supervisory relationships, team climate, and institutional conditions may shape or result from this condition but are distinct levels. This provisional definition does not assume that either person feels comfortable or agrees with the assessment.

Psychological safety was first described at team level [ 1 ] and has been synthesized for healthcare teams [ 17 ], but it applies to more than team climate: it has been studied within workplace feedback conversations, including educators’ approaches that appeared to foster learner disclosure and productive engagement [ 2 ]. Our proposed focus is narrower in content, not necessarily in social scale: whether both parties can sustain respectful, connected, truthful work on a specific observed performance and next action. These features overlap with psychological safety and educational alliance [ 9 ]; a distinct construct has not been demonstrated. Belonging concerns acceptance and membership; relatedness in self-determination theory concerns connection as a basic need [ 18 , 19 ]; relationship-centered care considers relationships across care systems [ 20 , 21 ]. White’s account of relational well-being emphasizes social conditions rather than an individualized feeling [ 22 ]. Educators’ responses to ambiguity and the hidden curriculum may also shape whether trainees voice uncertainty [ 23 ].

The proposed distinction as positioned in Table 1 , is a testable claim, not evidence of novelty: if episode-level relational wellness cannot be measured reliably or distinguished from episode-level psychological safety and educational alliance, it should be folded into those established constructs. Learner and educator reports, observations, and context measures will be needed to test this possibility.

## 4. What Feedback Evidence Supports—And Complicates

## 4.1. Task Focus, Specificity, and Possible Harm

Feedback is not inherently corrective. Kluger and DeNisi found that a substantial minority of feedback interventions reduced performance; their feedback-intervention theory proposes that performance suffers when attention shifts away from the task and toward the self [ 8 ]. Hattie and Timperley similarly organize effective feedback around three questions—where the learner is going, how the learner is going, and what comes next—and distinguish task, process, self-regulation, and self-level feedback [ 7 ]. These findings support observable, task-relevant language and a prioritized next action while challenging the assumption that warmer wording alone improves learning.

The proposed heuristic therefore avoids generic praise and identity labels. “You are naturally gifted” provides less usable information than “You identified the change in hemodynamics and revised the differential before prompting.” Specificity can direct attention toward behavior, reasoning, and standards. It cannot guarantee accuracy, fairness, or uptake.

## 4.2. Relationship, Dialogue, and Learner Agency

The educational-alliance literature reframes feedback as negotiation within a relationship rather than transmission from a credible expert to a compliant recipient [ 9 ]. Feedback literacy further emphasizes learners’ capacities to appreciate feedback, make judgments, manage affect, and take action, alongside educators’ and programs’ responsibilities to create usable feedback processes [ 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 ]. A study of medical students and residents also identifies feedback orientation as a learner attribute relevant to how feedback is taken up [ 24 ]. R2C2 and its in-the-moment adaptation operationalize relationship, reaction, content, and coaching through reflective dialogue and collaborative planning [ 14 , 15 ].

These perspectives expose a weakness in an educator-only script: observation, explanation, and direction are insufficient unless the learner can offer a perspective, test understanding, participate in planning, and revisit the change. SEED is therefore framed as an opening to dialogue. It is not a complete feedback cycle.

## 4.3. Emotion, Shame, and Accountability

Emotion influences attention and meaning, but the framework does not assume that discomfort is harmful or that emotional reassurance is always indicated. Shame becomes educationally relevant when attention shifts from a remediable behavior (“I omitted the allergy”) toward global identity threat (“I am unsafe and do not belong”). Shame experiences in medical training have been associated with concealment, withdrawal, and changes in professional identity [ 25 ]. A dignity-respecting response may reduce avoidable identity threat, but this remains a proposed mechanism rather than evidence of SEED effectiveness.

Accountability remains explicit. Safety-critical action must be stopped; standards and consequences must be named; repeated concerns require documentation and a formal process. Respecting dignity does not mean cushioning every message with praise, delaying an urgent instruction, or promising a favorable outcome.

## 5. Positioning SEED Among Established Approaches

 Table 2 positions the proposed heuristic alongside established models. R2C2-in-the-moment has an empirical and implementation history [ 14 , 15 ]. Ask–Tell–Ask also opens with learner perspective, offers observer feedback, and returns to reflection and planning [ 26 ]. SEED proposes three incremental emphases for testing: explicitly connect the observation to clinical or educational significance; prioritize one immediately actionable next step; and apply dignity as a constraint throughout the exchange. These emphases may already occur in skilled Ask–Tell–Ask practice. Comparative study must test whether their explicit packaging adds value beyond familiar models or merely adds acronym burden.

SEED is also the acronym for the Supporting Educational Excellence in Diversity curriculum [ 29 ], which addresses a different educational purpose. We use “SEED dialogue heuristic” to identify this prototype; stakeholder co-design should consider a more distinctive name.

## 6. The Proposed SEED Dialogue Heuristic

SEED names four observable conversational functions as noted in Table 3 , not four mandatory sentences or an invariant sequence. Dignity is a continuous constraint on S and both E functions as well as D. The distinctive D function is the educator’s explicit check of understanding and invitation to respond, correct the account, or negotiate follow-up after offering a next action. Dialogue can also occur earlier. In urgent hazards, a safety command takes precedence; learner perspective and the closing check can follow once the hazard is controlled.

## 6.1. A Brief Opening and the Required Continuation

For feasibility testing, the author-proposed opening target is approximately 30–90 s, not a validated threshold. A possible sequence is: “How did that go from your perspective? I observed [specific performance]. It matters because [standard, reasoning, or safety significance]. One next step could be [prioritized action]. What did you hear, and what would you try or change?” This resembles Ask–Tell–Ask [ 26 ]; SEED’s proposed explicit additions are the significance link, one prioritized action, and dignity as a constraint throughout. The template is not verbatim language and may be reordered for safety. Understanding, support, and follow-up must be checked.

## 6.2. Medicine and Dentistry: Shared Logic, Different Conditions

SEED is proposed for health professions feedback and illustrated in medicine and dentistry, without independent validation in either. Across settings, hierarchy, uncertainty, and patient-safety obligations can shape feedback. In the authors’ clinical illustrations as positioned in Table 4 , chairside procedural teaching may involve direct patient observation and decisions that cannot simply be undone, and faculty continuity may vary across programs and rotations; these are contextual descriptions by the authors, not findings attributed to the grouped references. Dental education studies address learning climate [ 30 ], expectations about feedback [ 31 ], students’ use of feedback [ 32 ], and assessment concerns [ 33 ]; these sources do not establish every feature of a particular chairside exchange.

Therefore, examples must be technically reviewed within each discipline, and implementation should test whether the same move functions similarly across diagnostic, procedural, and longitudinal supervisory contexts.

## 7. Contextual Limits: Culture, Language, Equity, and Safety

## 7.1. Cultural and Linguistic Variation

Feedback is interpreted through professional, institutional, and cultural contexts. Learning cultures vary in their approaches to authority and correction [ 4 , 34 ]. The scoping review [ 35 ] draws on heterogeneous Asian settings, with variation both within studies and between settings; it does not describe a uniform “Asian” feedback culture or establish transfer to other geographic, linguistic, or cultural contexts. It discusses power distance and collectivism as relevant influences on feedback seeking and interpretation. The effects of specific English phrases, translation, and linguistic pragmatics on SEED remain hypotheses rather than established findings.

Cultural responsiveness therefore requires more than softening tone. Local learners and educators should co-design acceptable prompts and test how authority, disagreement, privacy, and directness influence interpretation in specific settings. Interpreters and bilingual experts should be involved when language adaptation is studied, and translated versions should undergo cognitive interviewing rather than literal substitution alone. Fidelity should be defined by function—observable evidence, significance, an actionable next step, and genuine opportunity for response—not by literal English wording. These recommendations are proposed adaptation principles requiring empirical testing; no claim of international or cross-linguistic generalizability is made.

## 7.2. Equity and Bias

Specificity may make feedback more auditable, but it does not eliminate bias. Narrative evaluations have shown racialized and gendered differences in descriptive language [ 36 ], and race-neutral educational structures can reproduce inequity [ 37 ]. Even a highly specific comment may reflect unequal observation opportunities, a biased standard, stereotype-based interpretation, or differential tolerance for error. Asset framing may broaden what evaluators notice, but it likewise requires attention to context and structural conditions [ 38 ].

Equitable use of SEED would require disaggregated audits of who receives observation, developmental challenge, coaching time, second chances, and formal escalation; calibration around evidence and standards; protected routes to contest inaccurate feedback; and examination of downstream decisions. The heuristic cannot correct structural inequity, mistreatment, inaccessible systems, or retaliation. Institutions remain responsible for those conditions.

## 7.3. When SEED Is Inappropriate or Insufficient

SEED should not delay commands during emergencies or an imminent procedural hazard. It is not a substitute for formal remediation, disciplinary or fitness-for-duty processes, documentation, due process, supervision, or mandatory reporting. It is also insufficient when a learner discloses hopelessness, impairment, harassment, discrimination, or danger; escalation routes vary by institution and jurisdiction and should follow local policy and emergency guidance.

Consent, shared decision making, teach-back, trauma-informed care, and interpreter-mediated communication are distinct clinical domains. Transfer of this educational feedback heuristic to patient care would require separate patient co-design and validation.

## 8. Staged Development and Research Agenda

Development of relational wellness as a construct and evaluation of SEED as a conversational heuristic as proposed in Table 5 , are related but separate work streams. Neither should be treated as validated by the other. Broad implementation or claims about distal outcomes would be premature.

For the construct work stream, paired learner and educator ratings and independent observation should assess episode-level respect, connection, truthful participation, and agreement about next actions, alongside established comparator measures. Because SEED as written specifies educator behavior while relational wellness is defined as dyadic, both work streams should also record learner behaviors (for example, questions, corrections, and disagreement) and learner perceptions rather than inferring them from educator fidelity. For SEED, initial outcomes are specificity, significance comprehension, actionability, co-created plan quality, learner voice, educator experience, time, and fidelity. Record adverse outcomes including scripting, intrusion, obscured standards, suppressed disagreement, inequitable use, and delayed safety action. Distal outcomes should await construct and feasibility evidence.

## 9. Limitations

The framework was developed before the claim-driven searches. Their focused, non-systematic source selection risks confirmation bias, especially when judging the proposed construct against established ones. Search logs, filters, counts, comprehensive screening, and formal appraisal are unavailable. Medicine is better represented than dentistry among the cited feedback sources, and equivalence across disciplines is untested.

Relational wellness and SEED are author-proposed concepts. Neither has undergone learner or educator co-design, expert consensus, content validation, cognitive interviewing, cultural or linguistic adaptation, feasibility testing, reliability assessment, or outcome evaluation. The examples were written by the authors and may be experienced as formulaic, patronizing, intrusive, or technically inappropriate outside the stated context. The paper is written by US-based authors and may embed North American assumptions about hierarchy, individual recognition, and direct dialogue.

SEED overlaps substantially with established models and may not add enough value to justify another mnemonic. The acronym also collides with the Supporting Educational Excellence in Diversity curriculum [ 29 ]. The proposed sequence and mechanisms are hypotheses; all four moves may not be necessary, and a different name or structure may emerge through co-design. No causal, neurochemical, educational, well-being, or patient outcome can be inferred. Finally, respectful communication cannot compensate for workload, mistreatment, biased assessment, unsafe supervision, inadequate mental-health resources, or weak due-process and reporting systems.

## 10. Conclusions

Brief clinical feedback can be immediate and uncompromising while avoiding identity-level judgments. The proposed SEED functions invite perspective, explain evidence and significance, prioritize an action, and check understanding and response. Dignity applies throughout, and urgent safety instructions come first. Whether the proposed episode-level relational-wellness condition is distinct from established constructs remains an open empirical question.

This is an unvalidated prototype that substantially overlaps with existing approaches. Separate construct work and comparison with Ask–Tell–Ask and R2C2 should test whether the explicit significance link, prioritized action, and continuous dignity constraint produce useful differences without unintended effects.
