## Summary

Brain-computer interface (BCI) technology serves as an important bridge between the nervous system and external devices and has shown considerable promise for the diagnosis and treatment of neurodegenerative diseases, whose global burden continues to increase: in 2021, more than 57 million people were living with dementia worldwide, with Alzheimer’s disease (AD) accounting for approximately 60–70% of cases, while an estimated 11.77 million people were living with Parkinson’s disease (PD) in 2021, according to the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021. This growing disease burden, together with the progressive loss of cognitive, motor, and communicative functions, highlights the need for more precise approaches to early detection, functional assistance, and individualized intervention. Conventional diagnostic and therapeutic approaches remain poorly suited to early detection and timely intervention. This paper analyzes the development of BCI technology, from the first detection of electroencephalogram (EEG) signals to the parallel development of invasive and non-invasive systems, and outlines the basic principles of signal acquisition, processing, and decoding, together with the relative advantages and limitations of different approaches. It then focuses on the use of BCI in Alzheimer’s disease (AD), Parkinson’s disease (PD), and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), covering early diagnosis, functional reconstruction, and neuromodulation. The article also discusses the difficulties encountered by BCI technology in terms of technological realization, clinical translation, and ethical considerations, as well as several future research directions, including multimodal fusion, adaptive algorithms, closed-loop control systems, and personalized treatment so as to drive the applications of BCI in precision medicine and in neurological diseases.

 Keywords: brain-computer interface, BCI, neurodegenerative diseases, closed-loop regulation, neuroplasticity, precision medicine

## Introduction

Neurological diseases are among the leading causes of death and disability worldwide, posing an increasingly serious public health challenge. Based on the latest Global Burden of Disease data (Global Burden of Disease Study 2021), approximately 3.4 billion people worldwide were affected by neurological disorders, which caused more than 11 million deaths and resulted in approximately 443 million disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) in 2021. Neurodegenerative diseases (NDs) constitute a major and rapidly growing subgroup within this broader category. Globally, there are over 57 million people living with dementia (2021 estimate), 60–70% of whom have Alzheimer’s disease, and approximately 11.77 million individuals were living with Parkinson’s disease (PD) in the same year; both figures are taken from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021 and are therefore methodologically comparable. 1 , 2 Population growth and aging trends are expected to further increase the incidence and socioeconomic burden of these diseases. 3 

The core pathology of neurodegenerative diseases lies in the irreversible loss of neurons and the progressive disintegration of neural networks, ultimately leading to cognitive decline, loss of motor coordination, and even comprehensive disorder of sensorimotor and autonomic nervous system regulation. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 However, the pathophysiologic mechanism of these diseases remains incompletely understood. They are widely considered the result of a complicated network of multi-mechanisms, such as protein homeostasis disequilibrium (for instance, abnormal aggregation of amyloid-β and α-synuclein, as well as hyperphosphorylated Tau protein), 8 neuroinflammatory response, 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 mitochondrial dysfunction, 13 , 14 epigenetic regulation abnormalities, 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 among others. This complexity accordingly results in two significant predicaments in clinical diagnosis and treatment: one is that biomarkers for early-stage disease are scarce, which frequently leads to delayed diagnosis and thus to a failure to intervene in time; another is that current treatments are largely symptomatic and do not meaningfully slow or reverse disease progression. 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 

In such a context, BCI technology, which enables direct interaction between the brain and external devices, has become a new conceptual framework for diagnosing, treating, and studying neurodegenerative diseases. Brain-computer interfaces (BCIs) are defined here as systems that establish a direct, real-time functional link between the central nervous system and an external device, bypassing peripheral nerves and muscles. Operationally, a BCI must incorporate three core components: (1) the acquisition of neural signals (e.g., action potentials, local field potentials, or scalp EEG); (2) algorithmic decoding of these signals to infer user intent or physiological state; and (3) translation of the decoded information into external device commands or, in closed-loop configurations, therapeutic feedback. Consequently, this review distinguishes true BCI systems from auxiliary technologies: diagnostic biomarkers (e.g., resting-state EEG patterns, FDG-PET metabolism) and wearable motion sensors (e.g., IMUs) are treated as standalone monitoring tools unless they are actively integrated into a real-time decoding-and-feedback loop. Similarly, conventional neuromodulation (e.g., open-loop DBS) is differentiated from closed-loop BCI systems, which dynamically adjust stimulation parameters based on ongoing neural feedback. This three-component operational definition is applied consistently throughout the review: the term “BCI” is reserved for systems meeting all three criteria, whereas standalone diagnostic biomarkers, neuroimaging, wearable sensors, and conventional open-loop neuromodulation are labeled as BCI-enabling auxiliary technologies. Three application categories are therefore excluded from the term “BCI” throughout this review unless they demonstrably close such a loop: (1) offline diagnostic classifiers, including resting-state EEG and resting-state or task-state fMRI biomarkers, which are reported offline and are not connected to any device or therapeutic feedback; (2) conventional neuromodulation delivered with pre-set, open-loop parameters, notably rTMS and open-loop DBS, which lack online decoding of the patient’s own neural state; and (3) ALS diagnostic and monitoring applications, including offline ERD, P300 and SSVEP-quality indices used for cognitive and consciousness assessment, which inform clinical evaluation rather than driving a real-time interface. Table 2 states this classification explicitly for every technology reviewed.

On the one hand, the BCI system directly records and extracts signals from the central nervous system (CNS) and translates them into control instructions to guide peripheral devices; on the other hand, it enables delivery of external information via the BCI system back to neural tissue, namely implementing a closed-loop communication. 23 , 24 , 25 At the diagnostic level, EEG-based biomarkers and machine-learning approaches have shown potential for detecting subtle neural abnormalities associated with neurodegenerative diseases, including AD. 26 At the level of treatment and rehabilitation, BCI may induce cognitive function remodeling by neurofeedback training, 27 , 28 or regulate abnormal neural activity via techniques such as adaptive deep brain stimulation (aDBS) or functional electrical stimulation (FES), which could help patients perform some motor functions or interact with the environment. 24 , 29 These advances suggest that BCI technology may break through the traditional diagnostic and therapeutic model, shifting from simple symptom control to a new intervention pathway based on neural function remodeling.

Although BCI technology has broad application prospects, its clinical translation in neurodegenerative diseases faces many difficulties. BCI still suffers from numerous technical barriers, such as the poor robustness and generalization of decoding algorithms in real clinical settings, as well as practical barriers in clinical application and ethical concerns. In addition, to date, most studies have focused on only a few types of diseases or a single technical path, and a systematic investigation of the application of BCI in various types of neurodegenerative diseases is still lacking. This restricts the pace of development and clinical translational efficiency of BCI technologies in neurodegenerative disorders. To solve the above problems, a cross-disease and cross-technology systematic research framework must be built.

The present article summarizes BCI technology in the context of major neurodegenerative diseases. As illustrated in Figure 1 , we will review, in turn, the history of BCI technology development and its technical principles (such as signal acquisition, processing, and classification algorithms), and make a detailed comparative analysis of invasive, semi-invasive, and non-invasive methods. Then, we highlight applications and recent developments of BCI in the early diagnosis, functional rehabilitation, neuromodulation, and state monitoring in several NDs (such as AD, PD, and ALS). In addition, we will discuss the multi-dimensional aspects across BCI technology and therapeutic strategies in neurodegenerative diseases. Finally, this article systematically analyzes the current technical bottlenecks, clinical translational obstacles, and ethical issues, and looks forward to future research directions, such as multimodal signal fusion, artificial intelligence algorithm optimization, closed-loop systems, and personalized treatment, so as to provide theoretical reference and practical guidance for promoting the further development of BCI technology in precision medicine for neurodegenerative diseases.

## Introduction to BCI

## BCI principles

The essence of BCI technology is to collect brain activity signals effectively and translate them into commands that hardware can recognize and implement. Figure 2 shows that conventional BCI systems usually consist of the following rudimentary phases: signal acquisition, a process of acquiring neural activity by various sensors and transforming it into digital data to be understood by computers and other electronic equipment. 30 Afterward, these digital signals are preprocessed, for example by monitoring, filtering, and artifact removal, to improve the clarity and accuracy of the signals. The neural decoding following this step is the basis of BCI research and application. 31 The processed signals are turned into computer-executable instructions via feature extraction, classification, recognition, and post-processing. The computer then outputs the decoded instruction to be carried out by the external devices. At this stage, the conventional open-loop BCI system has completed one instruction cycle and is able to realize only a one-way (from human to device) output, with no capacity for automatic error correction or environmental adaptation, which leads to poor accuracy in the interpretation of signals. 32 In contrast, the feedback loop added by closed-loop BCI allows users to receive real-time external feedback stimuli and gradually optimize the interactive functions and performance of the device through continuous training. 33 Closed-loop BCI technology can also adaptively adjust stimulation parameters according to changes in user signals, enhancing the practicality of BCIs in complex application scenarios such as therapeutic neuromodulation and functional rehabilitation. 34 
 Figure 2 
Flow-chart of the BCI functional doctrine and signal processing algorithms

The figure depicts the four-stage closed-loop pipeline: 1) signal acquisition, involving non-invasive (EEG, MEG, fNIRS), semi-invasive and invasive methods; 2) pre-processing, which is responsible for A/D conversion, filtering and denoising; 3) feature extraction and classification probing into time domain, frequency domain as well as time-frequency domain using machine learning algorithms (LDA, SVM, and ANN); and 4) control & feedback where decoded commands drive external devices (e.g., wheelchairs, robotic arms), with sensory feedback sealing the real-time adjustment loop.

Based on different signal acquisition methods, BCIs are mainly divided into three categories: fully invasive, semi-invasive, and non-invasive. Each type has its own characteristics in terms of signal quality, risk level, and application scenarios. Table 1 summarizes the acquisition methods, principles, and advantages of BCI signal acquisition methods under this classification.

Non-invasive BCIs require no surgery and acquire signals through sensors attached to the scalp or through non-contact methods. Common signal types include electroencephalography (EEG), functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS), functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), and magnetoencephalography (MEG). The millisecond-level temporal resolution of EEG-BCI is ideal for capturing rapid changes in brain activity, offering advantages such as safety, convenience, relatively low cost, and decent signal quality. Therefore, it can be used in neurofeedback training, attention monitoring, and rehabilitation medicine. While fNIRS-BCI generally offers lower signal quality than EEG-BCI, it is more robust against motion artifacts and ambient electromagnetic interference. Unlike EEG-BCI, it is applied mainly to continuous monitoring of stable brain activity, where safety requirements are high, but response speed is not critical. However, compared with EEG-BCI, which requires high concentration and rapid response, fNIRS-BCI imposes lower demands on the user for continuous decoding of user intent and may have a lower false-trigger rate, which is beneficial for human-computer interaction and state monitoring. fMRI-BCI and MEG-BCI are expensive, but their advantage lies in providing high spatial or temporal resolution non-invasively, making them suitable for experiments and research with high requirements for safety and accuracy, such as brain functional mapping, precise localization of epileptic lesions, and real-time neurofeedback. Currently, multimodal fusion methods (such as the fNIRS-EEG-BCI model) have become a research hotspot, improving classification accuracy and control specificity by integrating different biological signals. However, mainstream EEG-based BCI paradigms each have inherent limitations that restrict their applicability in neurodegenerative disease scenarios: steady-state visual evoked potential (SSVEP)-based BCI requires users to continuously gaze at high-frequency flickering stimuli, which often induces visual fatigue, headache, and even photosensitive epileptic seizures after 30 min of use, making it unsuitable for long-term cognitive training or daily assistive use in patients with AD. Motor imagery (MI)-based BCI relies on users’ ability to stably generate sensorimotor rhythm modulation through imagined movement, but it has a steep learning curve (typically 3–5 training sessions required for proficient use) and is poorly adapted to patients with moderate-to-severe AD or Parkinson’s disease (PD) with executive function impairment, who often fail to produce distinguishable event-related desynchronization/synchronization (ERD/ERS) patterns. Event-related potential (ERP)-based BCIs, such as the P300 speller, depend heavily on users’ sustained attention and visual processing speed, which limits their utility in patients with advanced AD with severe attention deficits.

Semi-invasive BCI mainly records electrocorticography (ECoG) signals. In general, ECoG electrodes are placed on the cortical surface (subdurally) to record local field potentials (LFPs). It has higher spatial resolution and signal fidelity than EEG. 49 However, the risks of craniotomy and the long-term stability of the implant must also be weighed. It is mainly used for medical rehabilitation, such as motor function reconstruction in severely paralyzed patients and precise localization of epileptic foci. Another type of intravascular electrode, Stentrode, inserts the stent electrode array into the cerebral venous system (such as the superior sagittal sinus) through minimally invasive surgery to record LFPs or low-frequency neural activity signals from within the blood vessels. 50 Although its accuracy is not as good as the former, it is expected to become the least invasive clinical-grade BCI interface because it does not require craniotomy, and its position is relatively stable. It can be used for communication and control of external devices in severely paralyzed patients (such as patients with ALS). In general, semi-invasive BCI is suitable for scenarios that require high signal quality but cannot bear the risks of fully invasive procedures, such as epileptic focus localization and BCI control for paralyzed patients. However, a balance needs to be struck between signal quality and safety, because although it reduces the risk of invasive surgery itself, the signal quality is usually not as good as that of a fully invasive BCI, and the long-term safety of the implantation site is also a concern.

Fully invasive BCI involves surgically implanting one or more electrodes directly into the cerebral cortex or deep brain regions to record neural activity signals in specific areas. This method can directly acquire high-resolution, high signal-to-noise ratio neural signals and has strong anti-interference capabilities. Different types of microelectrode arrays and their implantation sites result in different signal acquisition effects and application scenarios. Among these, intracortical recordings from microelectrode arrays (e.g., Utah array, Neuropixels) represent the core of fully invasive BCI; DBS and sEEG are primarily neuromodulation/recording modalities that can be integrated into closed-loop BCI systems. Due to the significant advantages of fully invasive BCI in signal quality, it is particularly suitable for high-precision scenarios such as motor function recovery and speech decoding in paralyzed patients. However, it also increases the difficulty of signal processing and carries higher surgical risks. The stability and safety of long-term implantation (such as signal attenuation and biocompatibility) remain significant challenges.

The preprocessing stage after signal acquisition aims to remove interference components and artifacts and enhance signal quality through analog-to-digital (A/D) conversion, amplification, and filtering. 51 Preprocessing mainly includes two steps: denoising and signal enhancement. Noise mainly includes environmental noise and physiological artifacts (from breathing, heartbeat, blinking, and other movements), which can be removed by low-pass filtering (allowing the retention of low-frequency components but attenuating high-frequency components), high-pass filtering (retaining high-frequency components and omitting lower frequencies), and band-pass filtering (suppressing frequencies outside the defined frequency band). 52 The specific filter used depends on the EEG rhythm that needs to be focused on. Signal enhancement is based on denoising and uses algorithms (such as wavelet transform or adaptive filtering) to enhance the quality of the signal. 53 

Neural decoding mainly includes feature extraction algorithms and signal classification and recognition algorithms. The purpose of feature extraction is to select signals that reliably and reproducibly reflect the user’s intentions. These components can include time-domain features, frequency-domain features, and time-frequency features. Time-domain features include the mean, variance, and peak value of the signal. Frequency-domain (spectral) features include energy variations of specific cortical oscillation frequencies (e.g., β and γ waves) obtained through the Fourier transform. 54 Time-frequency features are a combination of time-domain and frequency-domain information, which could be more abundant by methods such as the wavelet transform. 55 Signal classification and recognition methods mainly fall into linear classifiers (e.g., linear discriminant analysis, LDA), 56 nonlinear classifiers (e.g., support vector machines, SVM, and artificial neural networks, ANN), and other trainable deep learning (DL) models. 57 The aim of this procedure is to enable a BCI system to transform its outputs into control commands—typically for controlling external devices such as a prosthesis, computer cursor, or wheelchair—so that both the precision and the real-time performance of the output can be guaranteed.

Feedback is the fundamental ingredient for closing the loop between the user and the BCI system. It allows users to regulate their own brain activity using information derived from the system, so that both can be optimized. Feedback mechanisms are visual feedback (VF), auditory feedback (AF), and somatosensory feedback (SF). VF is visual feedback displayed on the screen, such as graphics, text, color changes, or animation, to represent the system state or results of operation in order for the user to understand how the system works. AF is used when the user cannot observe the screen or must simultaneously engage in other visual activities, and conveys real-time operating information through sound or audio signals; however, AF is susceptible to background noise. SF provides the most direct sensation and feedback of direction, speed, and related parameters through sensory modalities such as tactile perception, pressure sensing, temperature sensation, vibration, and the sense of movement. Nonetheless, its implementation is complex and expensive, and the framework requires further hardware support. BCI feedback mechanisms have wide applications in medical rehabilitation and neuroscience research. For example, the BCI-FES system provides multimodal feedback to promote motor recovery in stroke or paralyzed patients, guiding participants to adjust their brainwaves to complete specific tasks through VF.

## BCI history

The discovery of brain waves is usually regarded as the beginning of the development of BCIs. 58 In 1875, British physiologist Richard Caton first detected electrical activity in the cerebral cortex of rabbits and monkeys. In 1913, Vladimir Vladimirovich Prawdicz-Nieminski first recorded seven different types of changes in bioelectrical activity in the animal brain and named them electrocerebrogram. 59 In 1924, German psychiatrist Hans Berger first recorded human brain waves (EEG) non-invasively and subsequently defined α and β rhythms in his seminal 1929 publications, becoming known as the “father of EEG”. 60 

Subsequently, the academic community gradually shifted its research on brain waves from theory to application. In 1963, Gray Walter had patients control the switching of slides through brain waves, which was the first prototype of BCI. In 1969, Eberhard Fetz’s research showed that monkeys could learn to control the firing rate of individual neurons through training, which was the first real BCI experiment and inspired later BCI research based on neuronal activity. 61 

These early applications led to the development of a true BCI. In the 1970s, UCLA conducted experiments on animals to develop new direct communication pathways between the external environment (or device) and the brain. In 1973, Jacques J. Vidal first proposed the concept of BCI and published the landmark paper Toward Direct Brain-Computer Communication , which pointed the way for the development of BCI technology and proposed a design framework for BCI systems. 62 

In the following decades, BCI systems have made progress in signal detection and processing, and control of motor devices. In 1977, Vidal developed a BCI system based on visual event-related potentials (ERPs). By having users gaze at different locations of the same visual stimulus, the system can identify and decode the control commands corresponding to these gaze behaviors. This was the first application after the term BCI was formally proposed, marking the transition of BCI technology from theory to practice. In 1978, William Dobelle implanted an array of 68 electrodes in the visual cortex of a blind patient named Jerry. By connecting a camera and activating the electrodes, he successfully induced phosphenes in the patient, marking the first major application of BCI technology in humans. 63 In 1988, Farwell and Donchin proposed the famous “P300 speller” paradigm, which allowed people with severe motor disorders to communicate by recognizing selected letters on a screen; these letters were associated with the P300 ERP waveform elicited by decision-making. In 1992, Erich E. Sutter developed a BCI system based on visual evoked potentials (VEPs), and in the mid-1990s, SSVEP-based BCIs were further developed by several groups. In 1998, Philip Kennedy first implanted a BCI into a human body, allowing a paralyzed patient to control a computer cursor with his thoughts. 64 

In 1999, the first BCI International Conference was held, which systematically standardized the classification of BCI technology. In 2000, Jonathan Wolpaw proposed a complete definition of BCI, laying the theoretical foundation for subsequent research. After 2000, with advances in computing power and signal processing, BCI development accelerated significantly. In 2004, the BrainGate trial (Leigh R. Hochberg and colleagues, USA) implanted a microelectrode array into a paralyzed patient (Matt Nagle), who was able to control a computer cursor and basic device functions with his thoughts—a milestone marking the transition of BCI from scientific validation to experimental application. 23 In 2011, the same BrainGate team demonstrated a paralyzed patient (Cathy Hutchinson) controlling a robotic arm to drink coffee, representing the first peer-reviewed demonstration of brain-controlled grasping. 65 At the 2014 World Cup opening ceremony, a paraplegic patient used a BCI-controlled exoskeleton to kick the first ball. In 2017, Facebook announced the “Brain Typing” project, and in 2019, Neuralink demonstrated a brain chip, which means that more complex and less invasive human brain interactions can be achieved. 66 Since 2020, BCI translation has accelerated from proof-of-concept to clinical implementation: home-use spelling systems for patients with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) have demonstrated long-term stability in real-world home settings, enabling sustainable communication for individuals with locked-in syndrome; concurrently, pivotal clinical trials of adaptive deep brain stimulation (aDBS) have been initiated, representing a critical milestone in translating closed-loop neuromodulation into routine clinical care for Parkinson’s disease. ## Application of BCI in neurodegenerative diseases

The clinical manifestations of neurodegenerative diseases mainly include motor disorders, cognitive impairments, mental and behavioral abnormalities, and other systemic abnormalities. Current applications of BCI systems revolve around four core functions: diagnosis, rehabilitation therapy, functional assistance, and monitoring and assessment. The core of BCI diagnosis is early identification and accurate classification, mainly relying on biomarker detection (such as abnormal EEG waves) and the quantification of motor symptoms. Rehabilitation therapy mainly involves neuromodulation and symptom intervention, targeting the control of the progression of mental and motor symptoms, such as cognitive decline, depression, kinetic tremor, and epilepsy, primarily through neurofeedback training and closed-loop neurostimulation. BCI-based functional support is centered on functional compensation, with a view to providing motor assistance, communication support, and independent living. Machine learning algorithms are applied to BCI monitoring and assessment, permitting not only remote home monitoring to avoid emergencies but also personalized interventions with dynamic adjustment of the treatment response. It is crucial to note that while neurodegenerative diseases generate a wealth of electrophysiological and kinematic data, not all data acquisition constitutes a BCI. As defined in the Introduction, this section focuses primarily on systems involving real-time neural decoding and, where applicable, closed-loop interaction. Technologies such as passive diagnostic biomarkers or non-adaptive stimulators are discussed only in the context of how they inform or interface with true BCI architectures. Regarding diagnostic modalities, it is important to distinguish between standalone analysis and BCI. EEG-based diagnostic systems, which lack real-time device control or sensory feedback loops, are more such as “BCI-enabling technologies” rather than complete BCI systems. However, the boundary is evolving: when diagnostic algorithms (e.g., AD risk patterns) are integrated into a pipeline that triggers immediate, automated therapeutic responses (e.g., adaptive neurostimulation or FES), the system transitions into a closed-loop BCI framework. Therefore, the qualifying criterion remains the presence of a functional, real-time loop connecting neural states to environmental or physiological modulation, rather than mere data analytics. This section emphasizes the transformative potential of BCI in neurology, while maintaining a rigorous distinction between monitoring tools and interactive neurotechnology. Consistent with the operational definition adopted in the Introduction, such a pipeline qualifies as a BCI only when the decoded output is coupled back to the user or to therapy within a functional real-time loop; when a human analyst, rather than the system itself, closes the loop, the pipeline remains a biomarker or decision-support tool. For example, risk patterns identified in early AD would qualify only where they automatically trigger an intervention (e.g., adaptive neurostimulation or FES) without a human re-entering the decision. Accordingly, Table 2 labels each technology discussed later in discussion either as a true BCI or as a BCI-enabling auxiliary technology. The remainder of this section reviews the clinical evidence for each disease while keeping this distinction in view. Table 2 summarizes the applications and functions of BCI in major neurodegenerative diseases.

## Alzheimer’s disease

The application of BCI in AD is mainly reflected in early screening and diagnosis, assistance, or rehabilitation training for the three major impaired functions (cognition, motor, and language), as well as disease mechanism research and new drug evaluation. Its application can run through the closed-loop management of the entire AD disease course. Combined with the AD disease process, it can form a complete intervention chain and a new model for managing patients with Alzheimer. Figure 4 demonstrates the application of BCI in AD.
 Figure 4 
Multi-mechanism applications of BCI in AD

(The figure illustrates BCI’s role across three domains: 1) Early Diagnosis, utilizing various neural markers (VEP wave changes, fMRI-based DMN activity, EEG power spectral shifts); 2) Symptom Intervention, employing techniques such as neurofeedback training to enhance prefrontal β-wave activity and rTMS to improve cognition; 3) Functional Assistance, divided into motor function aid (via restorative or compensatory pathways) and language/communication assistance through EEG-BCI spelling systems and multimodal fusion BCI for complex command recognition and cognitive training. The two technology categories are distinguished by line style throughout the figure: solid-outlined boxes denote true BCI applications, in which neural signals are decoded online and the decoded output is returned to the user as real-time device commands or therapeutic feedback, whereas dashed-outlined boxes denote BCI-enabling or non-BCI technologies, i.e., offline diagnostic biomarkers and open-loop stimulation that inform or support a BCI without themselves closing a real-time decoding-and-feedback loop. Accordingly, offline EEG and fMRI analyses are shown as BCI-enabling diagnostic tools and conventional open-loop rTMS as a non-BCI neuromodulation technique, whereas neurofeedback training, the EEG-based spelling system and cortical-potential control are classified as true closed-loop BCIs.

Timely diagnosis of AD is crucial for initiating effective intervention and alleviating disease progression. Traditional diagnostic methods rely heavily on clinical symptomatology and neuroimaging, which often suffer from drawbacks such as delays, high biomarker variability, limited imaging sensitivity, and susceptibility to various factors, resulting in diagnoses that are often only made in the late stages of the disease. 111 Offline EEG analysis has become one of the most promising directions for early screening due to its non-invasive nature, high temporal resolution, and relatively low cost. Under the operational definition adopted here, EEG recorded and classified offline is a BCI-enabling auxiliary technology rather than a true BCI, because no decoded output is returned to the user as device commands or therapeutic feedback. These technologies achieve diagnosis by capturing specific neuroelectrical activity variations caused by the pathophysiological process of AD. For example, based on the ERP paradigm, P300 is used as a sensitive early electrophysiological indicator for detection. Another example is based on the resting-state EEG paradigm, which quantifies the degree of abnormality of the DMN by analyzing the synchronicity of resting-state EEG signals (such as changes in alpha and theta wave power). In patients with mild cognitive impairment, the characteristics of increased theta wave power and decreased alpha wave power in EEG signals can be used as diagnostic indicators, and their sensitivity has been reported to be higher than that of traditional neuropsychological scales. 69 , 70 However, this conclusion requires validation in larger independent cohorts, and current evidence does not support its use as a standalone clinical diagnostic tool. In addition, most of the supporting studies feature selection and classifier evaluation within the same, relatively small cohorts of patients with mild cognitive impairment, frequently without nested cross-validation or an independent hold-out test set. This circular-analysis design can bias diagnostic accuracy estimates upward; the reported figures should therefore be regarded as an upper bound until they are replicated under strictly nested validation with pre-specified features and fully independent cohorts. Alternatively, based on the motor imagery (MI) paradigm, ERD/ERS is recorded by measuring the μ rhythm (8–12 Hz) and β rhythm (13–30 Hz) of the sensorimotor cortex. In patients, the appearance of this indicator is delayed, weakened, and more dispersed. Another paradigm based on VEPs detects the latency of VEP components such as P55, N65, P100, and N130, which has also been used to help differentiate AD from other types of dementia (such as Dementia with Lewy bodies, DLB). 72 , 73 Offline fMRI analysis provides more detailed brain functional images and detects reduced connectivity of brain networks in patients with AD (especially DMN abnormalities); as with offline EEG, it is a neuroimaging biomarker rather than a BCI unless the decoded state is returned to the subject as real-time neurofeedback. Since fNIRS is more suitable for assessing cortical blood flow and metabolic changes related to executive function and working memory, while EEG is good at detecting millisecond-level neural oscillation abnormalities in processes such as attention and memory encoding, fNIRS and EEG complement each other in the early diagnosis of AD. Therefore, multimodal fusion is an important direction for improving diagnostic accuracy in the future. Beyond these non-invasive approaches, invasive recording techniques—ECoG in particular, which is not a BCI in itself—have been used in animal models to record the firing patterns of hippocampal neurons (the hippocampus being a core region affected early in AD), providing direct evidence linking Aβ deposition to abnormal neural electrical activity. Such interfaces are best regarded as research “biomarker sensors” rather than as clinical BCI systems, and no invasive interface has yet been used for routine AD diagnosis. Spontaneous ultraweak photon emission (UPE) from brain tissue is another experimental non-BCI sensing signal explored for this purpose. Because there is a significant correlation between UPE intensity and AD, memory decline, and oxidative stress, it has been proposed as a candidate marker for early diagnosis. 75 These findings remain preliminary and have not yet been replicated in independent cohorts. However, to solve the problems of diagnostic sensitivity and diagnostic delay mentioned above, it is still necessary to find biomarkers with higher specificity in the early stages of the disease. Due to the lack of unified standardization and large-scale clinical validation, the specificity of existing technologies still needs to be further improved. In addition to whether the biomarkers themselves are accurate enough, it is also necessary to solve the problem of the insufficient ability of current technologies and equipment to capture and analyze information, while ensuring patient safety, so as to better achieve early diagnosis. Future research needs to integrate technological innovation, such as multimodal fusion, to build artificial intelligence learning and detection models, while improving clinical standards, building standardized databases, designing lightweight and edge computing devices to achieve commercialization, and building more robust and scalable early diagnosis models for AD, thus propelling AD management into a new stage of preventive medicine.

In the middle and late stages of AD, patients experience varying degrees of impairment in three major cognitive functions: cognition, motor function, and language, accompanied by certain levels of psychiatric symptoms. Neurofeedback-based BCI training has been reported to stabilize or improve cognitive performance in patients with AD, with gains reported for memory, cognitive flexibility, attention, reaction time, and executive function; these reports come from small, predominantly single-center studies. In addition, an enhanced β-wave activity in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) and improved positive modulation of hemodynamic response in the prefrontal cortex show a tendency of increasing cognitive processing efficacy, although whether such changes translate into slower disease progression or prevention of dementia has not been established. 76 , 77 NFB-BCI forms a closed-loop adaptive cognitive training system that online monitors the patients’ EEG indices and modulates the difficulty of training tasks according to their changes. Another pilot study evaluated the effectiveness of NFB-BCI to improve visual attention and language abilities in patients with mild AD. Patients with mild AD can use the BCI-based spelling system, which means that it is possible to train local neurofeedback-based BCI in patients with Alzheimer disease and that trained NFB-BCI might help improve attention and language abilities. 78 Overall, this technology has the potential for personalized intervention, such as by dynamically adjusting the feedback threshold to adapt to individual differences. However, current development faces bottlenecks such as the susceptibility of signal quality to interference, the need for further verification of the long-term stability of efficacy, and the cost and clinical accessibility of equipment. Future solutions focus on integrating multimodal brain imaging technologies (such as fNIRS-EEG) to improve decoding accuracy, developing more portable home training systems, and using artificial intelligence algorithms to optimize adaptive feedback control strategies. In addition to neurofeedback-based BCI, repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) has been investigated as a non-invasive neuromodulatory intervention for Alzheimer’s disease. Patients in the experimental group showed less cognitive and functional decline in multiple tests (such as ADAS-Cog, MMSE, ADCS-ADL) after 24 weeks of treatment, and their brain excitability remained stable with enhanced gamma waves, demonstrating the improvement of neural activity by the therapy. 79 Conventional rTMS is a neuromodulation technology rather than a BCI: it delivers pre-set stimulation and does not decode the patient’s neural state online. The potential relationship between rTMS and BCI lies primarily in their possible future integration. In a genuine rTMS-BCI architecture, ongoing neural signals could be acquired and decoded to estimate an individual’s neural state, and the resulting information could then be used to adapt stimulation parameters in real time. Such a system would satisfy the closed-loop criterion adopted in this review. At present, conventional rTMS and BCI-based neurofeedback should be regarded as complementary but conceptually distinct approaches. rTMS provides externally administered neuromodulation, whereas neurofeedback BCI enables real-time interaction between decoded brain activity and feedback delivered to the user. Combining the two approaches remains a promising direction for developing personalized closed-loop neuromodulation. If such a closed-loop rTMS-BCI architecture were eventually realized, its intended purpose would be to improve memory, executive function, attention, and language abilities in patients with AD; effects on brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) levels, brain network connectivity (such as the DMN), and neurotransmitter balance remain hypotheses that have not been tested in such a system. In contrast to single interventions, the rTMS preconditioning of brain excitability may help establish a more conducive neural substrate for subsequent BCI-training, thus generating a synergistic effect and enhancing rehabilitation efficiency. 112 , 113 , 114 , 115 Yet, this technique has limitations, including high interindividual variability in response, the absence of standardization regarding optimal stimulation parameters (site and frequency), and the necessity to further substantiate long-term effectiveness. It can be anticipated that in the future, with the extension of AI for individualized optimization and multi-neuron combined stimulation methods, the development of more portable home-use devices, as well as further evidence emerging from large randomized controlled trials, may validate its clinical benefits. Taken together, rTMS (a conventional neuromodulation technology) and NFB-based BCI (a true closed-loop BCI) are two complementary neuromodulation approaches in AD rehabilitation, with substantially different scenarios of use and mechanisms of effect; rTMS qualifies as a BCI only in the not-yet-realized closed-loop configuration described above. The main advantage of rTMS is an active and focal neuromodulation of the cortical area (e.g., DLPFC) by means of the magnetic field from the outside. High-frequency rTMS can increase the excitability of the cortex, and low-frequency rTMS can suppress excessive activity and act directly on neuroplastic effects, such as long-term potentiation (LTP), which constitutes a physiological basis for functional recovery. This technology is most suitable for patients with ADwho lack initiative or have very severe cognitive impairment, and has a significant “opening” effect. However, its limitations lie in the high cost of equipment, specialized operation, and significant individual variability in response, requiring personalized exploration of optimal stimulation parameters. In contrast, NFB-BCI focuses more on guiding patients to optimize brain activity patterns through internal self-regulation. It employs the operant conditioning approach to train patients to promote positive rhythms (e.g., sensorimotor rhythms, SMR) or suppress negative ones (e.g., excessive theta oscillations), enabling them to use real-time feedback (i.e., a gamified interface) to enhance their attention and executive function ability as well as alleviate psychological and behavioral disorders. This technology is non-intrusive and safe, with the potential to improve patient involvement and self-management (potentially favoring more home-based applications). But the effect of this therapy depends largely on the patient’s comprehension, compliance, and sustained effort, and it is difficult for patients in the middle and later stages of AD due to a variety of signal interferences. Despite promising pilot results, both neuromodulation approaches suffer from critical gaps in clinical evidence regarding sample size robustness and long-term efficacy sustainability. 116 For NFB-BCI, existing RCTs are predominantly small-scale. This limited sample size lacks statistical power to detect subgroup differences (e.g., between APOE ε4 carriers and non-carriers) and fails to account for placebo effects from repeated cognitive testing. For rTMS, no large-scale, multicenter trial has verified whether the gamma oscillation enhancement induced by rTMS translates to delayed disease progression (e.g., conversion from mild cognitive impairment to AD), and the durability of neuroplasticity changes after treatment cessation remains unproven. 117 

In functional support, BCI compensates as it offers relief to those with language and motor deficits. In terms of motor function assistance, FES combined with BCI based on MI can infer the patient’s motor intention and activate muscles or limbs by the FES device (or the like), to help restore or maintain the function of the motor system after running a loop of “intention-motor-sensory feedback”, and promote neuroplasticity. Another solution is aimed at compensating through environmental engagement control. For example, using relatively simple BCI paradigms (such as slow cortical potentials), the patient’s cognitive intentions are directly converted into control signals for operating external devices (light switches, call bells). The core of this approach is to bypass severely impaired motor output pathways and establish a new brain-computer communication channel. Its direct goal is to help patients achieve basic environmental control, thereby improving independence and quality of life. It is more like a functional substitute or aid. In brief, the former is restorative and attempts to restore normal intrinsic neuromotor function, while the latter is compensatory in that it tries to aid the patient's environmental interaction from the outside. The latter stage and the different focus of needs of the two approaches can be complementary and hold promise. As for language function support, the most common system used is an EEG-based BCI system (i.e., P300/SSVEP based spelling system) to help clients with their basic communication needs. In this model, we are treating the measured EEG signal from a given subject trying to express themselves (i.e., effortful activation of Broca’s area and motor cortex) as an input feature for writing or speaking output. 81 , 82 This system directly targets the typical loss of language expression ability and fine attentional control in patients with AD. Its effects are manifested on two levels: in terms of functional compensation, it achieves “mental typing” by detecting the patient’s attentional response to target characters (evoking P300 potentials) or flashes at specific frequencies (evoking SSVEP), thereby bypassing the damaged speech output pathway and helping patients conduct basic communication. In terms of neural mechanisms, the process of using this system can itself be seen as a form of cognitive training, which may help maintain or improve EEG activity patterns related to attention and working memory. However, patients with AD have attention deficits, and these systems are highly dependent on attention to operate; therefore, they are mainly suitable for patients with early- to mid-stage AD, aiming to help them overcome language expression difficulties, express their most basic needs, thereby improving their quality of life and reducing the burden of care. For late-stage patients with severe attention deficits, the effect of this technology is very limited. In addition, real-time fMRI-BCI can monitor the brain’s response to cognitive tasks and return that state to the subject as neurofeedback, which makes it a true BCI under the operational definition; it remains, however, an experimental approach with limited clinical validation in assisting basic communication and cognitive rehabilitation. For example, by using non-autonomous signals (such as emotional responses) to establish a yes/no communication paradigm through classical conditioning principles, even if patients cannot actively perform cognitive tasks, they can still convey basic information through emotional responses, providing basic communication possibilities for patients with severe attention deficit, 83 thereby making up for the operational limitations of the aforementioned systems that require a certain level of attention. In comparison, the EEG-BCI spelling system is more like a practical communication aid, which aims to bypass the damaged speech output pathway and directly solve the urgent need for “how to communicate”. Its value lies in the immediate functional compensation. Real-time fMRI-BCI is closer to a neurological function research and development tool. It focuses on deeply understanding the neural basis of language decline and exploring the potential to improve or delay language function decline itself by regulating the activity of specific brain regions. Its goal is intervention and reshaping.

At present, there is no AD-specific BCI device yet approved for routine clinical use. EEG and other non-invasive biomarker-based systems have demonstrated diagnostic and classification potential, but most remain in the research or early clinical validation stage and should not be considered clinically established BCI systems when they are used solely for offline diagnosis. Neurofeedback-based interventions have progressed to human clinical studies, including feasibility and preliminary efficacy investigations, but evidence remains insufficient for widespread clinical implementation. In contrast, conventional neuromodulation approaches such as rTMS have accumulated clinical evidence in AD but should be considered neuromodulation technologies rather than BCI systems unless stimulation is adaptively controlled by real-time decoded neural signals. Overall, AD-related BCI applications remain predominantly at the preclinical, early clinical feasibility, or preliminary-efficacy stages, with limited evidence supporting advanced clinical implementation. Maturity therefore has to be attributed to individual technologies rather than to AD as a whole: EEG- and UPE-based diagnostic classifiers remain at the internal-to-external validation stage; neurofeedback-based interventions have reached early clinical feasibility with preliminary-efficacy signals from small studies; P300/SSVEP spelling systems act as feasible assistive aids in early-to-middle-stage patients; real-time fMRI communication remains experimental; and rTMS, although supported by the most extensive clinical evidence base in AD, remains a conventional neuromodulation technology rather than a BCI unless its parameters are adaptively driven by decoded neural activity.

## Parkinson’s disease

The application of BCI in PD focuses on the monitoring and diagnosis of motor symptoms, adaptive neuromodulation, and rehabilitation assistance. Among these, symptom monitoring combined with diagnosis and adaptive neuromodulation are the most advanced and most promising directions for the application of BCI in PD. Early diagnosis remains a major challenge in the clinical management of PD. Current diagnosis relies on symptom assessment and MRI scans, but the large individual differences in symptoms lead to a high misdiagnosis rate. MRI analysis requires professional doctors and is expensive, and also has core limitations such as strong diagnostic lag, strong subjectivity, and inability to quantify. 118 Neural biomarkers acquired with the technologies on which BCI systems rely—scalp EEG, wearable sensors and implanted electrodes—may become abnormal before conventional clinical signs appear, offering a route to earlier identification of PD. In line with the operational definition adopted in the Introduction, these recordings constitute a BCI only when they feed a real-time decoding-and-feedback loop; most of the applications discussed later in discussion are therefore more accurately characterized as BCI-enabling diagnostic biomarkers. Spectral analysis based on high-density resting-state electroencephalography (HD-rsEEG) is one of the most important non-invasive methods. Its principle is to quantify the power characteristics of neural oscillations in different frequency bands after acquisition and signal processing. Patients with PD usually show characteristic spectral abnormalities. For example, in the early stages of the disease, there may be an increase in the theta band power and a decrease in the alpha band power. When dementia occurs, a significant increase in the delta and gamma band power can be used as a distinguishing marker. 85 In the AR Burg method (a quantitative EEG analysis method that combines an AR model with the Burg algorithm), the relative power of the delta and theta bands in patients increased, while the relative power of the alpha and beta bands decreased. Analysis based on weighted permutation entropy (WPE) showed that the entropy values of the patients’ EEG signals were significantly higher across the entire frequency domain, with the WPE of the γ band being higher than that of the control group, while the WPEs of the delta, theta, alpha, and beta bands were all lower than those of the control group. These dynamic changes in EEG may reflect early cortical dysfunction and can serve as biomarkers for early PD. Such abnormalities often appear before typical motor symptoms, and analysis combined with machine learning algorithms holds promise for early prediction of disease risk. 86 , 87 Because high-density EEG is sensitive to subtle spectral and functional connectivity abnormalities, it is under investigation as an early pre-symptomatic tool; reported performance derives largely from research cohorts and has not been established in prospective clinical screening. This method is safe and convenient, suitable for repeated examinations and long-term monitoring. It is often combined with machine learning algorithms to achieve high-precision automatic identification, and in clinical practice, it is mainly used to assist doctors in differentiating PD from essential tremor and similar diseases. Furthermore, by accurately identifying abnormal brain activity patterns associated with specific symptoms, high-density EEG holds promise for guiding the development of personalized treatment strategies. For example, based on real-time analysis of abnormal beta-band activity, aDBS systems can be developed to achieve more precise and on-demand neuromodulation. Brain imaging is also widely used to predict or assess PD, such as fMRI and metabolic imaging (FDG-PET). Based on the regional homogeneity (ReHo) and fractional amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations (fALFF) indices of resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI), a machine learning model was constructed. Through longitudinal analysis of 82 patients with PD at multiple time points (baseline, 1 year, 2 years and 4 years), and using the Movement Disorder Society-Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS) as the severity standard, the model showed significant predictive efficacy in rigorous internal and external validation: it could explain 30.4% of the variance of current symptoms and 55.8% of the variance of symptoms after 1 year, and the positive predictive value of classification for high and low severity populations reached 79%. The study found that elevated ReHo and fALFF in brain regions such as the DMN and motor network were significantly associated with milder current and future symptoms. This result indicates a promising neuroimaging prognostic biomarker, providing an important tool for clinical trial enrollment screening and individualized prognostic assessment of neuroprotective therapy. 90 Currently, fMRI has been widely used to study abnormal patterns of brain connectivity during rest and activation in patients with idiopathic PD during various tasks. Although the translation of fMRI into clinical practice is still in its early stages, fMRI measurements are expected to have multiple clinical applications in PD, including early detection, prediction of future changes in clinical status, and as a marker of brain physiological changes associated with neurotherapeutic drugs and neurorehabilitation strategies. 91 PET can show abnormal activation of pain-related brain regions in the drug-free state, such as enhanced response of the right thalamus and left anterior cingulate cortex to painful stimuli, and can also observe increased dopamine transmission in these regions in the early stages of the disease, which may be a compensatory mechanism. 18F-FDG PET imaging has shown outstanding value in the differential diagnosis of PD and atypical Parkinson’s syndrome (APS) by analyzing specific patterns of brain glucose metabolism. A preliminary meta-analysis showed that the sensitivity and specificity of visual PET reading for diagnosing APS based on voxel statistical analysis reached 91.4% and 90.6%, respectively. The diagnostic specificity for multiple system atrophy, progressive supranuclear palsy, and corticobasal degeneration remained at a high level (&gt;90%), which has a potential role in the early assessment and risk stratification of cognitive impairment. 93 In addition, PET can also detect changes in dopamine D2 receptors. The decreased binding capacity of D2 receptors in these regions further illustrates the changes in the dopamine system in the disease, and thus identifies patients with PD, especially those with mild cognitive impairment. 94 In PD, combining the high-spatial-resolution and functional-connectivity information provided by rs-fMRI with the regional metabolic patterns revealed by FDG-PET can yield a more accurate disease-specific biomarker network and support earlier diagnosis and quantitative severity assessment. These are neuroimaging biomarkers rather than BCIs, although they can be integrated into BCI pipelines as complementary inputs. Motor behavior analysis is another component of the early-diagnostic toolkit that interfaces with BCI. Wearable sensors combined with motion-capture technology quantitatively assess subtle changes in motor function in patients with prodromal PD, which may be difficult to detect with the naked eye in the early stages. For example, wearable inertial measurement units (IMUs) based on inertial sensing microelectromechanical systems (MEMS) can capture three-dimensional linear acceleration and angular velocity of specific body parts for analysis of gait disturbances, balance abnormalities, and motor symptoms. Outpatient IMU-integrated systems can also be used for fall monitoring and navigation for the visually impaired in the elderly. 88 Emerging active magnetoelectric motion sensing technology, which couples magnetoelectric sensors (ME sensors) with multi-light-source arrays, captures sub-millimeter-level 3D limb motion trajectories with minimal inertial lag. While its current standalone accuracy remains inferior to clinical-grade optical motion capture systems, it serves a critical role in ground-truth labeling for BCI motor intention decoding: high-precision kinematic data recorded by ME sensors can be temporally synchronized with simultaneously acquired EEG signals during motor imagery (MI) tasks, providing objective, artifact-free labels to train BCI classifiers to map neural patterns to actual motor output. This technology may help doctors detect motion abnormalities earlier and provide an option other than mainstream motion tracking systems (such as optical tracking or IMUs). 89 Another non-invasive approach to motor-behavior analysis uses the MI paradigm. Offline analysis of sensorimotor-cortex activation during imagined movement reveals impairments in motor initiation and planning through “mental rehearsal” and serves as an ERD-based cortical biomarker for diagnostic assessment; the same paradigm constitutes a true MI-BCI once ERD patterns are decoded online and fed back to the patient. It is therefore used both to grade motor impairment and as a component of closed-loop neurofeedback rehabilitation. Compared with the above non-invasive monitoring methods, invasive LFP recording provides more precise signals. It directly records the neural electrical activity of subcortical nuclei such as the subthalamic nucleus (STN) through deeply implanted electrodes, especially β-band synchronicity and phase-amplitude coupling (PAC). Its signal quality is extremely high, accurately capturing the core pathophysiological characteristics of the disease and distinguishing different motor subtypes. For example, patients with tremor-dominant (TD) have stronger slow high-frequency oscillations (sHFO, 200–260 Hz) in the upper STN region, while patients with postural instability and gait difficulty (PIGD) show abnormal fast high-frequency oscillations (fHFO, 260–450 Hz) in the lower STN region. 95 It has the potential to provide target localization for DBS surgery and provide real-time feedback signals for achieving aDBS. 96 Although invasive LFP recording is of great value, it is essentially a supplement to clinical diagnosis and a research tool, rather than an independent routine diagnostic method. Its application is strictly dependent on the needs of DBS surgical treatment and is invasive. The primary way forward is to encourage personalized and adjustable treatments. For instance, building a smarter closed-loop DBS system with the LFP biomarkers and integrating the LFP features with multimodal imaging and clinical information to create a more complete personalized treatment plan. In the future, more attention should be paid to multimodal information fusion and longitudinal monitoring of PD, as well as machine learning-based diagnosis for elucidating underlying pathology in PD. In particular, this includes the combination of different neurophysiological characteristics (e.g., power and functional connectivity based on EEG signals over multiple frequency bands) and using DL models for multimodal signal decoding. Conventional techniques to diagnose PD involve handcrafting features (speech, motion trajectories, samples of writing), then the use of machine learning models (SVMs or Random Forests) for classification. This process is cumbersome and has limited generalization ability. In addition, traditional classifiers have limitations when handling high-dimensional nonlinear data, making it difficult to achieve ideal overall diagnostic performance. DL models such as MobileNetV3, combined with improved optimization algorithms and attention mechanisms (such as pyramid channel attention networks), can automatically extract high-order features, optimize model hyperparameters by combining improved dwarf mongoose optimization (DMO), and introduce pyramid channel feature attention networks to enhance the expression of key features and suppress interference information, thereby effectively improving the accuracy and stability of PD identification. On the PPMI and NTUA public datasets, such methods have reported accuracy of 99.34%, with sensitivity, specificity, and F1 scores reported as superior to conventional methods. These figures were obtained on benchmark datasets and in silico classification settings rather than in prospective clinical cohorts, so the reported translation potential remains to be demonstrated. 119 Virtual reality evoked paradigms based on gait and eye movement modalities can achieve high-precision classification (average AUC 0.97, accuracy 0.92) on a small number of tasks, effectively overcoming the limitations of traditional subjective scales. 120 These methods can integrate large amounts of complex data and have been reported to improve the objectivity and accuracy of classification, supporting auxiliary differential diagnosis and severity assessment; prospective clinical validation is still required.

In addition to traditional drug therapy, PD treatment also includes surgical treatment for patients in the middle and late stages, namely, DBS. Traditional DBS is a surgical intervention in which electrodes are surgically implanted into precise brain regions to modulate abnormal neural activity. However, due to its significant limitations, such as fixed stimulation parameters, it cannot adapt to fluctuations in symptoms, often leading to overstimulation or understimulation, and may cause side effects such as speech difficulties and hypermotility. This means that the effectiveness and safety of traditional open-loop DBS therapy may not be stable. 97 , 98 Closed-loop optimization of DBS represents a relatively mature approach for the treatment of PD. aDBS combines BCI with the core principle of a closed-loop system. It utilizes electrodes implanted in target nuclei such as the STN to sense and decode specific neurophysiological signals (primarily the oscillation power of the β-band (13–30 Hz) in LFPs) highly correlated with PD motor symptoms (such as bradykinesia and rigidity) in real time. A closed-loop system is achieved through real-time monitoring and feedback regulation: when the system detects abnormally enhanced β-oscillations, indicating an impending worsening of symptoms, the built-in artificial intelligence algorithm automatically increases the stimulation intensity to suppress abnormal activity; conversely, when the brain state returns to normal or the medication takes effect, the system reduces stimulation or enters a standby state, thus achieving on-demand, precise closed-loop treatment. Because it can dynamically adjust parameters according to the patient’s current neurological state, it has been reported to improve the accuracy of treatment. 97 , 99 , 121 For example, taking essential tremor (ET) as the research object, a closed-loop control framework integrating a neurodynamic model and physiological signal feedback was constructed. The ventral intermediate nucleus of the thalamus (Vim) was selected as the stimulation target. A dynamic relationship model between Vim-DBS and electromyography (EMG) was established through a polynomial approximation method. A proportional-integral-derivative (PID) controller was combined to realize the real-time dynamic adjustment of stimulation frequency. The stimulation parameters predicted by the model are highly consistent with the commonly used clinical parameters. In this model study, the controller tracked symptom changes and maintained the treatment effect, illustrating a potential route to personalized treatment that has not yet been evaluated in patients. 122 Another type of DBS combined with neurofeedback is used for PD rehabilitation training. By selecting the most suitable neurobiomarkers for closed-loop neurofeedback to achieve personalization, it may enhance the patient’s motor performance. The system collects the patient’s EEG signals in the resting or task state through high-density electrodes, uses signal processing algorithms to filter out artifacts, and extracts characteristic neuro-oscillatory biomarkers related to the pathological state of PD (the most typical being the abnormal enhancement of β-band oscillations in the sensorimotor cortex). Subsequently, machine learning algorithms decode these features in real time and present the results to patients as intuitive audiovisual or SF. This allows patients to consciously and actively regulate their abnormal brain electrical activity through learning, thereby inducing changes in brain neuroplasticity based on operant conditioning and promoting functional reorganization of the corticobasal ganglia-thalamus circuit. For example, patients can be trained to consciously suppress overactive beta wave activity while enhancing μ rhythms (8–12 Hz) associated with motor preparation. This self-regulation training can enhance patients’ self-control over symptoms and reduce dependence on medication. However, this method has significant shortcomings, including insufficient signal quality and accuracy, significant individual differences in treatment effects, a lack of universally applicable biomarkers for predicting efficacy, reliance on the clinical environment, and bulky and costly equipment. Furthermore, the specific physiological mechanisms by which neurofeedback induces neuroplasticity are not fully elucidated, and the lack of large-sample, long-term follow-up clinical trial evidence, standardized training protocols, and efficacy evaluation criteria will all hinder its widespread adoption and actual clinical use.

Furthermore, BCI systems based on MI or SSVEP can also assist in movement by combining FES or exoskeletons/robotic arms. 123 The MI-BCI system decodes motor intentions into control commands by recognizing ERD/ERS EEG patterns evoked in the sensorimotor cortex when a patient performs specific limb MI. The SSVEP-BCI system, on the other hand, relies on EEG responses synchronized with the stimulation frequencies generated in the occipital cortex when the patient gazes at visual stimuli of different frequencies. The decoded commands drive the exoskeleton to assist the patient in performing rehabilitation movements such as flexion and extension or grasping of joints such as the shoulder, elbow, wrist, and hand, or trigger FES to precisely activate target muscles. In addition to sharing the load with the user, this technology can also beneficially stimulate neuroplasticity and promote patients’ self-activated rehab training. Application of action observation (AO) during BCI results in the generation of an output-feedback closed loop with simultaneous cortical and peripheral stimulation during MI. This associative sensorimotor stimulation facilitates the process of inducing long-term cortical plasticity changes specific to the task, more rapidly promoting remodeling in motor pathways. In summary, these BCI approaches provide a promising adjunctive rehabilitation tool for controlling motor symptoms in PD by requiring active patient engagement.

PD currently shows the most advanced BCI translation among the neurodegenerative diseases considered here, but this again reflects individual technologies rather than the disease as a whole. Closed-loop neuromodulation is the most mature: adaptive DBS (aDBS) implemented on the Medtronic Percept PC platform with BrainSense technology received European CE marking in January 2025 and US Food and Drug Administration approval in February 2025, supported by the ADAPT-PD trial, 100 thereby becoming the first regulatory-cleared, commercially available closed-loop deep brain stimulation system for PD; its sensing biomarkers, control policies and remaining implementation challenges have recently been reviewed elsewhere. 101 Regulatory clearance and commercial availability should, however, not be equated with routine deployment in everyday care, which remains limited by center-level expertise, programming burden and reimbursement. By contrast, PD-specific screening and diagnostic tools (HD-rsEEG, IMU-based kinematics, rs-fMRI, and FDG-PET classifiers) remain at the internal-to-external validation stage and serve only as adjuncts to differential diagnosis, while MI- and SSVEP-based motor rehabilitation BCIs remain at the preclinical or early-feasibility stage without standardized protocols.

## Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS)

The treatment of ALS is based on a multidisciplinary comprehensive management strategy, the core of which is the combination of disease-modifying therapy and systematic symptomatic support. The diagnosis is based on the clinical manifestations of progressively worsening upper and lower motor neuron involvement, and is mainly confirmed by neurophysiological examination. At the same time, other diseases need to be ruled out through imaging and genetic testing. Symptomatic support is a key link and needs to be adjusted individually according to the patient’s disease stage. 124 Currently, the clinical staging of ALS is mainly based on two systems: the London staging system, which divides the disease into five stages based on key milestones in the disease course (such as symptom onset, diagnosis, new area involvement, and the need for percutaneous gastrostomy or non-invasive ventilation); and the Milano Torino functional staging (ALS-MITOS) system, which stages the disease by assessing the number of functional losses in four functional domains: activity, swallowing, communication, and respiration. Stage 0 is no functional loss; Stage 1 is one functional domain loss; Stage 2 is two functional domain losses; Stage 3 is three functional domain losses; Stage 4 is four functional domain losses; and Stage 5 is death or mechanical ventilation. 125 , 126 , 127 The application of BCI in ALS closely focuses on the core needs of patients with ALS, namely communication, motor function, improved quality of life, including emotional expression, and clinical assessment. In the early stages of the disease, patients typically exhibit loss of independent ability in one or two functional domains. For example, loss of the motor domain manifests as severe limitation of walking and self-care abilities, while loss of the communication domain manifests as a lack of effective communication (loss of speech, inability to communicate, or inability to hold a pen and write). When two functional domains are lost independently, the patient’s functional impairment worsens, requiring more external support. BCI systems are primarily used for simple functional assistance, and combining them with closed-loop monitoring can achieve better individualized adaptation. For example, non-invasive BCI systems, such as those based on the SSVEP paradigm or the MI paradigm, should be considered first. These systems allow patients to operate computers for spelling and control smart devices by observing specific visual stimuli (SSVEP) or imagining actions to generate EEG signals. In addition, non-invasive technologies such as fNIRS have also been explored to improve communication abilities. Based on hemodynamic responses, fNIRS has shown higher accuracy than conventional EEG-based P300 spellers in small-sample studies; these findings still await confirmation in larger cohorts. As the disease progresses, patients may lose most of their voluntary motor abilities. Patients with late-stage ALS often lose their speech due to bulbar symptoms, falling into a locked-in state (LIS). Traditional assistive communication technologies rely on residual muscle movements (such as eye trackers), but about 10–17% of patients cannot use them effectively due to problems such as eye movement disorders. In this case, a higher precision signal acquisition scheme needs to be introduced. Semi-invasive or invasive BCIs control computers by directly recording brain signals, providing these patients with a muscle-independent communication channel, and are a key hope for restoring their communication ability. For example, intracortical microelectrodes record the activity of individual neurons with high decoding accuracy, enabling typing and speech decoding, but signal stability and long-term durability are still challenges. 129 Fully implantable wireless BCI systems eliminate percutaneous connectors, which have been associated with increased risks of infection and mechanical complications in chronically implanted BCI systems—making them uniquely suitable for locked-in patients with ALS who cannot tolerate surgical revisions or independent wound care. This connector-free design reduces infection and enables feasible long-term home use for this population. An intracortical speech prosthesis for patients with severe dysarthria due to ALS is based on ECoG-BCI. This prosthesis records neural activity by implanting a microelectrode array in the left ventral precentral gyrus and uses a novel decoding algorithm to convert cortical signals during attempts to pronounce words into text or synthesized speech. The results showed that the system achieved an accuracy rate of 99.6% with a vocabulary of 50 words on the first postoperative day. After a short period of training, the accuracy rate remained at 90.2% when the vocabulary was expanded to 125,000 words. It could support self-paced dialogue at a speed of approximately 32 words per minute, indicating that it has the characteristics of rapid calibration and high-precision output, providing a feasible solution for restoring conversational communication in severely paralyzed patients and maintaining stability during long-term implantation. 105 , 130 Since traditional vision-dependent BCIs (such as eye-tracking-based systems) fail at this stage, another approach is to use non-visual paradigm BCIs (such as auditory BCIs). These systems achieve basic communication through binary selection (yes/no). In addition, multimodal fusion BCI systems integrate EEG/ECoG with multimodal data such as fNIRS and diffusion tensor imaging (DTI), which can achieve more accurate and efficient assistive functions. For example, a single-trial visuo-mental (VM) paradigm fNIRS-BCI system can stably decode user intent without relying on visual input, providing a more reliable and flexible non-invasive communication method for patients with advanced ALS, and has important clinical application potential. The system screens the optimal channel and hemodynamic features through statistical parameter mapping (SPM) and correlation analysis, and achieves efficient classification by combining LDA. The average accuracy rate within 4 s reaches 81.3% ± 5.7%, which is better than the traditional P3Speller’s 74.0% ± 8.9%. Further longitudinal testing on a patient in late LIS showed that the accuracy rate of fNIRS-VM still reached 73.2% ± 2.0%, which is numerically higher than the P3Speller’s 61.8% ± 1.5%. 128 

BCI is not currently the primary tool for early diagnosis of ALS, and the offline neurophysiological indices discussed later in discussion are assessment tools rather than true BCIs, because they are analyzed offline and are not coupled to a device or to therapeutic feedback. They nevertheless define the measurement substrate on which any future online ALS BCI would be built. Early diagnosis of ALS mainly relies on clinical manifestations and auxiliary examinations such as EMG, which often results in diagnostic delays. Offline EEG analysis combined with machine-learning classifiers has been explored for this purpose, but such pipelines are assessment tools rather than BCIs: they are analyzed offline and do not drive a device or deliver feedback. Compared with healthy individuals, patients with ALS exhibit reduced ERD amplitude and time delay in MI tasks, especially in right-sided MI. This indicates that ERD features are significantly correlated with the patient’s disease course, bulbar function, and cognitive level, providing potential biomarkers for EEG-based ALS cortical function assessment. The main goal of monitoring in ALS is to provide objective and quantifiable indices of cognitive and conscious state in patients who are completely locked in and unable to express any behavior; these indices are BCI-enabling assessment tools, not themselves BCIs. Currently, commonly used assessment methods mainly include monitoring based on motor intention decoding, monitoring based on cognitive status, and monitoring based on visual system function. 106 , 107 , 110 Beyond the non-invasive indices used for monitoring and assessment, the speech neuroprostheses described above have shown that semi-invasive interfaces can support autonomous, real-time control of assistive devices in ALS; whether the same recordings can be used for reliable monitoring of neurological state and disease progression is still being investigated. 131 Taken together, these EEG/fNIRS-based indices offer the prospect of an objective means of assessing cognition and consciousness in completely locked-in patients who cannot express any behavior, addressing the question of how to determine whether a completely immobile patient remains conscious. Reducing misdiagnosis as a vegetative state and informing treatment and nursing decisions remain prospective benefits; validation in large locked-in cohorts is still lacking.

BCI translation in ALS likewise has to be assessed technology by technology. Non-invasive communication BCIs (P300-, SSVEP-, and MI-based spellers) are the most clinically mature and have demonstrated feasibility for sustained, long-term home use, although this conclusion rests largely on small case series and single-subject reports rather than on large controlled trials. Invasive high-performance interfaces—ECoG- and intracortical speech or text decoders—remain predominantly investigational, with early clinical feasibility studies in small numbers of participants addressing mainly safety, device functionality, signal stability, and proof of principle. Non-invasive (EEG/fNIRS) consciousness monitoring in the complete locked-in state and ERD-based cortical assessment remain at the preclinical to early clinical stage.

## Multidimensional association between BCI and neurodegenerative diseases

The core driving force behind the evolution of BCI comes from clinical needs: shifting from generalized symptom management toward individualized treatment based on patient-specific neural characteristics. Whether such personalization can ultimately support disease modification is not yet established, and remains an objective rather than a demonstrated outcome. Figure 7 illustrates a conceptual framework for how BCI-based neuroprotection might be organized; it depicts a proposed architecture, not a validated clinical pathway.
 Figure 7 
The conceptual framework of BCI-driven neuroprotection

The figure contrasts the traditional, late-stage palliative care paradigm with a novel, closed-loop intervention strategy. The new paradigm leverages high-density flexible electrodes for early detection of electrophysiological anomalies, enabling multimodal data fusion and intelligent strategy optimization. This framework is proposed to enable precise, personalized interventions (e.g., multimodal sensory feedback) that may reduce neuronal damage, strengthen synaptic connections, and mitigate abnormal protein deposition, thereby potentially shifting the treatment model from reactive to proactive. As the figure indicates, these neuroprotective effects remain hypothesized and require clinical validation.

The fundamental purpose of closed-loop monitoring is to achieve dynamic and personalized treatment. Traditional neuromodulation techniques, such as DBS and spinal cord stimulation (SCS), mainly adopt an open-loop model. Their essence is unidirectional, fixed-parameter stimulation, which cannot be dynamically adjusted according to the patient’s real-time neurophysiological state. Therefore, they have limitations such as poor adaptability, easy tolerance, and side effects. 132 In contrast, the core of closed-loop BCIs lies in constructing a complete feedback loop of “real-time monitoring-decoding-intervention”: relying on implantable electrodes, optical probes, and fNIRS, a three-dimensional signal acquisition and monitoring network covering neuroelectrophysiology, inflammatory response, and metabolic state is constructed. Efficient decoding algorithms are used to identify abnormal neural activity patterns in real time and trigger precise electrical or chemical stimulation intervention within milliseconds, thus forming a bidirectional adaptive interaction mechanism. This dynamic and individualized strategy is intended to support neuroprotection: for example, adjusting stimulation parameters in real time according to the fluctuations in motor symptoms of patients with PD; such adjustment has been reported to improve symptom control while extending device battery life. Comparable closed-loop neuroprotection in other indications, such as pre-empting epileptic seizures, remains at the proof-of-concept stage and lies outside the neurodegenerative diseases reviewed here.

Recent progress in closed-loop monitoring technology has primarily focused on achieving high-precision, multi-dimensional neural signal acquisition and decoding algorithms, gradually improving real-time neural activity monitoring and prediction capabilities. Implantable electrodes, optical probes, and fNIRS can simultaneously capture a three-dimensional monitoring network of neural electrophysiological activity, local metabolic state, and the microscopic cellular environment: implantable electrodes provide high temporal resolution recording of neural electrical signals at the millisecond level, fNIRS presents a panoramic view of brain hemodynamics and metabolism at centimeter-level spatial resolution, and optical probes (such as gene-encoded calcium indicators) analyze the molecular activity of specific neuronal types at the single-cell level. Regarding implantable electrodes, although rigid electrodes (such as the classic Utah and Michigan electrodes) have demonstrated excellent conductivity, high electrode density, and signal stability in short-term applications and have been used in specific clinical scenarios such as DBS, their long-term biocompatibility and signal fidelity still have significant limitations. Flexible electrodes, due to their high biocompatibility, minimal damage, and stable signal quality, have become the focus of technological competition. For example, flexible high-density microelectrode arrays (flexible HDMEAs) are high-channel, high-spatial-resolution microelectrode arrays with electrode spacing typically less than 200 μm. Their substrates are flexible (e.g., polyimide), and this allows both high-resolution neural signal recording and electrical stimulation at the same time. 133 They represent key components of a closed-loop system aimed at the real-time “reading” and “writing” of brain signals, with superior biocompatibility and long-term stability over rigid arrays. 44 , 134 However, this material is still faced with the following problems: its flexible nature results in inadequate mechanical stiffness, which makes it difficult to implant and may damage tissue 135 , 136 , 137 ; high-density electrodes with small footprint lead to high impedance and cross-talk of signals, hence impairing recording and stimulation performance 138 , 139 , 140 , 141 , 142 ; stable, reliable electrical interconnection between hundreds or thousands of channels is a limiting technical challenge 143 , 144 , 145 ; regarding long-term biocompatibility, inevitable immune responses in the body may form scar tissue which would isolate electrodes from neurons leading to poor signal over time. 146 Overall, the advantages of flexible electrodes are substantial and address the fundamental limitations of rigid electrodes, whereas the advantages of rigid electrodes (such as ease of implantation and high conductivity) often reflect technological maturity and intrinsic material properties. Therefore, the evolution from rigid to flexible is an inevitable trend in BCI electrode technology toward higher biocompatibility, less trauma, and long-term stability. Novel composite materials and structural designs combining rigidity and flexibility are becoming important directions for overcoming existing bottlenecks. For example, developing “rigid-flexible composite” electrodes that maintain rigidity during implantation for ease of manipulation and regain flexibility after entering the body to adapt to tissues, or exploring new materials with both excellent electrical and mechanical properties. In terms of optical probes, current research mainly focuses on improving imaging performance and enhancing functional intelligence. NIR-II probes with deeper tissue penetration can be developed to achieve higher imaging performance by exploiting the unique optical advantages of the NIR-II window to overcome the key limitations of traditional visible light and near-infrared I probes in vivo applications, such as shallow penetration depth, strong background interference, and insufficient resolution. NIR-II fluorescent probes, with their significantly reduced tissue scattering and absorption characteristics, combined with high signal-to-noise ratio and low autofluorescence, have successfully achieved high-sensitivity in vitro detection and high-resolution in vivo imaging of deep tissues for a variety of biomarkers, covering a range of targets from tumor markers and pathogens to small molecule metabolites. 147 Despite these technical advantages, the translational safety profile of NIR-II probes in humans remains poorly characterized, representing a critical barrier to clinical adoption. Biocompatibility gaps are further exacerbated by the lack of standardized human safety assessment protocols for intracranial or transdermal NIR-II probe use in neurodegenerative disease populations, who often have impaired blood-brain barrier integrity and altered systemic metabolism. NIR-II fluorescence imaging has progressed beyond the preclinical stage and has been investigated in human neurosurgical applications, including intraoperative imaging of brain gliomas. 148 These first-in-human clinical studies demonstrate the feasibility of using NIR-II probes for human brain imaging and provide an important translational milestone for this technology. However, its clinical application in neurodegenerative diseases remains at an early stage, and further studies are required to establish safety, imaging performance, target specificity, and clinical utility in these populations. In terms of fNIRS, it can provide a core feedback dimension for a BCI system to achieve true closed-loop monitoring and adaptive regulation. For example, in rehabilitation training of MI BCI control function electrical stimulation (FES), studies have found that MI-BCI-FES training combined with BCI closed-loop can induce more significant brain activation (stronger ERD and HbO 2 response) compared to open-loop training. The hemodynamic changes monitored in real time by fNIRS can serve as an objective indicator for quantifying the level of neural activation in training, enabling the system to dynamically adjust training parameters according to the user’s current brain state, thereby achieving personalized and adaptive neurorehabilitation. Moreover, some research has been able to detect light signals completely through the cranium in adult subjects by utilizing high power pulsed lasers and an ultra-high sensitivity detector. This approach overcomes the depth limitation of conventional fNIRS (roughly 4 cm from the brain surface), and it also provides theoretical support for the future development of new optical brain-imaging diagnostic instruments for deep brain areas such as memory, emotion, or sensation. In the decoding algorithm, the most important work is the design of an adaptive machine learning model. Adaptive machine learning algorithms on the other hand adapt continuously to changing data distribution by combining online and incremental learning modules. The fundamental idea is dynamic model adjustment with incremental learning and transfer learning, which allows a process where parameters and structure of the model are fine-tuned gradually according to newly arriving data streams, without fully retraining the model. This approach is superior to classical static models in terms of accuracy, applicability, and computational cost since it processes data instances or batch updates instantly and increases the robustness as well as reliability of the system for cases where the distribution of data changes rapidly (e.g., real-time prediction, anomaly detection). The decoding algorithm, in this manner, may also be capable of dynamically following and accommodating changes to the neural activity patterns due to the progression of a disease or daily state variability without overly relying on frequent recalibration. 149 Notably, direct cross-disease transfer of decoding models across AD, PD, and ALS lacks biological plausibility given their distinct pathological substrates. Therefore, such transfer learning should be strictly limited to intra-individual calibration within the same disease entity. Only disease-non-specific technical components (e.g., adaptive calibration logic, incremental updating frameworks) can be reused across indications, whereas disease-specific biomarkers require disease-matched retraining to avoid decoding bias. Overall, the current progress in neural signal decoding algorithms is mainly reflected in the shift from “precision” to “universality” and “stability”. The next generation of BCI algorithms not only pursues high precision in an ideal laboratory environment but also strives to overcome the signal variation problem in the real world.

The mechanism most often invoked for BCI-mediated neuroplasticity is the recruitment of latent networks through neurofeedback and closed-loop feedback training. This mechanism is plausible and supported indirectly by neuroplasticity literature, but it has not been demonstrated in neurodegenerative patients; increases in synaptic strength and functional connectivity remain inferred rather than measured outcomes of BCI training.

Neuroplasticity, the brain’s ability to reorganize itself by forming new neural connections, plays a crucial role in the recovery from neurological injury or disease. This dynamic process enables the brain to adapt to change, recover lost functions, and improve cognitive and motor skills. 150 The core objective of multimodal intervention strategies is to enhance this ability, thereby altering disease progression. Traditional treatments for neurodegenerative diseases often focus on delaying neuronal death, neglecting the brain’s inherent plasticity potential. Although in the late stages of the disease, surviving neurons can partially compensate for damage through synaptic remodeling and functional reorganization, their spontaneous compensation efficiency is low and lacks targeted guidance. 151 , 152 Multimodal intervention systems, through the intelligent matching of three intervention modes—electro, chemical, and optical—complete closed-loop feedback from pathological identification to treatment implementation. For example, modulating neural oscillations (such as 40Hz gamma oscillations) has significantly improved cognitive function for up to 8 weeks in a study of 37 patients with possible AD, compared with sham stimulation. The mechanism is to modulate gamma band oscillations and promote multi-level connectivity of brain networks: rsEEG power spectral density analysis showed that rTMS enhanced the gamma oscillation power in the left posterior temporoparietal lobe, and neuroimaging analysis further showed that 40-Hz rTMS not only prevented gray matter volume loss, but also enhanced both the local functional integration of the bilateral angular gyri and their global functional integration with the left middle frontal lobe, while strengthening the information flow from the left posterior temporoparietal lobe to the prefrontal lobe and the dynamic connectivity between the anterior and posterior brain regions. 153 Multimodal BCI is intended to shift treatment from passive symptom management toward active neural function remodeling, although remodeling of this kind has so far been demonstrated only in small, short-duration studies of specific cognitive or motor protocols rather than at the disease level. It is no longer just a replacement for damaged functions, but rather a means to stimulate the brain’s own repair capabilities through precise, dynamic, and personalized closed-loop interactions, which may change the course of the disease.

The attraction of BCI technology in neurodegenerative disease lies in the possibility that an integrated closed-loop system combining monitoring, analysis, and intervention might eventually transform treatment paradigms. At present, only individual components of such a system have been realized: continuous multimodal monitoring, closed-loop stimulation for a single indication (adaptive DBS in PD), and offline decoding of disease-related signals. The fully integrated “monitoring-analysis-intervention” system described in this paragraph is a conceptual rproposal. The proposed paradigm would move beyond passive symptom relief in the later stages of disease, focusing instead on early warning, dynamic intervention, and proactive neural function remodeling, with comprehensive disease modification treated as a long-term goal rather than a demonstrated outcome. At its core is the closed-loop intelligent system. At the dynamic monitoring level, it transcends traditional sporadic imaging examinations, constructing a continuously operating multidimensional monitoring network using fNIRS technologies such as implantable electrodes and optical probes. This network can simultaneously capture neurophysiological activity, local metabolic states, and even microscopic cellular environmental changes, providing a possible data foundation for early detection of pathological changes. At the intelligent analysis and decision-making level, artificial intelligence algorithms integrate multimodal monitoring data to decode the patient’s neural activity or identify abnormal patterns in real time; in the proposed architecture, this would require capabilities well beyond those currently validated. For example, the system can analyze the synchronicity of resting-state EEG signals to warn of cognitive decline, or determine the activity of neuroinflammation through the levels of specific inflammatory factors, providing a basis for subsequent precise intervention decisions. At the level of multimodal precision intervention, the system can automatically trigger the most appropriate intervention strategy based on the analysis results. This is no longer a single electrical stimulation, but a synergistic effect of multiple means, such as electrical stimulation targeting abnormal neural oscillations, using focused ultrasound to open the blood-brain barrier for targeted drug delivery, or neuromodulation through light modulation of specific wavelengths. Integrating closed-loop monitoring, multimodal intervention, and intelligent decision-making is therefore an active research direction, with the goal of moving from static, passive symptom management toward personalized intervention. Progress toward disease modification, however, has not been demonstrated: no BCI-based intervention has yet been shown to alter the natural course of AD, PD, or ALS in a controlled trial. Should such integration eventually be achieved, it might shift the therapeutic window; whether it could alter the natural course of disease through continuous digital therapy remains speculative.

## Limitations and challenges

BCI technology has progressed from proof-of-concept toward selected practical applications, most visibly in neurorehabilitation and assistive communication, although the number of indications supported by controlled clinical evidence remains small. However, its applications remain limited, and the market outside of medical research has not yet been fully developed. Ultimately, this can be attributed to three obstacles: technological bottlenecks, practical applications, and ethical and safety concerns, as shown in Figure 8 .  Figure 8 
A comprehensive overview of the multifaceted challenges facing BCI technology

The diagram categorizes the major hurdles into three interconnected domains: 1) Technical Challenges, including signal processing artifacts, algorithmic limitations, individual variability, and hardware constraints; 2) Clinical and Practical Application Obstacles, encompassing performance trade-offs (e.g., speed vs. accuracy), hardware integration issues, and user experience barriers such as biocompatibility and long training periods; 3) Ethical and Privacy Challenges, focusing on informed consent, data security, responsibility attribution, and fundamental questions concerning human identity.

At the technical level, the development of BCI is mainly limited by the reliability of signal acquisition, processing, and system performance. In terms of signal acquisition, existing stimulation paradigms have obvious limitations: ERP and SSVEP require specific visual stimuli, SSVEP has a high information transmission rate but can lead to visual fatigue, and MI has a slow response speed. 161 , 185 , 186 , 187 Hybrid BCI (such as SSVEP/ERP and SSVEP/MI) can improve robustness, but the performance is still not ideal, and multimodal signal fusion leads to high algorithm complexity. 188 , 189 , 190 In addition, EEG signals acquired by non-invasive BCI are susceptible to physiological artifacts (such as eye movement and EMG) and environmental interference (such as 50/60Hz power frequency noise), resulting in a low signal-to-noise ratio. 154 , 155 , 191 Various physiological factors, such as attention, memory, load, fatigue, age, gender, and location, can affect a person’s brain dynamics. 172 For example, sensitive or emotional subjects may have different performance or EEG waves when performing the same task compared to enthusiastic subjects. Resting physiological parameters (i.e., resting heart rate variability, respiratory rate, and electrocardiogram) also affect the results. 192 , 193 , 194 Limitations in electrode technology also affect signal quality: firstly, it is difficult to balance signal quality and ease of use in the selection of electrode materials. Wet electrodes require conductive gel, and the preparation process is cumbersome; dry electrodes are portable but have a low signal-to-noise ratio; semi-dry electrodes attempt to balance the two. 157 , 158 , 195 In addition, electrode placement errors may also cause signal distortion, so the 10–20 system standard must be strictly followed. 196 , 197 In comparison, although invasive BCI can provide higher signal quality, the surgical risks and long-term stability issues cannot be ignored. 198 Signal quality and complexity also closely affect the signal processing steps. For example, the non-stationarity and individual variability of EEG signals complicate signal processing, requiring reliance on adaptive algorithms. 156 Secondly, dimensionality reduction of high-dimensional EEG data is one of the core challenges of BCI systems. Although PCA and ICA are classic feature extraction methods, their linear assumptions limit their effectiveness in complex EEG signals. In the future, nonlinear models and DL need to be combined to improve the robustness of feature extraction. 199 , 200 Linear classifiers (such as LDA) are computationally efficient but struggle to capture complex patterns, while nonlinear classifiers (such as SVM and ANN) are highly accurate but require a large amount of training data. If machine learning is applied, insufficient cross-subject generalization ability and the data requirements of DL models limit the practicality of the algorithm, while the balance between latency and accuracy in real-time processing remains an unsolved problem. 163 , 164 , 165 

The practical application obstacles involve whether BCI can be extended from the laboratory to real clinical applications. The core is to develop high-performance acquisition technology in system integration solutions, such as establishing stable signal acquisition and creating a theoretically robust interaction paradigm, while ensuring user safety, enhancing patient capabilities, and providing greater daily convenience. 201 Among the aforementioned technical challenges, the system requires a large amount of training data for complex data processing, which leads to a problem: user experience. Approximately 15%–30% of people may not be able to generate sufficiently robust EEG signals under any circumstances. Some researchers have proposed techniques based on adaptive machine learning methods to address this issue. 163 Recent adaptive machine learning frameworks specifically integrate transfer learning and domain adaptation techniques to mitigate non-stationarity and reduce calibration burden. Transfer learning leverages pre-trained models from existing users or previous sessions of the same user, fine-tuning only a small subset of parameters with limited target-subject data, which reduces the need for lengthy subject-specific calibration and improves decoding performance for novice or low-performance users. Domain adaptation further aligns the feature distributions between source (e.g., training data from healthy controls or previous sessions) and target domains (e.g., real-time data from an AD patient with cognitive fluctuations), by minimizing distributional discrepancies such as maximum mean discrepancy or adversarial training, thus maintaining decoding robustness even as the user’s neural patterns drift over time. However, the computational overhead of these methods still poses challenges for real-time edge deployment, and their generalization across diverse neurodegenerative subtypes remains unproven at scale. Meanwhile, current brain-computer interface systems generally require long training periods (typically 3–5 sessions to complete motor imagery paradigm training) and heavily rely on users’ sustained attention and emotional stability. Fatigue and emotional fluctuations can further degrade signal quality and prolong calibration time. 172 , 194 , 202 Existing research mostly attributes these phenomena to individual differences in users’ neurophysiology, but a deeper systemic cause is the lack of a standardized calibration framework across laboratories: electrode placement schemes, signal preprocessing pipelines, and feature extraction thresholds vary among different research teams. This leads to calibration results that cannot be transferred across different devices for users, and some cases of “BCI illiteracy” are essentially false negatives caused by calibration mismatch. In terms of safety, the biocompatibility of invasive BCI remains to be solved. BCI requiring surgical implantation may pose a direct risk of harm to users, such as complications such as infection of surrounding tissues, acute brain trauma, or the formation of glial scars around the implant, affecting the function of the BCI. Even non-invasive BCI devices may have unknown effects on brain plasticity. Furthermore, it is unknown whether the potential side effects of BCI are reversible after device removal. 180 , 203 , 204 , 205 To practically deploy and apply a BCI system, limitations in current hardware and performance metrics must be considered. Hardware integration faces multiple constraints: limited sampling rates and channel counts for wireless devices, unstable power supply and data transmission for implantable devices, and the difficulty in compatibility of imaging technologies such as fMRI with EEG systems. 166 , 167 , 168 Performance-wise, there are conflicts between high information transmission rate requirements and classification accuracy, trade-offs between system response speed and false alarm rate, and the conflict between long-term stability and adaptive capability. 160 , 161 , 162 Signal interference and latency issues in virtual-reality integration also urgently need to be addressed. 173 , 174 In addition, the lack of unified hardware interfaces and data format standards will hinder device compatibility and large-scale deployment. 169 , 170 

From an ethical standpoint, ensuring patients’ genuine right to informed consent (especially critically ill patients with conditions such as locked-in syndrome who cannot communicate effectively) is a significant challenge. Research teams need to share moral responsibility and conduct reasonable and prudent public communication to avoid placing unrealistic expectations on patients. 206 , 207 Furthermore, whether completely paralyzed patients should be allowed to exercise their right to terminate their lives through BCIs may also become an ethical dilemma that needs to be addressed in the future. This could paradoxically improve their quality of life and will to live, but it also requires resolving technical and ethical security issues, such as ensuring the authenticity of patients’ wishes and preventing abuse. 179 Regarding privacy, BCIs may read and leak users’ thought processes (such as psychological state and personal attitudes), potentially leading to workplace discrimination or government surveillance concerns. Wireless transmission technology also faces the risk of hacker attacks, which could result in malicious manipulation of devices or leakage of sensitive information. 176 , 177 , 178 The issue of liability is equally thorny; the current legal system is unable to adequately address such issues, and some scholars suggest placing responsibility on manufacturers. 180 , 181 The potential impact of BCI on “humanity” and “personality” has also sparked controversy. Some scholars believe that BCI may blur the line between humans and machines, turning users into “cyborgs”, hybrid entities that combine organisms with mechanical or electronic systems through technological means to enhance or extend the functions of the organism. However, opponents point out that humans have long extended their capabilities through technology (such as prosthetics). The issue of personality identity is also controversial: BCI may change users’ self-perception or social identity, but some argue that such changes are no different from other medical interventions. It is worth noting that BCI, as a communication tool, may restore the social participation of severely disabled individuals, thereby maintaining their personality integrity. 182 , 183 , 184 , 208 While BCI, as an emerging technology, is likely to raise new ethical controversies, it is essentially similar to the ethical issues (such as responsibility, risk, and side effects) encountered in traditional medicine (such as intensive care). A clear ethical framework and guidelines are crucial for the responsible use of technology and the protection of users’ rights and dignity. Although there are some lessons to be learned, most of the current discussions remain at the theoretical exploration stage and still lack concrete solutions. 169 

## Discussion

BCI technology, as a disruptive innovation in the field of neural engineering, is being explored across the chain of diagnosis, treatment, and rehabilitation of neurodegenerative diseases, with the strongest evidence so far in assistive communication and adaptive neuromodulation. This systematic review shows that BCI technology, by constructing a two-way communication bridge between the brain and the device, not only offers a potentially valuable adjunct for the earlier detection of major neurological diseases such as AD, PD and ALS, but also points toward a paradigm shift from passive symptom management toward active neural function remodeling—a shift that is currently supported mainly by early-phase clinical evidence. Its core value lies in upgrading the traditional “observation-intervention” model to an intelligent closed-loop system of “monitoring-analysis-intervention-feedback”, providing a new path for tackling the global public health challenge of neurodegenerative diseases. However, its transformation from laboratory research to routine clinical application is still in the experimental stage and is constrained by a series of multifaceted challenges. Key technical challenges include improving the biocompatibility and long-term stability of implant materials, while improving electrode and sensor design to achieve more reliable neural signal acquisition. In addition, there is an urgent need to achieve greater system miniaturization and portability while improving the overall reliability and operational stability of the device. At the computational level, research focuses on optimizing signal processing algorithms to improve the accuracy and speed of neural decoding, ensuring real-time performance in practical applications. Developing more intuitive user interfaces is also crucial for improving patient interaction and accessibility. More importantly, large-scale clinical trials are needed to generate strong evidence of clinical efficacy to validate the technology and drive its widespread adoption. Beyond technical and clinical hurdles, the responsible development and deployment of BCI technology requires addressing significant ethical and privacy issues related to neural data and fostering deeper interdisciplinary collaboration among clinicians, engineers, neuroscientists, and ethicists. To ensure the healthy development of the technology, comprehensive ethical guidelines and regulatory frameworks must be established, clearly defining standards for data privacy protection, patient rights safeguards, and technological safety. Finally, strengthening international cooperation and standardization is essential. Developing unified technical specifications and data-sharing mechanisms will facilitate the rapid translation and global adoption of BCI technology. The synergistic advancement of these research directions will collectively constitute a complete path for BCI technology to move from the laboratory to clinical application, with the potential to incrementally improve the diagnosis and treatment of neurological diseases.

By establishing direct communication between the brain and external devices, BCI has begun to influence diagnostic and therapeutic practice in neurodegenerative disease, though the change is incremental and confined to a small number of applications. This review indicates that, in early-phase studies rather than in established clinical practice, the use of BCI in Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, and ALS is beginning to move from functional replacement toward functional remodeling based on neuroplasticity. Table 4 compares the three diseases across their primary clinical objectives, neural signals, major applications, evidence maturity, technical limitations, and the advantages and disadvantages of each BCI path. The shift in question lies in the construction of closed-loop systems, in which neural activity is monitored in real time and decoded output is returned as feedback. Whether such feedback stimulates the brain’s self-repair potential to an extent that reconstructs neural function is an open question; evidence is currently limited to early-phase studies of specific protocols. The combination of multimodal signal fusion and adaptive algorithms gives BCI unique advantages in early biomarker identification and personalized neuromodulation, providing a new path to overcome the global challenge of neurodegenerative diseases.

However, the clinical application of BCI technology in the field of neurodegenerative diseases still faces multidimensional challenges. At the technical level, the reliability and stability of signal acquisition remain the core bottleneck. Non-invasive BCI is susceptible to physiological artifacts (such as eye movements and EMG) and environmental noise, resulting in a low signal-to-noise ratio. While invasive BCI can acquire high-quality neural signals, issues such as biocompatibility with long-term implantation, signal attenuation, and surgical risks have not been fully resolved. Existing stimulation paradigms (such as P300, SSVEP, and MI) each have limitations: some rely on specific sensory channels, leading to fatigue, while others have insufficient response speed; hybrid paradigms, while improving robustness, increase system complexity. At the signal processing level, the non-stationarity and individual variability of EEG signals place higher demands on feature extraction and classification algorithms. Traditional linear methods struggle to capture the complex dynamics of neural signals, while non-linear methods, such as DL, although highly accurate, face problems such as insufficient training data, weak cross-subject generalization ability, and real-time computation latency. At the clinical translation level, insufficient hardware integration, poor wireless transmission stability, and the lack of a unified data interface standard limit the systematic deployment of BCI. Furthermore, user experience is a significant limiting factor, with approximately 15–30% of users exhibiting “BCI illiteracy”, being unable to generate effective signals. System performance is also susceptible to fatigue, fluctuations in attention, and emotional states, further restricting its widespread adoption. Ethical and security challenges are equally undeniable. The long-term security of invasive BCI remains unclear; the risks of cognitive privacy breaches, data security breaches, and hacking highlight the urgency of protecting neural data; and issues such as informed consent from severely disabled patients, altered identity, and attribution of responsibility require further discussion within a legal and ethical framework.

A possible future trajectory for BCI in neurodegenerative disease is an extension beyond functional replacement toward neuromodulation that senses, analyzes and intervenes dynamically; this trajectory is a research direction rather than an established outcome, and progress depends on the evidence reviewed above. The core of this transformation lies in achieving a leap from open-loop assistance to closed-loop regulation. Future BCI may no longer be limited to converting brain signals into external device commands, but will instead focus on real-time monitoring of pathological activities in specific neural circuits (such as excessively synchronized beta oscillations in PD or characteristic gamma wave abnormalities in AD), and implementing precise neural modulation accordingly. The realization of this adaptive system requires overcoming millisecond-level signal processing delays, developing targeted stimulation strategies based on individual brain network characteristics, and establishing an intelligent algorithm system capable of dynamically adjusting parameters as the disease progresses. Faced with the high heterogeneity of neurodegenerative diseases, BCI technology needs to deepen its development toward multimodal fusion and personalized intervention. A single signal modality is insufficient to comprehensively capture the complex pathophysiological processes of diseases; future development requires integrating multi-dimensional information such as electrophysiology, blood flow metabolism, and molecular imaging to construct a dynamic disease atlas across scales. By combining high temporal resolution EEG signals with high spatial resolution imaging data, BCI systems hold the promise of identifying specific neural network dysfunctions in the very early stages of disease and tailoring intervention plans for patients with different clinical subtypes. This means that BCI will transform from a general-purpose tool into a personalized diagnostic and treatment platform that can adapt to individual brain characteristics, disease stages, and even daily fluctuations in condition. Although this review focuses on neurodegenerative diseases, the closed-loop principles are conceptually transferable to psychiatric disorders such as treatment-resistant depression and autism, where similar biomarker-driven closed-loop paradigms are under investigation. 209 However, achieving widespread clinical application of BCI still requires overcoming multiple challenges from technology validation to clinical application. These include demonstrating its disease-modifying effects in delaying disease progression through large-scale clinical trials, developing low-cost, easy-to-operate hardware systems suitable for home environments, and establishing an ethical and regulatory framework that keeps pace with technological innovation. Particular attention needs to be paid to the impact of long-term brain-computer interaction on neuroplasticity, the privacy and security boundaries of neural data, and ethical and social issues such as informed consent mechanisms for severely disabled patients. Crucially, these challenges—ranging from signal stability and biocompatibility to data governance—are not confined to neurodegeneration; they represent universal hurdles equally pertinent to psychiatric BCI applications, thereby validating the cross-disease applicability of the roadmap proposed herein. Only through the deep integration of neuroscience, clinical medicine, engineering, and ethics can BCI technology truly become a key force driving the transformation of precision diagnosis and treatment for neurodegenerative diseases.
