The science of the dynamics, consequences and treatment of child sexual abuse developed rapidly after passage of the Child Abuse Prevention and Treatment Act of 1974. However, the necessity for some set of methods to both evaluate and treat the unexpected number of survivors of child abuse led to both innovation and controversy. One contribution that has deepened our understanding of child abuse is the enhanced attention to the grooming process, a term first offered to the field by Jon Conte in 1984. Virtually all experts agree that knowledge and accurate identification of this process as it is occurring would aid prevention. However, the field suffers from definitional muddle, lack of accurate prevalence and descriptive data, and limited attention to ecological validity considerations. This paper is a review of the state of this subfield. Issues raised in the paper include the lumping of unlike behaviors (consensually inappropriate and situationally dependent actions), the problem of hindsight bias, and the lack of reliable norms. This paper attempts to address these issues and to propose some directions for further work.

## Introduction

At the risk of betraying my age early in this festschrift contribution, I would first note that I vividly recall being told in graduate school that it was unlikely that I would see a case of child sexual abuse (CSA) in my clinical lifetime, as the assumed prevalence rate was one in one million in the United States, and the experience was not considered to be particularly damaging ( Kaplan et al., 1980 ). Nonetheless, an adolescent treatment center that served as one of my early placements housed ten teenagers who told me of their prior sexual abuse (although no one else had asked); my advisers assured me that this astounding “coincidence” was the largest sample of CSA survivors I would ever encounter. The decision to make trauma psychology my specialty, coming soon after both this experience and the passage of the Child Abuse Prevention and Treatment Act in 1974, meant that I was an early witness and minor contributor to the explosion of research that followed. PsycInfo, a prominent database of psychological publications, lists one publication with CSA as a keyword phrase in the 1970s, 216 in the 1980s, with a fivefold increase in the 1990s, my first years as a professor, then holding steady or inching upward each year thereafter.

This history—researchers and clinicians with limited preparation struggling to keep up with both increasing incidence rates and enhanced understanding of distress in victims over a short time period—perhaps explains the large number of controversies that remain in the CSA field. Despite immeasurable contributions by founding theoreticians/ researchers (John Briere, David Chadwick, Jon Conte, Kathleen Faller, David Finkelhor, and John E.B. Myers come to mind), the necessity for immediate interim solutions inevitably led to excesses by the field’s growing number of proponents and by its critics—for instance, simplistic checklists for victim identification, overemphasis on child suggestibility, adherence to false dichotomies between recovered and false memory, treatment approaches with inadequate scientific underpinnings, etc. There is much to be done in each of these subfields to come to balanced and scientifically founded conclusions today. I will focus on one area, but several more will briefly be noted.

A key step in understanding the dynamics of CSA, a critical issue for those focused on prevention, was the introduction of the concept of the “grooming” process—the steps taken by the perpetrator to prepare the victim to accept sexual abuse as normal behavior and/or to prevent disclosure. Grooming behaviors typically continue throughout the course of an abuse process but often begin before illegal behaviors are initiated. Our editor, Jon Conte (1984) , may have been the first to use the term in print, referencing the difficulty in recognizing this early preparatory phase. He wrote that in most sexual abuse cases “the perpetrator involves children in sexual abuse through a grooming process in which a combination of kindness, attention, material enticement, special privilege, and coercion are expertly applied” (p. 558). Since that time, an understanding of grooming has been identified as critical to both clinical and forensic evaluation, despite nearly universal recognition that the term is loosely and inconsistently applied ( Bennett & O’Donohue, 2020 ; Kaylor et al., 2022 ; Lanning, 2018 ). Further, while underlining that the term can be helpful, Lucy Berliner (2018) notes that it “can only be accurately applied in hindsight after the victimization has occurred” (p. 26). This is one of two essential difficulties of the study of grooming. The other, just as problematic for researchers, is that if grooming is successful, the abuse itself is more difficult (and sometimes impossible) to detect. The most successful grooming techniques, those that accommodate the child to abuse, thus might escape scientific scrutiny. Further, relying on child distress or child lack of comfort as determinants that the behavior is inappropriate is at odds with the typical purpose of the grooming process in the eyes of the offender; grooming is in part designed by the offender was a mitigator of those child behaviors that would signal that abuse was occurring (e.g., distress, distrust, and avoidance).

In research, behaviors differ in the likelihood of being labeled as grooming before and after the adult respondent has been told that sexual abuse eventually occurred ( Scurich et al., 2023 ; Spenard & Cash, 2022 ). A mentor spending extra time with a child to solve a math problem is kind before applying the label of sexual abuser to the adult, but taking advantage of an opportunity after the label is known is not. Even in situations in which study participants are trained to “detect” grooming, respondents are rarely able to make reliable distinctions between behaviors that the researcher labeled as grooming and those that they felt did not meet the definition ( Bennett & O’Donohue, 2020 ). Understandably, the self-imposed guilt for not recognizing and responding appropriately to behaviors that seem so obviously problematic (after the fact) is one more source of damage to the victim.

There are several facets of grooming research that could be tightened or improved. First, the definitional crisis has produced a wide range of allegedly all-encompassing suggestions for categorization of behaviors as fitting or not fitting under the “grooming” rubric. Among present choices, I prefer the concise offering of Ken Lanning (2018) , who defines grooming as “the use of nonviolent techniques by one person to gain sexual access to and control over potential and actual child victims” (p. 11). Winters et al. (2022) suggest a “universal definition” with more complexity, but it is also quite difficult to apply. Their definition states that sexual grooming is a process used “to facilitate sexual contact with a minor while simultaneously avoiding detection.” They list various stages of the process: selection of a victim, gaining access to and isolating the child, developing trust with those who might block access, and “desensitizing the minor to sexual content and physical contact.” Importantly, they include “maintenance strategies [used] on the victim to facilitate future sexual abuse and/or to prevent disclosure” (p. 7).

One problem with these definitions is that they attempt to cover two distinct sets of behaviors used by perpetrators with one term—(a) a set of behaviors that would be rejected by almost anyone in a given profession as inappropriate and (b) a set of behaviors that is quite context-dependent. The need to highlight this distinction has recently been underlined by Metcalf et al. (2026 ; Metcalf, 2025 ), who separate behaviors that they term Desensitization (D; e.g. unnecessary nudity, inappropriate language, excessive touching) from those they label Relationship Enhancement (RE; e.g. time spent in support of athletics or tutoring, listening to a child’s problems, small gifts or favors). Labeling the act of a coach giving a child a ride home from practice as on a par with having the child sit on the coach’s lap ( Winters & Jeglic, 2016 ) by merely labeling both as grooming can have negative consequences both for the field and for individual lives. First, the automatic grooming label for the ride home implies that study participants who note otherwise are incorrect, that adults never extend small kindnesses to children in need (buy lunch, sports, or music equipment for the financially strapped child, spend extra time with the child of immigrants who is struggling with reading, comfort the child who has fallen) absent nefarious motives. Metcalf et al. (2026) provided evidence that most psychology researchers and most educators would see time spent alone with a child (in the car, in tutoring, in counseling) as serving Relationship Enhancement functions, but would note that, in context, it may or may not fit the grooming definitions provided by Lanning (2018) or Winters et al. (2022) above. Second, overextension of terms can foment the types of backlash that we see so often in the history of child abuse research. Fear of false accusation ( Hoyle et al., 2023 ) is a double-edged sword, potentially reducing both concerning and harmless (or even helpful) behaviors and potentially trivializing the term itself.

One useful research direction, therefore, would be to begin to routinely differentiate these categories. Context-specific instruments could be developed that could be used to offer testimony that could be much more useful to the court than is possible with currently available information. That is, instruments could be developed that show that a given individual is displaying significantly more RE behaviors or D behaviors than (a) norms of appropriateness based on others in the given profession, (b) norms of appropriateness based on psychological research, and (c) standards of a given profession. Metcalf et al.’s (2026) work utilized educational professionals and is a step forward, but the study is the first of its type and has a number of limitations (e.g. age of the child was not controlled).

If this research direction is taken, the expert witness could pull away from the hindsight bias the colors the behavior of the individual after the fact, noting that there is external evidence that the behavior is unusual and differs from expectations. Cultural variation could be studied, and the resulting checklists could be useful administratively (e.g., RE behaviors could be allowed in a given institution, but frequency could be logged). D behaviors were more frequently seen as automatically worrisome by researchers and survey respondents, and a number of researchers ( Metcalf, 2025 ) use the term “red flag” for such behaviors. It would also be possible to develop profession-specific versions of the checklists (medicine, daycare, education).

Prevalence studies are also important for the development of the grooming field. Data on sexual misconduct by educators is extremely limited, with information on the prevalence of grooming behaviors almost nonexistent in samples that did not identify sexual abuse victims. Nonetheless, survey results suggesting a 9.6% base rate for experiences of “unwanted” contact or verbal sexual comments (language we would not use today) provided by Shakeshaft (2003) , based on a large and representative sample, remains a frequently cited figure. Given the challenges involved in asking those potentially in current exploitative relationships to hand in surveys about this exploitation at the site of their abuse, a more workable methodology might be that used by Cameron et al. (1986) and Jeglic et al. (2023) , who gathered their surveys from young adults after graduation.

There are at least two major problems with many of the surveys to date, however. First, to my knowledge, no current surveys use sufficient data integrity measures within the survey methodology to allow adequate certainty that data were trustworthy. Multiple studies show that rates of misreporting of rare disorders or traumatic experiences on anonymous surveys are quite high ( Merten et al., 2020 ). This has been best confirmed by authors asking for sufferers of nonexisting diseases or unlikely symptoms ( Meade & Craig, 2012 ). The problem is heightened when the pathological outcomes are measured by unbalanced scales, that is, scales with no reversed items, such as the Psychological Flourishing scale used in Jeglic et al. (2023) . Inclusion of reversed polarity items appear to improve accuracy in part by slowing automatic responding ( Meade & Craig, 2012 ) and can provide the researcher with a measure of likely malingering/inattentiveness through the statistical calculation of psychometric antonyms ( Goldberg & Kilkowski, 1985 ). More succinctly, surveys of prevalence of these phenomena must take into consideration the growing literature on ensuring data integrity more thoroughly. Syntax for antonym and synonym inconsistency calculation for R and SPSS is widely available.

A second problem is illustrated by Shakeshaft’s (2003) use of the label of “unwanted” contract, or the more frequent modern use of “inappropriate” sexual contact. The subgroup we lose here are those (often teenage) participants who may even now accept the perpetrator’s labels—mutual affection, love, or mentorship. This group will not typically show up in a caseload for a post-traumatic stress disorder specialist; nonetheless, the experience they had is sexual abuse and their perpetrators are often serial offenders. Study of this subgroup requires thoughtful design and more careful use of language in our questioning.

The clear evidence that sexual abusers do misuse Relationship Enhancement behaviors (including many small kindnesses) as grooming is known and presented as testimony by knowledgeable professionals. However, clear possibility for hindsight bias exists here, as jurors view context-dependent behavior through the lens of an existing accusation of child abuse perpetration. Perhaps the most carefully presented evidence for this idea is offered by Scurich et al. (2023) , who used both between-group and repeated-measures analyses, concluding that hindsight bias (here, knowledge of a conviction) doubled the size of the perceived likelihood that ambiguous behaviors were signs of sexual abuse perpetration. But again, much work remains to be done to enhance applicability and ecological validity of such results. These changes include inclusion of data integrity procedures, language that is more similar to actual judgments that jurors are asked to make in civil and criminal trials, examination of the effect of education on hindsight bias, and examination of the impact of perpetrator characteristics. Gender of the perpetrator, for instance, may have an impact on perception of victim shared responsibility and likely damages ( Russell & Gruys, 2022 ).

A final issue of great import is the cultural specificity of the grooming concept. Arguably, D behaviors will carry less cultural weight than will RE behaviors, but both types of grooming deserve more culture-specific attention than they have been given. Norms for appropriate interpersonal interaction, including use of language, touch, and exchange of information, vary strongly across both regions of the United States and cultural subgroups ( Suvilehto et al., 2019 ). A type of touch or an area of interpersonal sharing between adult and child might be entirely appropriate in one culture and broadly rebuffed (and perhaps more indicative of grooming) in another.

In conclusion, trauma psychologists have made a strong contribution to global society in their recognition of CSA and its potential consequences, including the beginnings of a science of grooming. I would urge attention to this subfield as part of our overall goal to increase accuracy in forensic decision-making. Other issues relevant to the courtroom include methods of accurate child trauma interviewing, structuring nonbiased forensic evaluations, and improving the court’s understanding of trauma memory. Trauma psychologists can take great pride in the advancements in this field, some of which have been introduced in the pages of this journal. I remain convinced of the inevitability (truth arising from the ashes) of the growing recognition, despite repeated episodes of backlash against trauma work, of the centrality of the study of trauma in understanding human growth, success, achievement and contentment.

## Author Biography

 Constance J. Dalenberg , Ph.D. is a distinguished professor at Alliant International University and Director of the Trauma Research Institute. She teaches in the area of trauma psychology, trauma research methodology, social psychology, and multivariate statistics, publishing over 100 peer-reviewed papers in these areas. She is a recipient of the APA Division 56 Lifetime Achievement Award and the International Society of Trauma and Dissociation Scientific Achievement Award. Her academic and clinical/forensic work is in San Diego, California.
