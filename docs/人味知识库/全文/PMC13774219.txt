## Abstract

Previous research shows that remembering repeated events differs from remembering single events, yet little research has examined suggestibility in adults’ memory for repeated events. In the present experiment, we investigated the effects of eyewitness misinformation and the role of a warning against misinformation in a repeated-event scenario. We examined how different types of misinformation – entirely new items versus items present in non-target instances – influence reporting. Three hundred and nine participants watched four surveillance-mission videos that shared a general story structure but differed in key details. Participants were later exposed to pieces of misinformation in a video narrative of someone else reporting the first mission. Reporting was measured using free recall, cued recall, and recognition. We replicated the standard misinformation effect in the repeated-event context. We found that the warning reduced misinformation reporting, but the effect was present only in the recognition task. Based on the source-monitoring framework, we expected it would be easier to resist external (entirely new) misinformation than misinformation consistent with what was present in non-target instances, and this was also true. The warning’s lack of efficacy may be accounted for in two ways. First, the inherent difficulty of attributing details to specific instances of repeated events may have overshadowed the potential effects of the warning. Second, ours was a general warning; it might not have been strong enough to restore an adequate understanding of a memory task distorted by misinformation.

## Introduction

Improving the accuracy of memory reports is of utmost importance in many real-life situations, and observing which factors lead to this improvement may provide valuable insights into the processes involved in remembering (Blank, 1998 ; Blank & Launay, 2014 ; Oeberst & Blank, 2012 ; Skopicz-Radkiewicz et al., 2023 ). A common challenge to accuracy is the misinformation effect, in which encountering erroneous information about an event leads to its later incorporation into a memory report (Loftus et al., 1978 ). Post-event misinformation from sources other than the event itself can target both single (i.e., unique) and repeated events. Repeated events are built from a series of similar instances, such as work team meetings or tennis lessons. Each instance is a separate event with unique characteristics but at the same time bears similarities to other instances in terms of general structure. For example, imagine a secret agent who goes undercover to infiltrate an organized crime group and, in doing so, witnesses several surveillance missions carried out by its members. Together, these missions constitute a repeated event. Each instance (i.e., each of the single missions) has its unique characteristics, such as time and place, specific goals, and outcomes. But the general pattern of preparation of the group, receiving instructions, executing the covert surveillance, and reporting back remains consistent across instances. If this agent were to later report on what happened in the first mission, it would be a report of one (target) instance of a repeated event. Unlike in single events, where post-event misinformation necessarily comes from outside the event, in repeated-event situations, post-event misinformation targeting one instance may be consistent with a detail encountered within one of the non-target instances. Since such misinformation would be experienced, although not in the target instance, it would likely be processed differently, perhaps with increased susceptibility (Rubínová et al., 2026b ) and decreased resistance to it. Warnings against the potential presence of misinformation have been shown repeatedly to reduce or eliminate the misinformation effect in single-event scenarios (Greene et al., 1982 ; see Blank & Launay, 2014 , for a meta-analysis). However, to the best of our knowledge, the effectiveness of warnings against misinformation has never been tested in the repeated-events paradigm. In the present study, we examined how different types of misinformation influence memory reports of one instance of a repeated event, and the effect of a warning against misinformation in this context.

Remembering repeated events differs from remembering single, unique events in many aspects. With repeated experiences, people tend to recall what typically happens accurately; however, reporting unique event details specific to a particular instance is usually more challenging (Deck & Paterson, 2021 ; Hudson & Nelson, 1986 ; for a review, see Dilevski et al., 2021 ). Another characteristic of memory reports of repeated events is the frequent occurrence of internal intrusion errors, that is, the misattribution of details across instances (Rubínová & Kontogianni, 2023 ; for a meta-analysis using child samples, see Woiwod et al., 2019 ). For example, an undercover agent reporting on one of the missions might mistakenly recall that during the phone call the group communicated that they finished ahead of time, when in fact during this specific phone call they received further instructions, and the “ahead of time” communication belonged to a different mission. Consequently, memory reports of repeated events tend to be less accurate than reports of single events (Deck & Paterson, 2021 ).

These differences in reporting reflect the different nature of remembering single versus repeated events. First, with several similar instances, it becomes more challenging to monitor the source of information correctly (i.e., to attribute the retrieved pieces of information to their source, which in this case is a particular instance; Johnson et al., 1993 ; Lindsay, 2008 , 2014 ). Second, with repeated events, people form memories of specific, individual instances, but they also form scripts – general event representations that contain information about the typical content and temporal order of events (Fivush, 2002 ; Hudson & Mayhew, 2009 ; Rubínová et al., 2021 ). Individual instances of repeated events contribute to the formation of the overarching memory representation of the general structure of the event (Deck et al., 2026 ; Rubínová et al., 2021 ). As Bontkes et al. ( 2025a ) point out, remembering repeated events relies on both episodic and semantic memory, as it involves aspects such as the unique spatial–temporal context of each instance (e.g., Rubin & Umanath, 2015 ; Tulving, 1972 ), as well as more semantic aspects connected to script memory (e.g., Fivush, 1984 ; Schank & Abelson, 1977 ). In this sense, remembering repeated events, as compared to single events, typically involves more semantic aspects (Bontkes et al., 2025b ). Studying resistance to misinformation in the repeated-events context can inform our understanding of remembering repeated events by observing how it is affected by factors such as more careful source monitoring, better understanding of the questioning situation, or shifting the report criterion.

## The misinformation effect: The single- and repeated-event paradigms

The misinformation effect – the tendency to incorporate inaccurate post-event information into a subsequent memory report – has been replicated in hundreds, possibly thousands, of studies using a variety of materials and methodologies since Loftus et al.’s seminal study ( 1978 ; e.g., Blank, 1998 ; Blank et al., 2022 ; Meade & Roediger, 2002 ; Saraiva et al., 2021 ; Skagerberg & Wright, 2008 ; Underwood & Pezdek, 1998 ; Zaragoza & Mitchell, 1996 ; for reviews, see Chrobak & Zaragoza, 2013 ; Zaragoza et al., 2007 ). Yet the predominant paradigm has been a single-event scenario: participants witness an event, are exposed to post-event material involving misinformation, and provide a memory report. But people’s daily lives are composed of repeated events: school classes, work assignments, tennis lessons, or play activities sharing the same structure, to name just a few (Bontkes et al., 2025a ), and some repeated experiences may become of interest in forensic contexts (such as mobbing or domestic abuse; Deck & Paterson, 2021 ; Dilevski et al., 2024 , 2025 ; Sullivan et al., 1992 ; Tidmarsh et al., 2012 ).

In repeated-event paradigms, researchers have extensively examined the susceptibility of child participants to misinformation introduced via suggestive questions, typically by comparing children who experienced a single versus a repeated event (for a meta-analytic review, see Woiwod et al., 2019 ). In these experiments, misinformation typically targeted two categories of details: fixed details consistent across all instances of the repeated event, and variable details , which belong to a single semantic category yet differ systematically between instances. For example, Connolly and Lindsay ( 2001 ) compared children’s suggestibility in single versus repeated play sessions (four of them), which involved paper-folding and a magic trick. Each session involved building a paper model on the floor, repeating a phrase about folding paper, waving the silver wand, and saying assigned magic words while standing around the table (fixed details). The type of paper model (sailboat, house, etc.), wording of the phrases, or type of magic trick performed were variable details. Single-event children, naturally, experienced only one variant of every detail. Connolly and Lindsay ( 2001 ) found that repeated-event children were less susceptible to misleading information targeting fixed details (see Powell et al., 1999 , for similar results). Variable details, however, are more important from the perspective of the present study, since only they were targeted with misinformation. In previous studies, the results on children’s suggestibility concerning the variable details were mixed, since in some studies repeated-event children were more suggestible regarding variable details (e.g., Connolly & Lindsay, 2001 ; Powell & Roberts, 2002 , when using yes/no recognition test; Price & Connolly, 2004 ), and in others, repeated-event children were not more suggestible (e.g., Powell & Roberts, 2002 , when using cued recall; Powell et al., 1999 ).

Much less attention has been paid to studying the misinformation effect in repeated-event contexts in adult samples. Dilevski et al. ( 2025 ) compared the impact of a biasing questionnaire (using only external/non-experienced misinformation) on single- versus repeated-event reports and found no differences in suggestibility. Their study employed suggestive questioning, whereas another study examining susceptibility to misinformation in single- versus repeated-event scenarios (Dilevski et al., 2024 , Experiment 2) utilized a classic misinformation paradigm. However, the latter study observed a generally low rate of suggestibility, which could be due to misinformation targeting rather memorable aspects of the event (Dilevski et al., 2024 ; see also Loftus, 1979 ).

A crucial factor regulating susceptibility to misinformation in repeated-event scenarios is the type of misleading information: whether it was experienced in other non-target instances ( internal misinformation ) or was not experienced before ( external misinformation ). Following the example of the type of paper-folding activity used as variable detail, imagine a child is being interviewed about what happened in the first play session. If they were building a kite in the first session, and the sailboat in the second, they can receive post-event information consistent with a second instance – “a sailboat” – which would constitute internal misinformation. In a study with children, Powell et al. ( 2007 ) found that pieces of internal misinformation were more likely to be accepted into children’s reports than non-experienced misleading details. Sharman and Danby ( 2022 ) observed a similar pattern in adults’ suggestibility: experienced non-target details were endorsed more than non-experienced details. Importantly, using misinformation embedded in test questions, Sharman and Danby ( 2022 ) employed suggestive questioning and measured the misinformation effect in responses to those questions. Susceptibility to internal versus external misinformation was also tested in the classic misinformation paradigm, where higher misinformation endorsement was found for internal than for external misinformation in free and cued recall (Rubínová et al., 2026b ).

As Rubínová et al. ( 2026b ) point out, Signal Detection Theory makes it possible to form predictions regarding the endorsement of internal versus external misinformation. Since internal misinformation details were part of the original event, their memory strength is higher than that of external misinformation details. Figure  1 presents simulated distributions of memory strength for target instance items and internal and external misinformation items. Target instance items overlap with internal misinformation items to a greater extent than with external misinformation items; therefore, at any level of the memory-strength criterion, internal misinformation has a greater chance of being recalled than external misinformation.

In sum, the SDT perspective, as well as studies with both adult and child samples, indicate that resistance to internal misinformation is more challenging than resistance to external misinformation. Therefore, we expect that internal misinformation will be more disruptive to memory performance than external misinformation. Since source decisions are more complex for internal than for external misinformation, we also expect that internal misinformation conditions will yield a poorer confidence–accuracy alignment than external misinformation conditions.

## Warnings against misinformation

Numerous studies on warnings against misinformation demonstrate that warnings are effective in reducing the misinformation effect (see Blank & Launay, 2014 , for a meta-analysis). Some types of warnings are especially effective, such as enlightenment warnings – disclosure of the study’s real purpose to participants during the experiment (a procedure similar to debriefing, but embedded in an experiment; e.g., Blank, 1998 ; Greenspan & Loftus, 2022 ; Highhouse & Bottrill, 1995 ; Oeberst & Blank, 2012 ). However, many kinds of warnings can reliably reduce the misinformation effect. These include statements about the (possible) presence of misinformation (e.g., Wright, 1993 ; Zaragoza & Lane, 1994 ), discrediting the misleading source as incompetent or untrustworthy (e.g., Dodd & Bradshaw, 1980 ; Echterhoff et al., 2005 ), or a mixture of both (Greene et al., 1982 ). In the present study, a warning stated that event reports from others could include information inconsistent with the event. The warning also emphasized that participants should rely on their memory of the target event when responding to questions. Notably, warnings can be administered before or after encountering misinformation. The present study focuses on post-warnings, since this scenario has greater ecological validity: in many real-life situations requiring accurate memory reports, interventions can only occur immediately before retrieval. How do post-warnings work?

In essence, post-warnings undermine processes that would otherwise lead to the misinformation effect (Blank & Launay, 2014 ). Blank and Launay outlined three major mechanisms underlying the misinformation effect: (1) impaired ability to remember original information targeted by misinformation, (2) biased responding in favor of the misinformation, and (3) source misattribution. Importantly, they added a fourth mechanism tied to memory conversion processes: instead of focusing on memory of information, its source, and information and source accessibility, the authors argued that participants’ understanding of the memory task may guide their responses. Specifically, after encountering misinformation, participants are often left with suboptimal task representation, having been led to believe that the post-event material was an accurate account of what originally happened (Oeberst & Blank, 2012 ). Warnings can correct participants’ representation of the memory task, effectively prompting more careful memory search and source monitoring rather than providing answers based on availability (Blank & Launay, 2014 ; Oeberst & Blank, 2012 ).

From the source-monitoring perspective, people endorse misinformation when they incorrectly attribute their memories to the source (see Echterhoff et al., 2005 ; Johnson et al., 1993 ; Mitchell & Johnson, 2000 ). Importantly, according to the Source Monitoring Framework (Johnson et al., 1993 ; Lindsay, 2008 , 2014 ), the source is inferred by an individual at the time of retrieval, so it is a “property of a signal,” not a constant characteristic of an encoded piece of information. As such, source monitoring is a cognitive process that can be influenced by situational factors, such as the credibility of the post-event source (e.g., Pena et al., 2017 ; Skagerberg & Wright, 2009 ). When participants are oriented towards scrutinizing the sources of their memories, they commit fewer source misattribution errors (Lindsay, 2008 ).

There is some empirical support for the claim that warnings can indeed improve source monitoring. First, when participants are instructed to make source decisions (i.e., decide whether they remember a particular detail from an original event, post-event material, or both), the misinformation effect is usually reduced (e.g., Lindsay & Johnson, 1989 ; Multhaup et al., 1999 ; Zaragoza & Lane, 1994 ). Second, Pena et al. ( 2017 ) observed that perceptions of the source’s credibility are inversely related to the accuracy of source monitoring. Since warnings tend to reduce the credibility of post-event sources (make people more cautious), this likely also translates into better source monitoring. Third, Echterhoff et al. ( 2005 ) found that warned participants rated the reality characteristics (see Johnson et al., 1988 ) of original event items more accurately: they assigned higher ratings of visual details, vividness, and clarity of spatial context to these items, compared to not-warned witnesses. In the same study, warnings also increased the time to respond to questions about items mentioned in the post-event material. In other words, warnings can effectively motivate eyewitnesses to put more effort into source monitoring (Echterhoff et al., 2005 ). How could warnings impact the retrieval of repeated events following misinformation exposure?

The presence of a warning should also increase resistance to misinformation in the context of repeated events, though perhaps to a different degree than in single-event situations, depending on the type of misinformation. With exposure to a repeated event, the correct attribution of a piece of information to a specific instance is often a challenge. Given suboptimal source monitoring in recall of instances of repeated events and the frequent occurrence of internal intrusions, warnings should arguably be more effective in reducing the endorsement of external than internal misinformation. In addition, it is possible that warning effects would go beyond resistance to misinformation and impact the general recall of repeated events. If this is the case, warnings should also result in fewer misattributions from non-target instances.

Warnings can also negatively impact the accuracy and/or quantity of memory reports. Echterhoff et al. ( 2007 ) found that after a warning participants rejected some original event details correctly reported by the post-event source (see also Szpitalak & Polczyk, 2010 , 2011 ). They referred to this finding as the tainted truth effect , since the details reported by the post-event source (vs. not reported) were “tainted” by their inclusion in the post-event material.

The tainted truth studies referenced above focused on the recall of non-critical items (what could be described as filler items in misinformation effect studies). Blank and Launay ( 2014 ) point out that post-warnings can make participants more skeptical of any piece of information that cannot be clearly attributed to the original information and feels familiar. This could be framed as a general overcorrection mechanism towards any information that was, or could have been, mentioned by the post-event source. In our study, both control items (reported by the “co-witness” in general terms) and original information repeated items (accurately repeated in their original form) were used. We believe that the overcorrection mechanism in the warned group will mostly apply to the latter.

## Present study

To the best of our knowledge, the present experiment is the first investigation of the effectiveness of warnings against misinformation in the context of repeated events. We presented participants with a series of videos representing a repeated event. Participants then watched a co-witness statement targeting the first video. The statement contained accurately repeated information (original event details repeated in the post-event material), control details mentioned with low specificity, external misinformation (details not presented in any of the videos), and internal misinformation (details that were part of non-target instances of the repeated event; see Table  1 for an explanation and examples). We had four main goals. First, to replicate the standard misinformation effect in a repeated-events scenario. Second, to replicate the higher levels of misinformation endorsement for internal than for external misinformation in memory reports of repeated events (Rubínová et al., 2026b ). Third, to investigate, for the first time, the impact of a warning against misinformation targeting one instance of a repeated event, focusing separately on internal and external misinformation. Fourth, to examine participants’ confidence ratings, as potentially informative in terms of mechanisms underlying warning effects in repeated-event contexts. We formulated and preregistered seven hypotheses. Memory performance (reporting misleading details, correct details, and internal intrusions) was probed by three different measures: free recall, cued recall, and recognition.

## Preregistered hypotheses

First, we expected to observe the misinformation effect: lower accuracy for misled items (internal and external misinformation combined) compared to items not targeted with misinformation (control and repeated items combined). Second, we expected internal misinformation to be more disruptive than external misinformation for memory performance, as expressed in measures of accuracy and misinformation endorsement. We expected lower accuracy and higher misinformation endorsement for items targeted with internal misinformation compared to external misinformation. Third, we expected that the warning would reduce the misinformation effect: warned participants would provide more accurate responses and report less misinformation compared to their counterparts who were not warned.

Following the commencement of data collection and before data analysis, we realized that we had overlooked preregistering a crucial hypothesis for our paradigm: the difference in the effectiveness of warnings for internally and externally misled items. Previous studies have argued that post-warnings might prompt more accurate assessment of reality characteristics for original event items, effectively reducing the impact of misinformation presented in a narrated form (Echterhoff et al., 2005 ). In repeated-event contexts, this enhanced accuracy of reality characteristics assessment should help reduce the negative impact of external misinformation, but not of internal misinformation. The rationale is that external misinformation details were not viewed in any of the instances, whereas internal misinformation details were viewed as part of the non-target experimental videos. Therefore, assessment of the reality characteristics of internal misinformation details cannot help in realization that the information was encountered outside of the target instance (it does not follow, however, that this realization cannot be achieved by other mechanisms, such as boosted source monitoring). In sum, we expected warnings to be more effective in reducing misinformation endorsement and increasing correct responding for external misinformation details than for internal misinformation details.

Fourth, we expected warned participants to report fewer internal intrusions and more accurate responses for control items than not-warned participants. Fifth, we expected that original information repeated items would yield more correct responses than control items. Sixth, consistent with the tainted truth effect, we expected that warnings would negatively impact the accurate reporting of original information repeated items, but not of control items that were not specifically mentioned.

Finally, we made predictions about the confidence–accuracy relationship in different conditions. We argued above that source-monitoring is much more difficult for internal than for external items, and familiarity with items from other non-target instances may lead to inflated confidence. Therefore, we expected that externally misinformed items would lead to a better confidence–accuracy relationship than internally misinformed items. Relatedly, previous research (e.g., Pena et al., 2017 ; Weingardt et al., 1994 ) has shown that misled participants held their erroneous responses with greater confidence than non-misled participants. Therefore, although not preregistered, we expected that the confidence–accuracy relationship would be superior for non-misled items (control and original information repeated items collapsed) than for misled items (internally and externally misinformed items collapsed).

## Exploratory analyses

Retrospective confidence often adequately reflects the accuracy of the target memory (e.g., Brewer & Wells, 2006 ; Costermans et al., 1992 ; Mandler & Boeck, 1974 ; Perfect & Hollins, 1996 ; Perfect et al., 1993 ; Tulving & Thomson, 1971 ), but cases of confidence-accuracy dissociation have been observed in numerous and various contexts (e.g., Brewer & Sampaio, 2006 ; Koriat, 2012 , 2018 ; Leippe et al., 2009 ; Odinot et al., 2008 ; Schwartz et al., 2020 ; Tulving, 1981 ; see Schwartz, 2024 , for a review). We have described at length the inherent difficulty of reporting instances of repeated events. The task difficulty, however, does not have to be accompanied by participants’ full awareness of this difficulty, perhaps especially when taking into account the potentially high fluency of retrieval of many candidate responses (see, e.g., Schwartz, 2024 , on review of how retrieval fluency affects retrospective confidence judgements). The presence of misinformation is another likely factor to contribute to overconfidence. For example, previous research demonstrated that reporting misinformation was accompanied by confidence equal to reporting correct event details (Loftus et al., 1989 ; Luna & Migueles, 2009 ), and Higham et al. ( 2017 ) describe compromised accuracy with accompanying high confidence as the signature pattern of misinformation. One of the warning’s functions is to draw participants’ attention to the fact that the accessed candidate answer(s) can be erroneous, and to elicit their more careful evaluation (alongside other related mechanisms, like prompting more careful source monitoring and better task representation). Therefore, we believe that warning presentation might lead to a better confidence-accuracy alignment. More specifically, if overconfidence is observed, the presence of the warning should reduce it.

## Method

## Design

The study was a 2 × 4 mixed design. The Presence of a warning was a between-subjects factor with two levels (warning present vs. absent). The Type of detail was a within-subjects factor with four levels (original information repeated, control, internal misinformation, external misinformation) and reflected the type of information presented in the post-event material. Table  1 provides descriptions of detail types with examples for one of the items (location).

For the sake of stimulus variation, we also manipulated the target video, i.e., the first video of the repeated event (A or D, counterbalanced across participants). The type of detail was counterbalanced across items, with each type represented by two stimulus items (i.e., eight target items); see Table  2 for a selection of items for this manipulation. There were four versions of the post-event misinformation narrative per target video (A and D), resulting in eight versions of the task. Adding the between-subjects manipulation of warning, we effectively had 16 versions of the experimental stimuli, with participants randomly assigned to one of these versions.

## Participants

## Sample size rationale

Our power analysis was based on the expected effect of the warning (Hypothesis 3). We anticipated a small effect because the misinformation effect, operationalized as the difference in accuracy between control items and misled items, is likely to be smaller in repeated-event than in single-event contexts: in repeated events, control-condition accuracy would be reduced by misattribution errors. With a smaller misinformation effect, there would be less scope to observe the impact of the warning. Therefore, we set Cohen’s h  = 0.37 as our smallest effect size of interest (Lakens, 2022 ) and determined that, with an alpha of.05 and 90% power, 300 participants (150 per experimental group) would be necessary, which we deemed feasible. To ensure counterbalancing across stimulus versions, we planned to recruit 304 participants.

## Inclusion criteria

We used the preregistered participation criteria: (1) age between 18 and 60 years old, (2) normal or corrected-to-normal vision, (3) no hearing impairment, and (4) native Polish language use. Additionally, we chose not to recruit psychologists and psychology students since previous research (e.g., Niedźwieńska, 2004 ) has demonstrated that psychological education can influence the accuracy of memory reports. Participants received 50 PLN (~ 14 USD) in remuneration. Two subjects participated in exchange for a course credit.

## Sample size and exclusions

Our resources allowed a slight oversampling, so we recruited 309 participants for the study. Based on the preregistered exclusion criteria, we excluded 11 participants (ten participants reported being inattentive in at least some of their responses, and one participant reported less than 10% attention for one of the experimental videos). Data from 298 participants were included in the analyses (227 women, 70 men, one person chose not to disclose their gender; M age  = 22.37 years, SD  = 4.54). Although the age inclusion criteria were broad (18–60 years), the vast majority of participants were young adults, with only 3% older than 35 years. More than half of the sample was 21 years of age or younger. To ensure that potential age effects did not influence memory performance, we examined whether there were significant age differences based on the between-subjects factor (the presence of a warning) and found none, and the lack of differences was confirmed using the Bayesian ANOVA. Results of these additional analyses and the exact age distribution in the sample are available in the Online Supplementary Material on the Open Science Framework (OSF).

## Materials

## Translation and piloting

We used the materials developed by Kontogianni et al. ( 2021 ) and adapted by Rubínová et al., ( 2026a , 2026b ) and Rubínová et al. ( 2026b ). All materials were translated into Polish by the first author, and the translation was evaluated by a colleague fluent in both English and Polish. We conducted a pilot study with 41 participants. Following evaluation of the pilot data, we revised our translation of some questions to help participants provide more specific and informative responses. The experiment was run in PyCharm.

## Videos

All participants watched four videos described as recordings of surveillance missions. Each video was overlaid with a narrative describing the installation of a surveillance device (web camera, audio recorder, small computer, or Wi-Fi router) at a distinct urban location: a train station, an area near a viewing tower, a university building, and a public park. The videos followed the same general pattern (a three-person group undertaking steps to gather information about people in a public place), but each incorporated unique elements (e.g., the spying device, the location, the purpose of the mission, and the post-mission meeting point; see Table  2 , columns A–D, for a list of video-specific items).

Video duration ranged from 1.23 to 1.45 min. The videos were presented in two orders: ABCD or DABC, with either video A or D serving as the target mission featured in the subsequent misinformation narrative.

## Post-event narrative

Before watching the surveillance videos, participants were asked to imagine they were undercover agents participating in a mission. The post-event material was also presented in a video format and introduced as a memory report of another person: someone who had been involved in the witnessed event in a role similar to the one the participant had been asked to imagine (see Procedure ). The account was designed to resemble an authentic memory report. To achieve this, the narrator reported only some of the items from the mission video, mirroring the natural process of forgetting. Out of the 15 unique items, four were omitted, two were always accurately reported, one was always mentioned in general terms, and the remaining eight details were systematically manipulated so that every version of the post-event account contained two items corresponding to each Type of detail (original information repeated, control, external misinformation, internal misinformation; see Table  2 for the general pattern of implementation of items in the post-event narratives, and the Online Supplementary Materials for the full variation and counterbalancing of details across each post-event narrative).

Each version of the post-event video featured the same person, and the length of the videos ranged from 1 min and 13 s to 1 min and 21 s.

## Warning

The content of the warning was designed to draw participants’ attention to the fact that the event report presented to them could be erroneous. The warning also explicitly indicated that participants should base their responses only on their memory of the event, not on any other source of information or consideration. The warned participants read the following information:

People’s reports of events can sometimes include mistakes or contain information that is inconsistent with the event. It is important that in your responses to the questions below, you rely on what you remember from the first mission, and not on any other information.

## Procedure

The experiment was administered on a computer in a single laboratory session, lasting approximately 50 min. Participants were instructed to read the instructions carefully and be attentive towards any tasks they were given. They were informed that during the experiment they would watch videos, complete various computer-based tasks, and answer some questions. The real purpose of the experiment (misinformation processing in the context of repeated events and the effects of warning) was not disclosed. It was, however, indicated that the study focuses on the processing of memory information in different contexts.

After demographic and screening questions and a sound test, participants were asked to imagine they were a secret agent who had infiltrated a group of terrorists and participated in a series of missions with them. They were told that they would later be required to report information about the mission. Then they were presented with four mission videos. After each video, there was a question about attention paid to the content, and a short arithmetic task (see Fig.  2 for a visual representation of the procedure flow). After watching a sequence of mission videos, participants were given a different distractor task (anagrams, 7 min).
 Fig. 2 
Schematic representation of the experimental procedure

Next, participants read that another agent was working undercover on the same mission as them and that she was also waiting to report on what happened. The post-event material was introduced as her account of the first mission. Participants who watched target video A watched one of the four post-event accounts describing video A, and those who watched target video D watched one of the four post-event accounts describing video D. This was followed by another set of anagrams (7 min). After that, those participants who were assigned to the warning conditions were presented with the warning. The warning was presented in the center of the screen as a short instruction (41 words in the original Polish version). Participants needed to confirm that they had read it in order to go to the next steps of the procedure. As with other study instructions, participants could read the warning as long as they chose to, but the minimum time of the warning presentation was 10 s (the timer prevented skipping the warning).

The last block of the experiment consisted of retrieval tasks. Participants’ reporting was tested using the free recall, cued recall, and recognition questions, always in that order, with all participants receiving the same questions. In the free-recall phase, participants were instructed to write down everything they remembered from the first mission as comprehensively as possible, without guessing. In the cued-recall phase, they were instructed to respond to questions based on what they remembered about the first mission, i.e., the video they had watched first, and received nine questions: eight questions about eight critical items (see the Design and Materials sections), and one cueing question about the device that was used in the first story. After each question, they were asked, “How confident are you that this is the correct answer to this question?”, and they used a slider to provide their confidence rating (0–100%; anchored at 50). Then, participants were informed that they would again receive questions about the first mission, but this time they would be required to choose the correct answer. They were also informed that they would be asked about their confidence in the provided responses, and from where they remembered the information (the source question was specific to the recognition task). The recognition questions were the same as the cued-recall questions, and the confidence ratings were collected in the same manner as in the cued-recall task. Each recognition question involved six potential responses. From the participant’s perspective, the options to choose represented: a correct response from the target video, details from non-target instances, new detail(s), and, if the item was targeted with misinformation, internal or external misinformation. One option always constituted a correct response. Three options were derived from the three non-target videos, and depending on counterbalancing, one of these options could, at the same time, constitute internal misinformation. Two options were external to the repeated event, meaning they were not part of any of the mission videos, target or non-target. They were either both entirely new (introduced only in the questions) or one of them constituted external misinformation (again, depending on the counterbalancing).

After each recognition question and a confidence rating, participants were asked, “Now think about the response you provided. Do you remember this piece of information from the recording of a mission, or the person’s account of the mission, or both?” After choosing one of the three options (video, account, or both), they were directed to the next recognition question, followed by a confidence rating and a source question.

At the end, participants read that we aim to gather high-quality data and were asked to assess if they were attentive in their responses to the questions, or if at least some of their responses had been given inattentively. They were informed that the response to this question is also anonymous. We later excluded data from participants who reported being inattentive.

## Coding of responses

We followed the coding scheme developed by Rubínová et al. ( 2026b ). Data from the pilot study were used for training coding, and based on insights gained during this training, the coding manual was subsequently revised. Raters validated the responses based on their semantic content rather than a verbatim reproduction of the video. All cued-recall and free-recall responses were independently coded by two raters who were blind to the experimental conditions. 1 

For the cued-recall responses, inter-rater agreement was strong, with Cohen’s kappa values ranging from .72 to .96 ( M  = .86; SD  = 0.09). In the case of free recall, coding for one item had substantial agreement (with Cohen’s kappa value of .64), and coding of the remaining eight items resulted in almost perfect agreement, with Cohen’s kappa values ranging from .87 to .97.

All discrepancies between the raters’ assessments were discussed, and consensus evaluations were included in the analyses.

## Statistical analyses

Our preregistered hypotheses focusing on accuracy (Hypotheses 1–6) involved three dependent variables: correct responding, misinformation endorsement, and internal intrusions. Table 3 provides definitions and ranges for these dependent variables. Misinformation endorsement was only measured on two levels of the within-subject factor: internal and external misinformation (i.e., only for items where misinformation was present). Hypotheses 1–6 were tested using a mixed ANOVA model with the presence of a warning as a between-subjects factor and Type of detail as a within-subjects factor. For our predictions concerning correct responding (Hypotheses 2a, 3a, 4a, 5, and 6), the Type of detail was included in the model with all four levels (original information repeated, control, internal misinformation, and external misinformation), and the same was true for predictions regarding reporting of internal intrusions (Hypothesis 4b). We used contrast comparisons to compare the four detail type conditions. Since misinformation endorsement was not measured for items not subjected to misinformation (non-misled items), predictions concerning misinformation endorsement were tested using a model including only internal and external misinformation conditions, as two levels of Type of detail (Hypotheses 2b and 3b).

In Hypothesis 1, we were interested in the difference in the frequency of correct reporting of misled items and non-misled items. For this analysis, the within-subjects factor consisted of a comparison of non-misled (both repeated and control) items on one side, and misled items (including both internal and external misinformation conditions) on the other.

For accuracy measures (correct responses, misinformation endorsement, and internal intrusions), participants’ responses were probed in three different memory tasks (free recall, cued recall, and recognition). We preregistered the calculation of these measures as proportions; however, calculating them as frequencies better fit our data in cued and free recall, where not all items were reported. Therefore, all reported analyses regarding the accuracy measures are based on frequencies.

Hypothesis 7 focused on the confidence–accuracy relationship, and confidence was only probed in cued recall and recognition. To evaluate Hypothesis 7, we constructed and subsequently compared confidence–accuracy characteristic curves by plotting mean accuracy along with 95% confidence intervals of the mean values for each detail type and warning condition. We binned confidence ratings to three levels: high (81–100%), medium (41–80%), and low (0–40%).

All analyses were run in JASP. Here, we always report the results in the order reflecting the experimental procedure: first, the free-recall task, followed by the cued-recall task, and then by the recognition test. In the free-recall task, we expected a high incidence of not-recalled items, with most critical items either not mentioned at all or mentioned at a general level, not allowing them to be attributed as correct, misinformation endorsement, or misattribution. Therefore, the cued-recall and recognition tests were more important in addressing our predictions, and for this reason we present the graphs displaying the recognition and cued-recall data in the Results section.

As preregistered, null results were followed by Bayesian ANOVAs. Bayesian ANOVAs were conducted using the default JZS priors (r = 0.5 for fixed effects and r = 1 for random effects), which correspond to Cauchy priors. Here, we report the Bayesian results corresponding to the non-significant results in the frequentist analyses, but the full model comparisons are available in the Online Supplementary Material. We report the results using Bayes factors, which describe the strength of evidence for one model relative to another; for example, the null hypothesis relative to the alternative hypothesis. They express how much one’s belief in one model versus another should be updated after having observed the data (Dienes, 2021 ; Lakens et al., 2020 ). For example, a Bayes factor of 12 means that the observed data are 12 times more likely under the alternative hypothesis than under the null one. A Bayes factor of 0.04 (1/25) means that the observed data are 25 times more likely under the null hypothesis than under the alternative. We interpreted the Bayes factors in terms of the strength of evidence (moderate, strong, etc.) according to Jeffreys’ ( 1961 ) guidelines (see also Dienes & Mclatchie, 2018 ).

We conducted Bayesian mixed-factor ANOVAs. When a factor of interest (e.g., warning) was not included in the best-fitting model – that is, the model under which the data were most likely – we report the Bayes factor for exclusion of that factor (BF exclusion ). BF exclusion expresses how much more likely the observed data are under the model without this factor than under the model including the factor. In cases where the factor was included in the model that best represented the data, we ran the post hoc tests and reported the BF 01 . BF 01 greater than 1 for a particular comparison would express relative evidence for the null hypothesis (i.e., lack of difference) over the alternative.

Following our preregistered analysis plan, we also ran all analyses using (generalized) linear mixed models with a planned maximum random-effects structure (Barr et al., 2013 ). These analyses are also reported in the Online Supplementary Material.

## Results

## Correct responses

The first of our preregistered predictions concerned the effect of misinformation presence (internal and external misinformation combined) on reporting correct information. Exposure to misinformation decreased the number of correct responses compared to no-misinformation conditions (original information repeated and control combined). 2 We found a significant main effect of misinformation presence in the free recall, F (1,296) = 8.00, p  = .005, η p 2  = 0.03, cued recall, F (1,296) = 13.99, p  < .001, η p 2  = 0.05, and the recognition test, F (1,296) = 19.15, p  < .001, η p 2  = 0.06, thus providing support for H1. This is the standard misinformation effect, replicated in the context of repeated events.

All remaining tests for the measure of correct responses included four Types of detail as separate conditions. The effects of the two factors (warning and Type of detail) on correct responses are presented in Fig.  3 . There was a significant main effect of Type of detail in the model of correct responses in free recall, F (2.80, 830.54) = 7.33, p  < .001, η p 2  = 0.02; cued recall, F (2.93, 867.10) = 10.46, p  < .001, η p 2  = 0.03; and recognition, F (3,888) = 12.41; p  < .001; η p 2  = 0.04. A planned contrast comparing original information repeated items and control items revealed that participants provided significantly more correct responses to the questions regarding the repeated items. This was true in the free-recall test, t (296) = 3.45, p  < .001, Cohen’s d  = 0.31, the cued-recall test, t (296) = 3.84, p  < .001, Cohen’s d  = 0.36, and the recognition test, t (296) = 4.21, p  < .001, Cohen’s d  = 0.34, thus providing support for H5.
 Fig. 3 
The effects of warning and type of detail on correct responses in cued recall (left), and recognition (right). CR = cued recall, Rec = recognition, NoW = No warning condition, W = warning condition. The misinformation effect was measured as a difference between non-misled and misled items. The graph demonstrates that the original information repeated items drove the effect

However, planned contrasts comparing the effects of internal versus external misinformation on correct responses were not significant in any of the three memory tasks. The non-significant comparisons in the free recall, p  = .586, cued recall, p  = .721, and recognition, p  = .418, were followed by Bayesian post hoc tests comparing internal and external misinformation conditions. Our data provided strong evidence for the null hypothesis over the alternative, with BF 01  = 13.33 for free recall, BF 01  = 14.53 for cued recall, and BF 01  = 11.10 for recognition.

One of the main aims of this research was to examine how the presence of the warning affects participants’ reporting. We did not find a significant effect of warning on correct responding in any of the memory tests. In the free recall, we did not find a significant effect of warning on the frequency of correct responses, F (1,296) = 0.15, p  = .700. This was confirmed in the Bayesian mixed-factor ANOVA, with BF exclusion  = 16.41, indicating the data are 16.41 times more likely under the model without warning than under the model with warning. The non-significant main effect of warning in the cued recall, F (1,296) = 0.17, p  = .683, was also followed by the Bayesian mixed-factor ANOVA, which indicated that the model with only Type of detail as a factor was the best-performing one. As in free recall, our data provided strong evidence for excluding the warning factor from the model of correct responses in cued recall (BF exclusion  = 18.70) compared to the model that included the warning. In the recognition test, we found no significant effect of warning on correct responding, F (1,296) = 1.74, p  = .189, ηp 2  = 0.01, and no significant interaction of warning and Type of detail ( p  = .376). These non-significant effects were confirmed in the Bayesian mixed-factor ANOVA: the model with only Type of detail as a factor was the best-performing model (with extreme evidence of being better than the null); there was moderate evidence for excluding the factor of warning from the model (BF exclusion  = 7.92), and very strong evidence for excluding the interaction (BF exclusion  = 61.23). In sum, contrary to our predictions, our data suggest that the presence of a warning did not influence correct responding, with moderate to strong Bayesian evidence supporting the null compared to the alternative model.

## Misinformation endorsement

We were interested in the participants’ susceptibility and resistance to the two types of misinformation: internal and external. A summary of the comparisons between conditions is presented in Fig.  4 . In free recall, we did not find any differences in endorsement based on internal versus external misinformation presentation, F (1,296) = 2.65, p  = .105, η p 2  = 0.01. This null effect was supported by the Bayesian mixed-factor ANOVA: the data were best represented by the null model. In the cued recall, we found the difference in the expected direction (Hypothesis 2b): misinformation endorsement was significantly higher for internal than for external misinformation, F (1,296) = 9.28, p  = .003, η p 2  = 0.03. In the recognition test, we did not find a statistically significant difference between the two conditions, F (1, 296) = 3.11, p  = .079, η p 2  = 0.01. A non-significant effect of Type of detail was followed by the Bayesian mixed-factor ANOVA, but this time the results were inconclusive, with BF exclusion  = 2.63, which constitutes anecdotal evidence according to the accepted guidelines (Jeffreys, 1961 ).
 Fig. 4 
The effects of warning and type of detail on misinformation endorsement in cued recall ( left ) and recognition ( right ). CR = cued recall, Rec = recognition, NoW = No warning condition, W = warning condition

Similarly to no effects of warning on correct responses, we found no effect of warning on misinformation endorsement in the free recall, F (1,296) = 2.70, p  = .101, η p 2  = 0.01, and in the cued recall, F (1,296) = 2.41, p  = .122, η p 2  = 0.01. For both free and cued recall, we found that the data were more likely under the model without the warning than with a warning as a factor, with BF exclusion  = 3.28 for free recall, and BF exclusion  = 4.20 for cued recall, constituting moderate evidence.

In contrast to our findings on the effect of warning in free and cued recall, the main effect of warning on misinformation endorsement was significant in the recognition test, F (1,296) = 4.88; p  = .028, η p 2  = 0.02, providing partial support for Hypothesis 3b. We predicted that the warning would be more effective in reducing the misinformation effect in the external misinformation condition, and this was true. The analysis of simple effects revealed that the effect of the warning was significant only in the external misinformation condition, F (1,296) = 7.39, p  = .007, Cohen’s d  = 0.33, 3 but not in the internal misinformation condition, p  = .635.

## Internal intrusions

We reasoned that the effect of the warning might go beyond resistance to misinformation. We therefore expected that the warning might reduce the frequency of reporting internal intrusions in the control condition. However, the warning effect in the control condition was not significant in the free-recall, p  = 0.455, the cued-recall, p  = .784, or the recognition test, p  = .508.

The results of the Bayesian mixed-factor ANOVAs supported this lack of effect in all the memory tests. The data provided strong evidence for excluding the warning factor from the model of internal intrusions in the free-recall test (BF exclusion  = 16.16), the cued-recall test (BF exclusion  = 19.98), and the recognition test (BF exclusion  = 14.21), relative to the model with the warning as a factor. ## Confidence–accuracy relationship

To examine any differences in the confidence–accuracy relationship across manipulated detail types and warning conditions, we examined the curves plotted in Fig.  6 . Consistent with the pattern of correct recall, original information repeated items were associated with higher levels of accuracy compared to other detail types. The confidence–accuracy relationship was overall good across all detail types, although control details and both types of misinformation frequently showed overconfidence, particularly at medium and high levels of confidence (the dashed line represents a perfect confidence – accuracy relationship; participants’ responses fall below this line, indicating they were overconfident relative to their actual accuracy). Notably, in both cued recall and recognition tests, this overconfidence at medium and high levels of confidence was lower for participants who received a warning. It seems that warning participants about the possibility of encountering inaccurate information provided better conditions for participants’ confidence ratings to reflect the likelihood of response accuracy, and this difference was strongest for internally misinformed details reported at a medium level of confidence. Exploratory pairwise proportion tests comparing the proportions of details that were targeted with internal misinformation and were accurately reported at a medium level of confidence across the warning conditions indicated a nonsignificant difference in cued recall, z  = 1.75, p  = .081, and a significant difference in recognition, z  = 2.43, p  = .015. The confidence–accuracy curves for internally and externally misinformed details frequently overlapped (Hypothesis 7).

Importantly, the results reported here were largely consistent with the generalized linear mixed-model analyses presented in the Online Supplementary Material. The main discrepancies concerned Hypothesis 6. This hypothesis predicted negative effects of warnings on correct responses for original-information repeated items, driven by an overcorrection mechanism. Although the preregistered ANOVA analyses did not support these predictions, the mixed-model analyses provided partial support for Hypothesis 6, but only in the recognition task. A second discrepancy involved Hypothesis 5 in the free-recall task. Contrary to what we report here, the Online Supplementary Material analyses showed no effect. However, the findings for cued-recall and recognition tasks were consistent across both analytic approaches.

## Discussion

We tested each hypothesis in three memory tasks: free recall, cued recall, and recognition. As we indicated above, the free-recall data were the least conclusive since many of the items could not be included in the analyses because participants did not report them. Therefore, from the perspective of evaluating the hypotheses, the cued-recall and recognition tests were more informative, with the cued-recall task providing a more ecologically valid measurement.

The present study successfully replicated the standard misinformation effect in the context of repeated events, with misled items yielding significantly fewer correct responses compared to non-misled items across all three memory tests, providing support for H1. Additionally, the repetition of original information enhanced memory accuracy relative to control items in each of the memory tasks (H5 supported). However, contrary to predictions, internal and external misinformation did not differ in their impact on correct responding (H2a not supported). We expected the internal misinformation presentation to result in higher rates of misinformation endorsement than external misinformation (H2b). This was supported only in cued recall; analyses of recognition were inconclusive. Below, we elaborate on why the effect occurred only in the recall task.

One of our main predictions concerned the positive effect of warning on responding to items targeted with misinformation. The only significant warning effect emerged in the recognition test, where warned participants showed reduced misinformation endorsement compared to not-warned participants (H3b supported). Importantly, this warning effect was specific to external misinformation, as predicted in the non-preregistered hypothesis, with no corresponding benefit observed for internal misinformation. The data supported the absence of an effect of warning on correct reporting (H3a not supported). We also predicted that the warning would improve responding to the control items by reducing the reporting of internal intrusions and thus reporting more correct responses, but we found evidence for no effect of warning on reporting internal intrusions (H4a and H4b not supported).

Notably, none of our hypotheses concerning the measure of correct reporting was significant. As elaborated by Rubínová et al. ( 2026 ), using a measure of correct reporting to detect the impact of misinformation manipulation (and consequently also the effectiveness of warnings) might be problematic in repeated-event paradigms, where substantial proportions of inaccurate responses are driven by internal intrusions. Therefore, any potential effects of misinformation emerging as inaccurate reporting are intermixed with inaccurate reporting of internal intrusions. It seems that misinformation endorsement may serve as a more reliable measure of the impact of misinformation and any reversal effects associated with warnings.

Given the scarcity of studies on susceptibility to misinformation in repeated-events scenarios with adult samples, it is important to note that our results showed a clear advantage of non-misled items over misled ones. However, examining the correct responses with consideration of the four types of detail separately revealed that this difference was driven by the original information repeated items.

Previous research (Powell et al., 2007 ; Rubínová et al., 2026b (only in a cued-recall task); Sharman & Danby, 2022 ) showed that whether the misinformation is internal or external is a key factor in regulating suggestibility. Since internal misinformation items were part of the repeated experience, they should be accompanied by stronger subjective evidence than external misinformation items, which were presented solely in the post-event narrative. Additionally, rejecting internal misinformation is much more difficult than rejecting external misinformation, since it requires linking a piece of misinformation with a non-target instance (Rubínová et al., 2026b ). Our results replicated this difference in the cued-recall task. However, the recognition test did not show this difference. Importantly, Rubínová et al. ( 2026b ) obtained a corresponding pattern of results: the greater endorsement of internal than external misinformation was observed in cued recall, but not in recognition, and the reason for the lack of consistency across memory tasks may be in the nature of the recognition test. Specifically, the recognition test offered participants several familiar alternatives, including internal and external misinformation items and internal intrusion items from non-target instances – reporting across all these categories increased from the cued recall to the recognition task (see Table  4 ). On the other hand, greater reporting of internal than external misinformation in the cued-recall task may have been driven by higher familiarity that was unique to internal misinformation items (Rubínová et al., 2026a , 2026b ).

Warnings have proven highly effective in single-event misinformation scenarios (Blank & Launay, 2014 ), which contrasts with the limited effects of warnings observed in the present study. Several factors may account for this discrepancy. First, the inherent difficulty of remembering unique details from a target instance of a repeated event may have overshadowed the potential benefits of the warning. Unlike single-event scenarios in misinformation effect studies, where source monitoring involves distinguishing between one original event and one post-event source, repeated events require participants to discriminate among multiple similar instances of the same event, and the post-event source on top of it, thus creating a substantially more complex task. The potential mechanisms of a warning – encouraging more careful monitoring, enhancing the reality characteristics of accurate event details, and prompting more careful memory search for a correct candidate answer (Blank & Launay, 2014 ; Echterhoff et al., 2005 ; Oeberst & Blank, 2012 ) – might not have been effective when it was too difficult for the participants to successfully attribute details to specific instances. The occurrence of internal intrusions and the warning’s lack of efficacy in reducing them may support this view. This perspective could also be supported by the fact that the positive effect of the warning was only observed in the recognition test – arguably an easier task compared to free- and cued-recall tasks, with recognition usually yielding better accuracy rates than recall (Loftus, 1971 ; Postman et al., 1948 ). In sum, the cases of the lack of effectiveness of warnings may potentially be attributed to the difficulty of source monitoring in the repeated-events context, but further studies are needed to examine how warnings affect accuracy in repeated-event contexts. The warning effect observed only in the recognition task and in the context of external misinformation, where participants could rely on familiarity judgments rather than detailed source attribution, supports this interpretation, suggesting that the warning benefited responding to questions about one instance of repeated events only when the source monitoring demands were reduced.

Another explanation for the limited effect of the warning on memory performance may be related to how people understand the memory task in misinformation situations. Previous research indicates that how people understand the memory task can strongly shape their reports (e.g., Blank, 1998 ; Scoboria et al., 2013 ; Skopicz-Radkiewicz et al., 2023 ). As Oeberst and Blank describe (2012; see also Blank, 1998 , 2009 ; Blank & Launay, 2014 ; Highhouse & Bottrill, 1995 ), encountering misinformation might leave people with a suboptimal understanding of the task: participants were led to believe that the post-event source is an accurate source of information concerning what happened in the original event; however, the more adequate task representation can be restored, for example, with an enlightenment warning. As Blank and Launay ( 2014 ) describe, warnings vary in specificity: how specifically they indicate the incidence of misinformation and its source, and how thoroughly they explain its nature. The warnings that were found to be especially effective in Blank and Launay’s meta-analysis (Blank & Launay, 2014 ) were enlightenments that focused on restoring adequate task representation by explaining to participants that misinformation was present and why it was present. Importantly, ours was a general warning that did not state the presence of misinformation, only indicating the possibility of erroneous information in the post-event narrative. This is a comparatively weak warning, considering that some warnings (e.g., Wright, 1993 ) explicitly state which information was wrongly suggested (which, at the same time, compromises ecological validity). In sum, the potentially smaller effects of the general warning we used, combined with the general difficulty of recalling particular event details and source monitoring in repeated-event situations, might have contributed to the limited effects of the warning that we observed in correct responding and misinformation endorsement measures.

Another potential explanation of the limited effects of the warning needs to be addressed. Namely, could these results be accounted for simply by participants’ inattention to the warning? Although no direct manipulation check of attention and comprehension regarding the warning was used, we believe that participants attended to the warning. Aside from the precautions against missing the warning (described in the Procedure section) and numerous checks of participants’ attention throughout the experiment, the analysis of responses in both target experimental questions and distractors or additional comments suggests overall high participant engagement in the experimental procedure. At the end, when participants were asked about the perceived goal of the study, they gave mostly comprehensive explanations, such as “In my opinion the study was on how attentive we are to information, especially in the context where the distractors are present”, or “How attentional load from other tasks affects how well you remember the events that were the actual point of the task, and how suggestions from another person affect what you remember about the events.” We also specifically asked participants if they were attentive, and every participant who reported inattention even in part of the procedure was excluded. Promoting participants’ engagement was also one of the reasons for recruiting participants who do not routinely participate in studies, like psychology students do, and for conducting the experiment in the laboratory – Rubínová et al. ( 2026b ) found that in-lab participants had better performance in the repeated event memory task than online participants, which can indicate higher motivation and/or better attention to the experimental procedure. Most importantly, however, the acquired pattern of results suggests that the warning was noticed and understood. Namely, the warning affected misinformation reporting in the recognition test and affected the confidence-accuracy relationship. However, applying a manipulation check concerning attention to the warning is recommended in future studies.

An effect of the warning observed in the exploratory analyses of the confidence-accuracy relationship deserves further investigation: Warnings reduced overconfidence, particularly for internally misinformed items reported at medium levels of confidence. This effect was present in the recognition test, and there was a consistent though nonsignificant pattern in cued recall, indicating that warned participants were perhaps more aware that their responses to these items could be erroneous. The influence of the warning instruction on metacognitive judgments can be interpreted as an argument supporting our perspective that warning does indeed help participants understand the questioning task in a more adequate way. Warning might have helped adjust the confidence level to better reflect the difficulty of the task, which demanded source monitoring among several similar events and additionally involved misinformation. The effect of warning on confidence is also potentially informative in the context of discussion on the nature of retrospective confidence judgements. The observation that confidence was affected by retrieval instruction (warning) can be interpreted as supporting the inferential nature of metacognitive judgements: based not only on memory strength, but also on other mechanisms, for example the individual’s considerations and metamemory beliefs (see Schwartz, 2024 , for discussion).

## Constraints on generality

We recruited participants through various channels, including social media, leaflets, and information passed on by participants to other potentially interested participants. Importantly, we did not test psychology students. In doing so, we are likely to have gained a more heterogeneous sample than many previous laboratory experiments that have commonly focused on university students from one faculty. Although most of our sample consisted of very young adults (18–23 years old), participants older than that were also represented in our sample.

The prevailing majority of our participants were either students of various universities or people with higher education. It is difficult to ascertain whether our results would generalize to populations with lower levels of formal education.

Public significance statement.

Our lives largely consist of repeated events: series of events with a shared structure, but varying specific characteristics (e.g., work assignments, classes, grocery shopping). Improving people’s reports of events is important in itself, but can also help understand the processes involved in remembering. Event reports can be distorted with misinformation: information inconsistent with the event (e.g., from other people, the media). Warnings against misinformation can reduce or even reverse this effect, but they have never been studied in a repeated-event context. In this study, we investigated, for the first time, how warnings affect the reporting of a single instance of a repeated event after exposure to misinformation. Warned participants reported less misinformation than unwarned participants, but only in an easier, recognition-style task with response options. The lack of warning efficacy in more difficult recall tasks likely reflects the inherent difficulty of remembering event-specific details and attributing them to the correct instance.
