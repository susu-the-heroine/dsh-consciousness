## Abstract

Brain–computer interfaces promise restored communication and movement, yet accuracy achieved under development rarely survives new sessions, users, tasks, or hardware. Generalization, not peak performance, determines clinical scalability. Here we examine generalizable neural decoding from development to deployment, organized by the domain held out at evaluation, target-data access, adaptation strategy, and the clinical conditions under which performance persists, identifying benchmarking standards, prospective validation, and hardware-algorithm co-design as prerequisites for durable benefit.

## Introduction

Brain-computer interfaces (BCIs) translate neural activity into commands for communication and motor control and can incorporate sensory feedback or therapeutic stimulation within closed-loop systems 1 – 9 . These capabilities have expanded the functional possibilities available to people with severe neurological impairments. However, a system that performs well during development may require substantial recalibration or lose performance when recording conditions, user behavior, or clinical needs change 10 – 13 . The central deployment question is therefore whether useful function can be sustained under specified conditions and with an acceptable burden on users, caregivers, and clinical services. Answering this question requires connecting advances in neural recording and decoding to the circumstances under which their performance was evaluated.

Here, generalizable neural decoding refers to preserving task-relevant performance beyond the conditions used for model development. Cross-session, cross-participant, cross-task, cross-device, cross-site, and cross-modality generalization concern different deployment changes, and evidence along one dimension does not establish transfer along the others. Transferability concerns the reuse of learned structure, whereas adaptation describes changes made using information from the target domain. A fixed decoder evaluated on a held-out participant therefore provides different evidence from a decoder recalibrated using labeled recordings from that participant. Clinically scalable BCIs require a broader set of conditions: sustained functional benefit in the intended population and setting, acceptable risk and cost, and manageable training, recalibration, maintenance, computational, and support requirements 14 – 16 . Generalizable decoding can support these conditions but does not, by itself, establish clinical scalability.

Recent reviews have examined domain adaptation for neural decoding 17 , Edge-AI implementation 15 , clinical translation and outcome measures for implantable BCIs 18 , brain foundation models 19 , and generative AI in BCIs 20 . This narrative review connects these areas through a common deployment-centered framework. For each evidence stream, we examine what changes between model development and use, which domain is held out, what target-domain recordings and labels are available, how adaptation is performed, and which clinical or hardware constraints affect sustained performance. We first consider how recording interfaces shape these deployment changes, then examine decoding approaches and clinical applications, and finally address the biological, computational, and operational barriers to long-term use. Human and animal studies, acute and chronic recordings, decoding and stimulation, and demonstrated and prospective applications are considered separately. The review approach and evaluation criteria are described in the “Methods”.

## Distributed neural recording and representation complexity

The structure of neural data fundamentally constrains the representation space available to BCI decoders. Recording hardware determines not only signal fidelity, but also spatial scale, temporal bandwidth, anatomical coverage, long-term stability, and biological specificity. These factors directly influence the dimensionality, robustness, and generalizability of downstream decoding models. This relationship is particularly important for clinically scalable BCIs, as complex behaviors such as speech, dexterous movement, and cognitive control emerge from distributed population dynamics rather than isolated neural activity. Accordingly, modern neural interfaces have increasingly evolved from sparse, localized recordings toward systems capable of sampling broader and more stable neural representations across distributed brain networks.

## The spatiotemporal-invasiveness trade-off

Neural recording approaches differ in their physical signal source, spatial and temporal resolution, anatomical coverage, and requirements for access to neural tissue (Fig.  1 ). Scalp electroencephalography (EEG) 21 , 22 and magnetoencephalography (MEG) 23 measure electrical and magnetic activity, respectively, whereas functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) 24 and functional ultrasound (fUS) 25 infer neural activity through hemodynamic changes. The invasiveness of fUS depends on the implementation: transcranial imaging has been demonstrated under specific experimental conditions, while the closed-loop macaque BCI discussed here used a surgically created cranial opening 26 . Functional optical microscopy 27 encompasses techniques for measuring activity at cellular or subcellular resolution, but optical access, tissue penetration, and indicator requirements constrain their use. For example, the macaque optical BCI used two-photon calcium imaging through an implanted cranial window 28 . These distinctions are important because imaging resolution, access requirements, and demonstrated decoding capability cannot be inferred from the modality name alone.

Intracranial approaches improve signal specificity by increasing proximity to neural tissue. Endovascular BCIs 29 record neural electrical activity using electrodes positioned within cerebral blood vessels, whereas electrocorticography (ECoG) 30 , 31 and stereoelectroencephalography 32 enable mesoscale and depth-resolved recordings with greater anatomical specificity than scalp-based recordings. At the highest-resolution end of the spectrum, penetrating microelectrode arrays and silicon probes can record spike-related activity from local neuronal populations 33 – 35 . Multi-unit activity (MUA) 36 refers to aggregate spiking activity detected near an extracellular electrode and is a signal feature rather than a recording modality. Its contributing neuronal population depends on electrode geometry, recording conditions, and signal-processing choices. However, increased neural proximity also increases vulnerability to tissue response, micromotion, glial encapsulation, and long-term signal instability. As a result, no existing recording modality simultaneously achieves high spatial resolution, broad anatomical coverage, low invasiveness, and stable long-term performance.

## Evolution toward distributed and bio-integrated neural interfaces

The evolution of neural interfaces reflects a broader transition in BCI research from localized recording toward scalable sampling of distributed neural population dynamics (Fig.  2 ). Early intracortical systems, including Utah arrays and Michigan probes, established the foundation for multisite neural recording and enabled landmark demonstrations of cursor control and motor decoding from restricted cortical regions, particularly the primary motor cortex 33 , 34 , 37 . Although these systems validated the feasibility of neural control, they sampled only a small subset of the distributed neural activity underlying natural behaviors.

As BCIs have expanded to support more complex functions, including fluent speech, dexterous movement, and cognitive-state regulation, the limitations of sparse and localized recording have become increasingly apparent 38 – 40 . These behaviors emerge from coordinated activity across distributed cortical and subcortical networks rather than isolated neuronal responses. Accordingly, modern neural interfaces increasingly aim to capture large-scale population dynamics and latent neural representations across broader anatomical regions 41 , 42 .

This conceptual shift has driven parallel advances in neural-interface engineering. High-density silicon probes such as Neuropixels 43 have substantially increased neural sampling capacity by integrating dense recording sites and on-chip electronics within compact architectures 35 , 43 – 45 . At the same time, the chronic instability of rigid implants revealed a major limitation of conventional interfaces: mechanical mismatch between stiff devices and soft neural tissue can induce inflammation, glial encapsulation, and progressive signal degradation 46 – 49 . To address these challenges, flexible and bio-integrated technologies 50 , including polymer probes 42 , 47 , 51 , mesh electronics 48 , thin-film arrays 52 – 54 , high-density μECoG systems 55 – 57 , and wireless neural interfaces, have been developed to improve conformability, anatomical coverage, and long-term recording stability 40 , 58 , 59 .

## High-dimensional neural data and representation complexity

Modern neural interfaces can record neural activity simultaneously across hundreds to thousands of channels, capturing spatial, spectral, and temporal information for population-level decoding 60 , 61 . These recordings provide access to covariance structure, latent dynamics, and distributed behavioral representations that may be poorly sampled by individual channels 62 – 70 . Their potential value for generalization depends on whether behaviorally relevant structure remains accessible when the measured population or recording conditions change. Increased dimensionality should therefore be distinguished from demonstrated robustness to electrode drift, channel loss, and biological variability.

Larger recordings also increase the demands of preprocessing, inference, and model updating. For implantable systems, these operations must remain compatible with limits on power, memory, telemetry, latency, and heat generation. Evaluation should consequently relate gains in decoding performance to both the recording changes encountered and the computational resources required to maintain useful operation.

Taken together, advances in neural recording have expanded the scale and richness of signals available to BCIs, but no modality simultaneously optimizes resolution, coverage, invasiveness, and long-term stability. Each recording architecture also introduces distinct deployment shifts, ranging from temporal drift and channel loss in chronic intracortical interfaces to context, device, and site variability in non-invasive systems. High-density and preclinical platforms establish the potential of distributed population recording, whereas longitudinal human studies provide the more relevant evidence for sustained clinical use. Thus, increasing recording scale alone cannot ensure generalizable decoding; progress will depend on the joint design of stable interfaces, transferable neural representations and adaptation strategies that remain feasible under clinical and hardware constraints.

## Evolution of neural decoding architectures

As shown in Fig.  3 , the evolution of neural decoding architectures has been fundamentally shaped by the increasing complexity of neural population activity and the growing demand for robust extraction of behaviorally relevant information. Early decoding methods were primarily designed for relatively low-dimensional and locally recorded neural signals, where neural activity could often be approximated through explicit linear mappings. However, as recording technologies expanded toward large-scale distributed population measurements with richer temporal structure and stronger nonlinear interactions 70 , decoding architectures progressively shifted toward learned hierarchical representations capable of modeling complex spatiotemporal neural dynamics.

## From explicit feature engineering to learned representations

Classical decoders include population-vector methods, Wiener filters, and conventional Kalman filters, which represent neural-to-behavior relationships through explicit mappings or linear dynamical models 1 , 3 , 71 – 74 . Discrete tasks have also used linear discriminant analysis and support vector machines 26 , 75 – 77 . These methods supported early online cursor, spelling, and robotic-control systems and remain relevant where their modeling assumptions, calibration requirements, and computational costs suit the task.

Their deployment limitations should be interpreted in relation to the representation and update procedure rather than model age alone. A fixed mapping may lose validity when recorded features or their relationship to behavior change. Conversely, a comparatively simple decoder can remain useful when those relationships are sufficiently stable or when appropriate recalibration is available. Manual feature extraction and spike sorting can introduce additional dependencies on recording conditions, but replacing these stages with learned representations does not remove the need to evaluate transfer.

## Spatial and temporal representation learning in deep neural networks

Deep learning architectures substantially expanded the representational capacity of neural decoders by enabling hierarchical feature extraction from large-scale neural recordings. Rather than relying on predefined signal features or manually engineered preprocessing pipelines, multilayer neural networks learn distributed representations directly from neural activity, allowing decoding systems to model complex nonlinear relationships across spatially and temporally organized neural populations 5 , 6 , 8 , 9 , 78 – 88 .

Deep learning supports decoding from threshold crossings, MUA, local field potentials, and broadband signals, reducing dependence on explicitly isolated single units in some pipelines 89 . However, robustness to recording drift or channel degradation is an empirical property of the trained system and its evaluation conditions. Learning features directly from neural recordings does not, by itself, establish stability across sessions or recording configurations.

Different neural network architectures have subsequently been specialized in modeling distinct forms of neural structure. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) effectively capture local spatial organization within ECoG grids and high-density cortical recordings, making them particularly suitable for distributed cortical activity with spatially organized patterns. Such architectures have enabled auditory attention decoding for neuro-steered hearing aids, substantially improved visual-imagery decoding relative to classical CSP-based pipelines, and supported continuous neural trajectory tracking from non-invasive electrophysiological recordings 82 , 84 , 85 , 90 , 91 . In contrast, recurrent neural networks (RNNs), long short-term memory (LSTM) networks, and gated recurrent units (GRUs) were designed to model sequential temporal dependencies underlying continuous behaviors such as movement execution, handwriting, and speech production 5 , 6 , 83 , 86 , 87 , 92 , 93 . These recurrent architectures substantially improved continuous motor decoding and neural speech synthesis by learning temporally evolving neural representations that extend beyond instantaneous firing statistics. They have supported phoneme decoding and speech reconstruction from intracortical activity, substantially improving the intelligibility and recognizability of synthesized speech, and enabling high-performance large-vocabulary speech decoding that significantly outperformed earlier ECoG-based and non-invasive speech BCIs 6 , 8 , 80 , 87 , 94 .

More recently, transformer architectures and self-attention mechanisms have enabled increasingly large-scale modeling of distributed neural dynamics across extended spatial and temporal contexts. Unlike recurrent architectures that compress information through sequential hidden states, Transformers process neural sequences through adaptive attention mechanisms that capture long-range dependencies across both spatially distributed neural populations and extended temporal windows 95 , 96 . These approaches have proven particularly effective for neural speech decoding, enabling high-intelligibility spectrogram decoding and intermediate phoneme reconstruction, while also supporting the use of surface ECoG for direct speech feature decoding. They further extend to complex behavioral prediction, where behavior emerges from overlapping and context-dependent neural dynamics distributed across multiple brain regions 80 , 81 .

Collectively, the evolution from linear models to deep neural architectures has substantially expanded the complexity of neural dynamics and behavioral outputs that BCIs can decode. However, increased model capacity has produced more consistent gains in within-domain performance than in robustness across sessions, participants, tasks, and recording systems. The scope of generalization depends on the change being tested. Cross-session transfer concerns temporal variation within a participant; cross-participant transfer introduces differences in anatomy, physiology, and neural strategy; cross-task transfer may change the intended output; cross-device transfer alters the measurement configuration; and cross-site transfer may combine differences in populations, protocols, and care settings. These changes can occur together, and success along one dimension does not establish robustness along the others. Results must also be interpreted according to whether the deployed pipeline remained fixed or used target-domain recordings or labels for adaptation.

## Toward transferable neural representations

Despite remarkable improvements in decoding accuracy, most contemporary BCI systems remain fundamentally task-specific and participant-specific. High-performance decoders are typically optimized using narrowly constrained datasets collected from individual users under highly controlled conditions. As a result, decoding performance often deteriorates across recording sessions, behavioral contexts, and subjects due to neural non-stationarity, electrode drift, and substantial biological heterogeneity 97 . These limitations highlight a broader scalability challenge: supervised neural decoders frequently fail to generalize beyond the specific distributions on which they were trained 17 .

These limitations have motivated pretraining across multiple sessions, participants, or behavioral contexts to identify neural structure that can be reused in subsequent decoding problems. Approaches including Neuroformer, the Neural Data Transformer (NDT) family, and POYO explore different forms of shared representation learning 62 , 98 – 101 . Their scope of transfer depends on the pretraining corpus, signal representation, downstream task, and target-specific updating used in evaluation. Broad coverage within one signal family does not establish transfer across recording modalities. Rather than optimizing exclusively for a single decoding objective, these models aim to capture latent statistical structure shared across large and heterogeneous neural datasets. The central hypothesis underlying these approaches is that diverse neural behaviors may emerge from partially shared population-level representational principles that can be learned through large-scale pretraining.

Learning reusable neural representations involves several methodological levels. An architecture specifies how information is processed, a learning objective specifies the structure being optimized, and a pretrained model combines these choices with a particular training corpus and fitted parameters. These levels have different implications for transfer: the same architecture can support multiple objectives, and the same objective can be applied to either a single recording or a large, heterogeneous corpus.

The emergence of such models has been enabled by rapid expansion of neural data infrastructure and cross-institutional data integration efforts. Standardized formats such as Neurodata Without Borders (NWB) 102 , 103 , together with large-scale multi-institutional data aggregation efforts, are beginning to support the integration of heterogeneous datasets spanning ECoG, intracortical recordings, non-invasive neurophysiology, behavior, and multimodal clinical metadata 102 , 103 . Importantly, such large-scale heterogeneity is essential for learning neural representations that generalize across experimental conditions and patient populations. Whether recordings can be pooled meaningfully additionally depends on the compatibility of their tasks, signals, labels, and acquisition and processing histories. Pretraining scale should therefore be described through independent participants, sessions, behavioral coverage, and recording conditions as well as hours, channels, or tokens. Larger datasets provide opportunities for broader reuse, but their diversity and provenance determine what forms of transfer can be evaluated.

Representation-learning methods provide building blocks for reusable models, but their original evaluations address different questions. The original NDT uses masked reconstruction of binned population activity to infer neural dynamics 99 . STNDT adds spatial and temporal attention and combines masked modeling with a contrastive loss 95 . CEBRA instead learns embeddings through contrastive sampling conditioned on behavioral variables, temporal relationships, or both, supporting supervised, self-supervised, and hybrid analyses 104 . These methods establish ways of learning useful neural structure; applying a method to several datasets does not by itself demonstrate transfer of one pretrained model between them.

Pretrained candidates differ in their data and objectives. Neuroformer uses generative modeling of neural events with multimodal covariates; its biological demonstrations include events inferred from mouse calcium imaging and downstream behavioral prediction 98 . NDT2 uses masked pretraining of intracortical spiking activity across motor contexts, followed by target-context adaptation and decoding 100 . The original POYO uses supervised multi-session behavioral decoding with event tokens and learned unit embeddings 62 . These approaches should therefore be compared through their actual supervision and adaptation protocols rather than grouped under self-supervised pretraining.

Subsequent models extend different dimensions of this work. POYO+ accommodates calcium-imaging time series across mouse brain regions, cell types, and tasks 101 . NDT3, the generalist intracortical motor decoder, uses autoregressive pretraining on approximately 2000 h of spiking activity from more than 30 monkeys and humans and was evaluated on eight downstream decoding tasks 105 . This increases the breadth of evidence within defined neural signal domains. Combining neural activity with visual or behavioral covariates, or adapting an architecture to another signal type, does not establish transfer of the same pretrained representation between spikes, ECoG, EEG, and imaging.

Architectural efficiency is a separate consideration from pretraining breadth. Attention-based models enable interactions among neural tokens, whereas selective state-space architectures such as Mamba offer sequence processing whose computational cost scales linearly with sequence length under the specified implementation 106 . Mamba is considered here as a potential computational backbone. Its original architecture paper does not establish a pretrained neural foundation model or transfer across neural recording domains. Any advantage for BCI use must be evaluated in a particular neural implementation, including decoding performance, causal operation, latency, memory requirements, and adaptation cost.

Importantly, foundation-model and generalist approaches mark a shift from isolated task-specific decoders toward reusable neural representations. Pretraining across datasets, sessions, and tasks has shown potential to improve data efficiency and facilitate adaptation within defined signal domains. However, evidence for robust transfer across participants, devices and recording modalities, and for sustained prospective clinical performance, remains limited. The near-term value of these models may therefore lie less in learning a universal neural representation than in providing shared backbones that reduce, rather than eliminate, the need for deployment-specific adaptation.

## Clinical expansion of BCIs

Advances in neural recording, decoding and closed-loop control have expanded BCIs from discrete motor commands to naturalistic communication, sensory restoration and adaptive neuromodulation (Fig.  4 ). However, these applications differ substantially in technological maturity, clinical objectives and evidential support, ranging from prospective online use in humans to acute feasibility studies, preclinical experiments and conceptual applications. Their clinical relevance therefore depends not only on task performance, but also on durability, user burden, safety and meaningful functional outcomes. This section examines these application domains separately while considering the common challenges that determine whether experimental capability can translate into sustained clinical benefit.

## From discrete control to naturalistic motor and speech decoding

Early clinical BCIs primarily focused on restoring basic motor output in individuals with severe paralysis 71 , 107 . Initial proof-of-concept systems decoded low-dimensional movement parameters from localized motor cortical activity to enable two-dimensional cursor control, target selection, and simple robotic manipulation 108 – 124 . Although these demonstrations established the feasibility of direct neural control, the decoded behavioral space remained highly constrained and typically required extensive participant-specific supervised calibration.

Over time, advances in high-density neural recording, population-level neural modeling, and deep representation learning substantially expanded the complexity of decodable behaviors 13 , 123 . Modern intracortical and cortical-surface BCIs now support multi-degree-of-freedom robotic control, dexterous reach-and-grasp behaviors, and coordinated movement trajectories by extracting movement-related information from distributed neural population activity rather than relying on isolated channels or single-neuron features 122 , 123 , 125 . This evolution is exemplified by the progression from early M1-based point-and-click systems using linear filters to ReFIT-KF decoders that achieved >99% target-acquisition success while substantially reducing movement time and improving trajectory efficiency 108 , 117 , 126 . Similar advances have been observed in robotic-arm control, where increasingly sophisticated decoding architectures enabled neural control over 7-10 degrees of freedom and supported functional tasks such as self-feeding and object manipulation 123 , 127 . Motor-restoration systems can couple decoded neural activity to functional electrical stimulation of the user’s muscles, as demonstrated in studies of restored hand and upper-limb function 3 , 71 , 107 . These systems should be distinguished from fUS-based motor-intent decoding 26 , optical BCIs 28 , and endovascular control of digital devices 128 . Although all can support neural control, they differ in the recorded signal, controlled output, intervention, and evidence required for sustained use. Communication BCIs have undergone a parallel transformation 83 – 88 , 90 , 94 . Early systems were dominated by visually guided spelling paradigms based on event-related potentials or motor imagery signals, which imposed substantial limitations on communication speed, cognitive load, and long-term usability. P300 spellers demonstrated reliable but relatively slow communication performance 75 , whereas steady-state visually evoked potential (SSVEP)-based systems improved information transfer rates to approximately 50 characters per minute under controlled visual-attention conditions 76 .

Recent deep learning methods have greatly improved the decoding of temporally structured neural activity, including handwriting, phoneme articulation, and attempted speech production 94 , 120 . These methods include RNNs, sequence-transduction models, and transformer-based architectures, which model long-range spatiotemporal dependencies within distributed neural populations 6 , 87 , 88 , 129 . The increase in communication bandwidth is exemplified by RNN-based handwriting BCIs that achieved approximately 90 characters per minute, approaching practical text-entry speeds 5 . More recent CNN-Transformer and language-model-assisted systems further expanded vocabulary size, improved word-level decoding accuracy, and supported increasingly naturalistic neural text generation and real-time speech synthesis 80 , 81 . Communication outcomes must be interpreted in relation to the neural decoder, external language-model assistance, vocabulary, task constraints, and calibration procedure. Where reported, performance before and after external language-model assistance should be presented separately under matched evaluation conditions. Phoneme and word error rates are different endpoints and cannot be directly subtracted to quantify the contribution of a language model. Table  2 records the available performance measures together with their evaluation conditions and distinguishes missing information from evidence that assistance was not used.

These studies establish increasing functional capability, but their relevance to generalization depends on how that capability is maintained. Initial calibration, recurrent recalibration, and target-session updating impose different requirements on users and support teams. Evidence should therefore distinguish performance achieved after extensive task-specific training from performance maintained with limited intervention across later sessions. High communication speed or successful motor control within an individual does not establish transfer to new participants, devices, or settings.

## Bidirectional neural interfaces and adaptive neuromodulation

Beyond neural decoding alone, an increasing number of BCI systems now operate bidirectionally by delivering information or therapeutic modulation back into the nervous system 130 – 152 . This transition from unidirectional decoding toward closed-loop neural interaction represents a major expansion in the functional scope of neurotechnology.

Sensory neuroprostheses use patterned stimulation of surviving sensory pathways to evoke percepts, with outcomes depending on the anatomical target and the integrity of the remaining neural circuitry. Retinal and cortical visual prostheses therefore represent distinct intervention strategies. Human studies have evaluated the epiretinal Argus II system 149 and the subretinal alpha-IMS implant 150 , while stimulation of visual cortex has elicited recognizable forms in humans 130 , 151 , 153 and nonhuman primates 131 . These findings should also be distinguished from preclinical device-development studies: the subretinal implant-upgrade study by Bhuckory et al. evaluated replacement procedures and prosthetic grating acuity in rats 132 . Somatosensory cortical stimulation provides a complementary example of sensory feedback supporting BCI-mediated robotic control 9 . Auditory-attention decoding, including in-ear EEG recording 133 and EEG-based classification 83 , 84 , addresses a different objective by identifying perceptual attention from recorded activity rather than restoring hearing through stimulation.

At the same time, bidirectional sensorimotor interfaces increasingly incorporate haptic feedback and cortical stimulation to improve embodiment, movement precision, and adaptive motor control during robotic manipulation 9 , 89 , 91 , 123 , 124 . These developments indicate that BCIs are progressively evolving from purely output-oriented communication systems toward integrated sensorimotor interaction platforms.

Closed-loop neuromodulation systems further extend bidirectional neurotechnology by continuously monitoring neural activity and dynamically adjusting therapeutic stimulation in response to pathological brain states 134 – 137 , 140 – 148 , 154 , 155 . In contrast to conventional open-loop stimulation paradigms, adaptive systems leverage neural biomarkers and machine-learning algorithms to identify disease-relevant neural signatures in real time and deliver temporally targeted interventions. Parkinson’s disease provides one of the clearest clinical demonstrations of this transition 134 – 137 , 148 . Conventional deep brain stimulation (DBS) substantially improves motor symptoms, achieving approximately 40% improvement in Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (UPDRS) motor scores 135 , 136 . Adaptive DBS further extends this principle by using biomarkers such as β-band oscillatory activity to trigger or modulate stimulation, achieving UPDRS improvements of up to ~68% while simultaneously reducing total electrical energy delivery 134 , 137 , 139 , 144 , 148 . Similar closed-loop neuromodulation approaches are increasingly being investigated in epilepsy, depression, obsessive-compulsive disorder, chronic pain, and other disorders involving distributed network dysfunction 156 , 157 . Table  3 lists representative bidirectional BCI systems for adaptive neuromodulation and sensory restoration.

In bidirectional systems, generalizability concerns both neural readout and the consequences of stimulation. For sensory neuroprostheses, the relevant question is whether stimulation continues to evoke useful percepts across sessions without extensive remapping or retraining. For adaptive neuromodulation, it is whether the biomarker-symptom relationship and control strategy remain effective across changing clinical states. Calibration therefore includes perceptual mapping, biomarker selection, and stimulation programming, not only decoder fitting. Sustained benefit with individualized settings supports longitudinal clinical utility but does not establish transfer across patients. Long-term evaluation should accordingly relate functional outcomes to reprogramming frequency, monitoring requirements, and adverse events.

## Toward integrated neuro-digital ecosystems

As neural interfaces become increasingly adaptive, multimodal, and interconnected, BCIs are beginning to function as integrated neuro-digital platforms rather than isolated assistive devices 9 , 122 , 128 . Contemporary systems already enable users to control graphical interfaces, communication software, robotic systems, and smart environments through continuous neural interaction 8 , 9 . Long-term decoding frameworks are gradually reducing calibration demands and improving real-world usability. Examples include latent-factor stabilization methods, recurrent adaptive decoders, and unsupervised recalibration strategies 78 , 112 , 118 .

At the same time, neural interfaces are increasingly integrated with distributed sensing systems, wearable electronics, cloud computation, robotics, and edge-AI architectures to form unified closed-loop ecosystems 128 , 133 , 152 . This transition has already been illustrated by intracortical BCI systems that enabled individuals with tetraplegia to operate commercial tablet computers for emailing, web browsing, music playback, and digital communication in ordinary consumer environments 4 . Similarly, fully implanted endovascular BCI systems enabled individuals with severe paralysis to control digital devices using thought-driven switching paradigms without requiring open-brain surgery 128 . Such systems suggest a potential pathway toward scalable long-term neural access with reduced surgical burden.

These developments may ultimately support increasingly seamless interactions among neural activity, digital environments, assistive technologies, rehabilitation systems, and intelligent computational infrastructures 3 , 9 , 91 , 128 , 133 , 152 , 158 , 159 . However, the expansion of clinical functionality also amplifies the central challenge facing BCIs: maintaining stable and generalizable performance under highly variable real-world conditions 10 , 13 , 78 , 79 , 113 , 118 . As applications transition from constrained experimental tasks toward long-term autonomous deployment, neural decoders must continuously adapt to changing neural dynamics, behavioral variability, environmental context, and multimodal interactions 10 , 13 , 78 , 79 , 118 .

Assessing these systems requires separating the time a device has been implanted from the period over which useful decoding has been demonstrated. Implant duration describes device exposure, the recording interval describes when signals were collected, and stable-operation duration describes the interval over which a stated level of functional performance was maintained. Longitudinal evaluation should relate these intervals to initial and recurrent calibration, interruptions, degradation, and recovery. Prospective online use further reveals requirements for caregiver assistance, technical support, and maintenance that are not captured by retrospective decoding accuracy. These observations connect task performance to the practical conditions of sustained clinical use.

Across these applications, generalizability is clinically relevant insofar as it reduces the intervention needed to sustain useful function. Lower calibration burden, cross-session stability, and prospective use outside the laboratory provide complementary evidence, but none alone establishes transfer to new participants or settings. These distinctions connect the reported functional advances to the biological, computational, and operational barriers examined in the following section.

## Bottlenecks limiting clinical generalization

Despite major advances in neural recording and representation learning, the clinical deployment of BCIs remains constrained by a central unresolved challenge: achieving stable and generalizable neural decoding under long-term real-world conditions 8 , 80 , 160 . Unlike laboratory demonstrations conducted in controlled environments, as shown in Fig.  5 , clinically viable BCIs must maintain reliable performance across evolving biological states, heterogeneous users, variable behavioral contexts, and resource-constrained implantable hardware 161 . These limitations arise not from a single bottleneck, but from the interaction among unstable neural representations, fragmented data ecosystems, adaptive learning requirements, and stringent engineering constraints.

## Instability of neural representations

A fundamental barrier to long-term BCI performance is the instability of neural signals and their relationship to intended behavior 160 . After implantation, neural tissue undergoes a range of biological responses, including microglial activation, astrocytic encapsulation, vascular remodeling, and neuronal displacement near the recording interface. Mechanical micromotion between the device and surrounding tissue further alters the recorded signal over time. Together, these processes can affect signal amplitude, electrode impedance, unit identity, spectral content, and feature-behavior mappings, ultimately contributing to progressive decoder drift 12 .

Learning, fatigue, changes in behavioral strategy, rehabilitation, and disease progression can also alter the relationship between recorded activity and intended behavior 16 , 162 . Statistically, a change in the input distribution, p(x) , differs from a change in the conditional relationship, p(y | x) , where x denotes recorded neural features and y the decoding target. These categories do not map one-to-one onto biological causes. Electrode drift can change p(y | x) in the observed feature space, and fatigue can affect both activity statistics and their relationship to behavior. The distinction is useful for specifying model assumptions, while the mechanism must be interpreted in the context of the recording and experimental design.

When a behaviorally relevant latent mapping is preserved, alignment or a limited input adapter may be sufficient. If that mapping changes, matching input distributions alone cannot establish the validity of the original decoder; new behavioral supervision, state-dependent models, output adaptation, or coordinated user-decoder learning may be required. Evaluation should therefore distinguish recovery from measurement changes from learning a changed neural-to-behavior relationship, and report performance before and after the selected intervention. Similar losses in accuracy can arise from different mechanisms and need not respond to the same update.

## Data fragmentation and limited transferability

Neural datasets differ in recording modality, anatomical coverage, channel layout, sampling, preprocessing, behavioral protocol, and annotation quality. These differences affect both the feasibility of pooled training and the interpretation of transfer. Dataset scale should therefore be described through independent participants, sessions, behavioral coverage, and recording conditions, as well as hours, channels, or tokens.

NWB supports structured storage of recordings and metadata 102 , 103 , but a common format does not ensure compatible task semantics, labels, preprocessing, or electrode localization. Meaningful pooling additionally requires documented acquisition and processing histories, sufficiently complete metadata, and appropriate permissions for secondary use. Clinical datasets also need outcomes that can be related to the intended deployment setting. These requirements determine whether recordings can support a shared learning objective rather than merely be stored together.

Participant and session provenance is particularly important when datasets are released in overlapping forms. Evaluation should establish whether target participants or recordings were excluded from pretraining and model selection and should distinguish independent evidence from repeated use of the same underlying data. A practical objective for shared models is to reduce the recordings, labels, time, and effort required for a new participant or session while preserving useful prospective performance.

## Continual adaptation under non-stationary conditions

The need for adaptation depends on the magnitude and consequence of a deployment shift. A fixed decoder may remain useful over a defined interval, whereas sustained degradation may motivate periodic or event-triggered updating. When updates are introduced, the stability-plasticity problem becomes central: the model must accommodate new information while preserving mappings that remain useful 163 . Update frequency should therefore reflect evidence of changing performance and the availability of reliable supervision.

Supervised recalibration uses labeled target examples to update the neural-to-output mapping. Unsupervised or self-supervised adaptation uses target recordings, distributional structure, or proxy objectives without externally supplied task labels; pseudo-labeling and inferred intentions additionally depend on the reliability of their generated targets 78 . Continual learning describes updating over an ordered sequence of experiences and may use any of these supervision regimes. Replay, regularization, and modular updates address retention during this process, whereas latent alignment primarily addresses correspondence between representations. Each method therefore has both an update mechanism and a target-information requirement.

User learning introduces a further source of change. Users can improve control through practice or altered strategies even when decoder parameters remain fixed. User-decoder co-adaptation occurs when changes in user behavior and decoder updating interact over time 16 . Improvements during closed-loop use should therefore be interpreted in relation to both processes, using fixed-decoder periods or appropriate comparison conditions where available. For algorithmic updates, the supervision source, data available at each time point, update frequency, calibration burden, and subsequent performance should be specified. Retrospective analyses using later data should be distinguished from updates that could have been executed during prospective use.

## Constraints imposed by implantable systems

Even when robust decoding algorithms are available, their translation into implantable systems is constrained by hardware limitations. Fully implantable BCIs must operate under strict limits on power consumption, telemetry bandwidth, memory capacity, latency, and thermal dissipation 35 , 37 , 43 , 164 . Excess heat can damage neural tissue or disrupt neural function, making energy efficiency a core safety requirement rather than merely an engineering preference.

These constraints create a mismatch between the computational demands of modern neural decoders and the physical limits of implantable hardware. High-capacity models capable of capturing distributed neural dynamics often require substantial memory, computation, and data transfer, which are difficult to support in chronically implanted systems operating within milliwatt-scale power budgets 165 . This challenge becomes increasingly severe as recording systems scale toward hundreds or thousands of channels.

Hardware-algorithm co-design is therefore essential for clinically scalable BCI decoding. Strategies such as model pruning, quantization, knowledge distillation, low-rank adaptation, sparse computation, and event-driven processing can reduce computational cost while preserving decoding performance 15 . Neuromorphic processors and spiking neural networks are also attractive because they exploit sparse and asynchronous computation, potentially enabling low-power real-time decoding. Nevertheless, balancing model capacity, adaptation, latency, energy efficiency, and thermal safety remains a major unresolved challenge for autonomous implantable BCIs.

## Toward stable closed-loop clinical ecosystems

The clinical success of BCIs depends on whether advances in decoding can be translated into sustained functional benefit in everyday life. Stable representations and efficient embedded computation support this transition, but their practical value also depends on the training and recalibration required of users, the support provided by caregivers, and the risk and cost of maintaining the interface. For implanted systems, this extends to long-term adverse-event surveillance and access to revision or explantation when necessary. Continual adaptation introduces a further tension: the flexibility needed to accommodate changing neural signals can also destabilize control when updates rely on uncertain estimates of user intent. During home use, reliable supervision may be limited to occasional prompted calibration, user-confirmed selections, or verifiable task outcomes, leaving much of the incoming neural activity without independent labels. Decoder confidence alone therefore cannot establish that an update preserves useful control; changes in signal quality and functional performance, together with user or caregiver feedback where available, provide complementary evidence for detecting harmful drift. A clinically viable adaptive system consequently needs a validated update protocol that defines when adaptation can proceed, when reassessment is required, and how control can return to a validated fallback decoder or safe operating mode, with versioned records supporting audit and rollback. The sustainability of this model also depends on cybersecurity, protection of neural data, clear arrangements for model ownership and maintenance responsibility, and validation across demographic and clinical groups. Clinical scalability thus emerges from the interaction between adaptive decoding, the wider control architecture, and the infrastructure supporting long-term use, with prospective home-use evaluation needed to determine whether these elements deliver meaningful benefit at an acceptable burden.

Accordingly, shared-control architectures may improve reliability by distributing control between the user and external intelligent systems 166 . In this framework, the neural decoder may infer high-level intent, while robotic or environmental controllers manage low-level motor execution, obstacle avoidance, and task-specific adaptation. Bidirectional interfaces can further enhance control by restoring sensory feedback through cortical, peripheral, or somatosensory stimulation, thereby supporting embodiment and more stable interaction with external devices 167 . However, increasing system complexity also increases vulnerability. As BCIs move from short laboratory tasks to continuous real-world deployment, failures in representation stability, adaptation, latency, hardware reliability, or feedback control can propagate through the entire closed-loop system. Clinically scalable BCIs may therefore depend less on maximizing peak decoding accuracy than on building robust, adaptive, energy-efficient neural ecosystems that can operate reliably over years rather than hours 118 .

## Conclusion

Advances in neural recording, representation learning, and closed-loop control have expanded the functional capabilities of BCIs, from cursor and robotic control to speech communication, sensory feedback, and adaptive neuromodulation. Their relevance to clinical deployment, however, depends on more than the complexity of the decoded behavior or the accuracy achieved under development conditions. Recording interfaces determine the signals available and the changes encountered over time, while decoding and adaptation methods determine how those changes are accommodated. Across these systems, claims of generalization must specify the held-out domain, access to target-domain recordings and labels, calibration requirements, and evaluation setting. Shared pretrained representations and limited deployment-specific adaptation offer potential routes to reducing calibration burden, but their value requires prospective validation. Sustained benefit within an individual also remains distinct from transfer across participants, devices, or clinical settings.

Three practical priorities emerge from this review. First, standardized benchmarking should disclose overlap with pretraining data and compare fixed and adapted decoders under explicit target-data and label budgets, reporting performance degradation, recovery, and calibration time alongside task outcomes. Second, longitudinal validation should evaluate prospective online use in the intended population and setting, relating functional benefit to recurrent recalibration, interruptions, adverse events, and caregiver or technical support. Implant duration should be distinguished from demonstrated stable operation, and adaptive systems should be evaluated with defined update procedures, drift monitoring, and validated fallback options. Third, hardware-algorithm co-design should assess both inference and model updating against the power, memory, latency, telemetry, and thermal constraints of the intended system. Together, these priorities connect algorithmic transfer to the practical conditions of sustained use. Clinically scalable BCIs will require coordinated progress in neural interfaces, decoding methods, and the clinical infrastructure that supports reliable operation at an acceptable risk, cost, and burden.

Looking ahead, the central challenge is to translate advances across neural interfaces, learning algorithms, and clinical practice into dependable systems for everyday use. Progress will be reflected in how reliably BCIs sustain meaningful function, how readily they accommodate changing user needs, and how little intervention they require. Meeting these goals could extend the benefits demonstrated in individual research participants to broader clinical populations and support greater independence beyond specialist laboratories.
