## Abstract

Readers are often confronted with one-sided, conflicting, or even false information when engaging with socioscientific issues. Based on the notion that validation of information is a routine part of comprehension, we conducted four experiments to investigate how explicit validity judgments and nonstrategic validation of (mis)information on socioscientific issues are affected by the interaction between belief consistency and factual accuracy. Experiment 1 ( n = 73) and Experiment 2 ( n = 50) used a validity judgment task and found that participants made more errors in judging false belief-consistent claims and needed more time to correctly respond to true but belief-inconsistent and to false but belief-consistent claims. Experiment 3 ( n = 84) used the epistemic Stroop task to investigate whether readers’ beliefs and knowledge also influence nonstrategic validation, even when readers’ task does not require an explicit judgment of validity or belief consistency. In the epistemic Stroop task, affirmative responses did not differ significantly between true and false belief-consistent claims, whereas they were longer for false belief-inconsistent claims. Experiment 4 ( n = 226) replicated the results for both tasks, controlling for readers’ prior knowledge. The results suggest that beliefs may bias explicit validity judgments, but also the nonstrategic processing of socioscientific claims.

## Introduction

Most questions on socioscientific issues, that is, scientific debates that are relevant to everyday decisions and thus particularly important to the public (Sadler, 2011 ), have no simple answers (e.g., whether a vegan diet is good for children), partly because of the rapid evolution and fragility of scientific knowledge. Moreover, one-sided arguments are often used to deliberately influence political or public opinion on such issues, requiring that readers form a view on socioscientific issues based on competing and conflicting claims or arguments. In addition, misinformation can spread easily, for example on social media, which lacks gatekeeping mechanisms (Ecker et al., 2022 ; Lewandosky et al., 2012 ). In general, misinformation is defined as any information that is found to be false (Ecker et al., 2022 ) and misinformation on socioscientific issues can be particularly harmful because false or inaccurate information on such issues may hamper rational decision making and problem solving (e.g., Rapp & Salovich, 2018 ).

Previous research has identified important factors that influence the detection of misinformation (for an overview, see Eisemann & Pimmer, 2020 ), the acceptance of (political) misinformation (e.g., Hameleers & van der Meer, 2020 ; for an overview, see Ecker et al., 2022 ), or the spread of misinformation on social media (for an overview, see Chen et al., 2023 ). In this article, we take a complementary approach by focusing on the influence of readers’ beliefs and knowledge on the strategic and nonstrategic processing of (mis)information on socioscientific issues. Comprehension is viewed to depend on the three largely passive cognitive processes of activation, integration, and validation (O’Brien & Cook, 2016 ; Richter & Singer, 2017 ). Based on this view, we consider nonstrategic processing to be involved in basic comprehension as part of regular reading processes. These processes require little cognitive effort, and do not depend on readers’ strategies or goals. In contrast, strategic processes are mostly intentional and voluntary, and require cognitive resources and self-regulation processes (Schneider & Shiffrin, 1977 ). Beliefs and knowledge can exert their influence through both nonstrategic and strategic processing. Following Wolfe and Griffin ( 2017 ), we define beliefs as “a position about the truth value of a proposition” (p. 3) that is formed relatively quickly. As subjective cognitions, beliefs need not be accompanied by well-founded prior knowledge (Wiley, 2005 ) or be justified or objectifiable. Prior knowledge, in contrast, refers to representations of episodic or generic facts in long-term memory that, in principle, are objectively true and justified (Richter & Tiffin-Richards, 2024 ). The knowledge that is in the focus of the present research is generic knowledge, which may also be called semantic content knowledge (McCarthy & McNamara, 2021 ).

Readers’ beliefs can have a pervasive influence on information search (e.g., Nickerson, 1998 ), processing (e.g., Richter & Maier, 2017 ; Schwarz et al., 2016 ), and evaluation (e.g., Edwards & Smith, 1996 ). We propose that beliefs not only play a role in motivated reasoning (such as deliberately disconfirming belief-inconsistent information) as part of strategic processing, but also affect the processing, understanding, and acceptance of (mis)information through nonstrategic validation. Validation in this context refers to readers evaluating the consistency of incoming information with prior knowledge and beliefs as part of regular comprehension processes, that is, independent of their goals (O’Brien & Cook, 2016 ; Richter & Maier, 2017 ; Singer, 2013 ). If readers rely on prior beliefs instead of prior knowledge for validation, this might bias the comprehension of controversial information on socioscientific issues and hamper the critical evaluation of scientific content (Britt et al., 2014 ).

Against this background, we examined two research questions in four experiments. First, we were interested in whether and how readers’ strategic evaluation of (mis)information on socioscientific issues is influenced by their beliefs and knowledge. Second, we investigated whether readers’ beliefs and knowledge also influence nonstrategic validation, even when readers’ tasks do not require an explicit judgment of validity or belief consistency. To address these research questions, we first review previous research on the evaluation of and reliance on misinformation before discussing the concept of validation and the role of prior beliefs.

## Detection and evaluation of misinformation

Readers who wish to learn more about socioscientific issues frequently become exposed to misinformation, which implies that they must evaluate the factual accuracy of the information to acquire an adequate understanding of the issue. However, when judging information, readers often seem to rely on their intuitions instead of deliberating consciously on the factual accuracy of the information. Brashier and Marsh ( 2020 ) suggested that such an intuitive judgment of truth may be based on base rates (i.e., the general probability of judging that information is true or false), feelings (i.e., ease and fluency, Schwarz et al., 2016 ; see also the illusory truth effect, Dechêne et al., 2010 ), and the consistency of information with salient information in memory (e.g., Schroeder et al., 2008 ; Unkelbach & Rom, 2017 ). All three of these sources can be useful for strategically evaluating the factual accuracy of information. However, they can also lead to misconceptions about true or false information, for example, if readers read incorrect text information that matches one or several of the sources (e.g., Rapp, 2008 ). For example, Fazio and colleagues ( 2013 ) asked participants simple world knowledge questions such as “What is the largest ocean in the world?” In a subsequent task, conducted 2 weeks later, participants read stories that contained some plausible inaccuracies in these world knowledge facts before answering the simple world knowledge questions again. The results revealed that participants considered the inaccurate information to be true after having read it in the stories, even if they had known the correct answer in the initial assessment of general knowledge. This result suggests that misinformation embedded in stories can lead to false evaluation judgments, that is, considering false information as true even when readers already have the correct knowledge.

Another source of biased truth judgments are individual beliefs. Zmigrod et al. ( 2023 ) proposed that incoming information is compared in a reliability-weighting process with prior beliefs as part of the reader’s model of the world. Information consistent with readers’ beliefs might thus lead to a higher intuitive truth judgment for this information, which increases readers’ reliance on it. The effect of beliefs on the evaluation of arguments has been demonstrated, for example, by research from Kobayashi ( 2010 ). Kobayashi presented university undergraduates with arguments for or against the introduction of daylight-saving time in Japan. Participants evaluated the convincingness of belief-consistent arguments considerably higher than that of belief-inconsistent arguments (for similar results, see McCrudden & Barnes, 2016 ). One explanation for the effects of beliefs on such evaluations might be a greater perceived plausibility of this type of information. For example, Schroeder et al. ( 2008 ) found a bi-directional relationship between plausibility evaluations and the mental model of an expository text. Information that was part of the mental model was perceived as more plausible (regardless of objective plausibility), and information perceived as plausible became more often part of the mental model. Moreover, readers seem to rely on false information when they judge this information as plausible but not so much when they judge it as implausible (e.g., Hinze et al., 2014 ). In sum, the consistency of new information with prior beliefs can influence how the validity of new information is evaluated. However, an empirical examination of how belief consistency and factual accuracy interact in the explicit evaluation of claims about socioscientific controversies is still pending.

## Prior beliefs and validation

Prior beliefs affect not only the strategic evaluation of information, but they also impact more basic aspects of information processing such as the comprehension of information. A long tradition of research has investigated the role of prior beliefs in motivated reasoning. This research has found, for example, that readers primarily seek belief-consistent information (confirmation bias, Nickerson, 1998 ), whereas, under certain circumstances, they also scrutinize belief-inconsistent information (Edwards & Smith, 1996 ). However, research on validation has revealed effects of beliefs at earlier stages of processing (e.g., Richter & Maier, 2017 ). Validation designates a general mechanism during information processing in which the consistency and plausibility of incoming information is evaluated based on readers’ current understanding of the issue, their accessible world knowledge, and their prior beliefs without explicit intention and control (O’Brien & Cook, 2016 ; Richter, 2015 ; Singer, 2013 ). Hence, validation occurs immediately during comprehension as part of nonstrategic processing. The Resonance-Integration-Validation Model (RI-Val Model; O’Brien & Cook, 2016 ) proposes that, together with resonance and integration, validation is important for constructing a mental representation of the content of a text (situation model, Kintsch, 1988 ). The RI-Val model posits that a resonance-like process is initiated by the currently read information, which can trigger the reactivation of long-term memory content if there is sufficient overlap with the incoming text (e.g., Albrecht & O’Brien, 1993 ; Beker et al., 2016 ). In other words, specific information from long-term memory is reactivated and retrieved only if it resonates strongly enough during this reactivation phase. Subsequently, new information is integrated with the activated memory content based on conceptual overlap. As the third component of the triadic process, readers then assess the consistency between the new information and the reactivated memory content through validation, a process that occurs automatically, without deliberate intention or control (O’Brien & Cook, 2016 ; Richter, 2015 ). It is important to note that the RI-Val model explicitly assumes that the three nonstrategic processes of reactivation, integration, and validation for a specific piece of information may continue even if readers have already moved on in the text. Different lines of research support the triad of resonance, integration, and validation, and thus the idea that validation is an integral and routine part of text comprehension (e.g., Ferretti et al., 2008 ; Richter et al., 2009 ; Schroeder et al., 2008 ; for an overview, see Isberner & Richter, 2014 ).

The concept of validation suggests that readers are sensitive to information that is inconsistent with re-activated memory content such as knowledge, beliefs, or prior text information (e.g., Albrecht & O’ Brien, 1993 ; Cook & Myers, 2004 ). Such sensitivity becomes empirically visible as cognitive disruptions during reading such as longer fixation times for implausible information (e.g., Cook & Myers, 2004 ) or increased first fixations and gaze durations for such information (Matsuki et al., 2011 ; for an overview, see Isberner & Richter, 2014 ). In the case of belief-relevant true and false information, readers might be sensitive to false or to belief-inconsistent information. An experiment by van Moort and colleagues ( 2021 ) suggests that information processing depends on the type of information primarily used during validation. The authors used short texts that included knowledge-based contradictions (i.e., historically correct vs. incorrect target sentences) as well as text-based inconsistencies (i.e., congruent vs. incongruent context before the target sentence) and investigated reading times of sentences as indicators of immediate disruptions during reading (for similar approaches, see, e.g., Albrecht & O’ Brien, 1993 ). The results revealed that differences in processing depended on the type of inconsistency. Knowledge-based contradictions led to stronger and longer-lasting disruptions in immediate reading, whereas text-based inconsistencies influenced immediate reading only when the information was false, that is, when it constituted a knowledge-based contradiction. Hence, the effect of text-based inconsistencies during processing depends on the presence of knowledge-based inconsistencies.

In addition, readers are not fully able to control the impact of validation on processing and comprehension. To investigate the nonstrategic character of validation in the case of prior knowledge violations, Richter et al. ( 2009 ) introduced the epistemic Stroop task. In their version of the epistemic Stroop task, participants read simple statements word by word that were either true or false based on easily accessible world knowledge, and indicated with a binary judgment (i.e., yes vs. no) whether the last presented word of the statement was correctly written. Participants’ responses in the orthographic judgment task were longer when participants were required to make a true orthographic judgment for false statements. Similar effects have also been observed for belief-inconsistent information (Gilead et al., 2019 ). In their study, participants were asked to make grammaticality judgments (yes vs. no) for grammatically correct or incorrect statements, such as “The Internet has made people more isolated” versus “The Internet has made people more sociable.” These statements were designed to be either consistent or inconsistent with participants’ prior beliefs. To assess belief consistency, participants rated their agreement with each statement on a 4-point scale. Statements were classified as belief-consistent if participants selected “strongly agree” or “agree,” and as belief-inconsistent if participants selected “disagree” or “strongly disagree.” Results showed that participants responded more quickly with affirmative “yes” judgments to belief-consistent statements than to belief-inconsistent ones. The authors concluded that “opinion-confirmation processes can occur involuntarily” (p. 393).

The Two-Step Model of Validation (Richter & Maier, 2017 ) provides a theoretical basis for understanding how and when beliefs can distort the comprehension of information. Step 1 of the model proposes that belief-consistent information is more likely to be judged as plausible when validation feeds into nonstrategic plausibility checks primarily based on prior beliefs. These plausibility assessments are used as a basis for the allocation of cognitive resources for further processing of information. Information judged as plausible receives more cognitive resources and is more likely to be included in the mental representation of the text’s content, whereas information judged as implausible receives less cognitive resources and is less likely to be included in the mental representation of the text’s content. Several experiments support the assumption of this close relationship between plausibility judgments and mental model construction without readers’ explicit intention or control (e.g., Abendroth & Richter, 2021a ; Maier & Richter, 2013 ; Schroeder et al., 2008 ). Step 2 of the model proposes that readers can also engage in strategic processing, including the effortful elaboration of information, when they are willing to invest the necessary resources and time. Such a strategic processing should be beneficial for comprehension.

## Rationale and overview

Explicit judgments of the truth or plausibility of information depend on the consistency of this information with a reader’s knowledge and beliefs (Brashier & Marsh, 2020 ; Schroeder et al., 2008 ; Zmigrod et al., 2023 ). Beliefs and knowledge can also impact the comprehension of information on socioscientific issues through nonstrategic validation. Validation is routinely carried out during comprehension and provides monitoring of the plausibility of currently read information without explicit intention or control. These processes are based on all kinds of information stored in long-term memory, which is passively reactivated during reading, including prior knowledge (e.g., detecting information inconsistent with easily accessible world knowledge) and prior beliefs (e.g., detecting information that is inconsistent with readers’ prior beliefs). From the view advocated here, we assume that prior beliefs often serve as the basis for nonstrategic validation for socioscientific issues (Richter & Maier, 2017 ). Hence, it seems a plausible conjecture that belief consistency interacts with and may even trump factual accuracy when belief consistency and factual accuracy of information are dissociated. The goal of the present research was to test this assumption both for explicit validity judgments of statements on socioscientific controversies and for an implicit task that captures the influence of nonstrategic validation processes. In Experiments 1 and 2 , we used a validity judgment task to investigate explicit validity judgments for belief-relevant true or false claims on socioscientific issues (cf. Salovich & Rapp, 2021 ). Preregistered Experiment 3 investigated the influence of prior beliefs and the factual accuracy of the claims when readers are given the epistemic Stroop task (Richter et al., 2009 ) that does not require epistemic or semantic processing. Hence, this task is suitable for investigating the role of prior beliefs and factual accuracy in nonstrategic validation. Preregistered Experiment 4 replicated the previous experiments and added a measure of prior knowledge for each experimental item, allowing for a more stringent test of the assumption that biasing effects of beliefs can occur even when accurate and relevant prior knowledge is available.

## Experiment 1

Experiment 1 examined the effects of beliefs on readers’ explicit validity judgments. Participants read and evaluated 56 claims on 14 socioscientific issues in a validity judgment task. The argumentative stance (pro vs. con) and factual accuracy (true vs. false) of the claims were experimentally varied (see Table 1 for an example), and participants’ prior beliefs on the socioscientific controversies were assessed with belief scales. Error rates and response latencies for correct responses in the validity judgment task were collected as dependent variables.

Misinformation is often communicated via claims that are partly true or difficult to discredit (Bak-Coleman et al., 2022 ). Consequently, providing explicit validity judgments for such information might be difficult. Based on this reasoning, we expected a main effect of factual accuracy, that is, participants will make more errors in evaluating the validity of false claims compared to true claims (Hypothesis 1.1). In addition, we expected an interaction of belief consistency and factual accuracy of the experimental items on error rates (Hypothesis 1.2): More errors should be made for false belief-consistent claims compared to true belief-consistent claims. To be precise, errors in this regard pertain to “no” responses to true belief-consistent claims and “yes” responses to false belief-consistent claims. We expected more errors for false belief-consistent claims because we assume that readers tend to evaluate belief-consistent information as being true, regardless of its factual accuracy (e.g., Kobayashi, 2010 ; Richter & Maier, 2017 ). In contrast, no such effect should occur for belief-inconsistent claims. This is because for these claims, nonstrategic validation should lead to a detection of inconsistency. Given the structure and focus of the validity judgment task, providing the validity judgment is likely to include both nonstrategic and strategic processing. Therefore, we assume that the explicit task of judging the validity for belief-inconsistent information can lead to more strategic processing once inconsistencies are detected, which are often based on considering factual accuracy (e.g., the disconfirmation hypothesis, Edwards & Smith, 1996 ).

Theory and research on validation during comprehension suggest that readers are sensitive to false or belief-inconsistent information (Isberner & Richter, 2014 ). From the theoretical perspective advocated here, the belief consistency of information should be the main source for validation (Richter & Maier, 2017 ). If, however, readers successfully recognize false belief-consistent information or true belief-inconsistent information in their explicit validity judgments, this process is expected to require more time as both sources of validity judgments, beliefs and knowledge, suggest incompatible responses (a kind of inconsistency effect, e.g., Cook & Myers, 2004 ; Cook & O’Brien, 2023 ). For instance, to correctly judge the factual accuracy of the experimental true claim “As an officially approved form of therapy, psychoanalytic therapy is considered scientific and effective,” participants must possess specific contextual knowledge. In Germany, where the data for Experiment 1 were collected, psychoanalytic therapy is officially approved, meaning it is recognized by the state as scientifically valid and is covered by public health insurance. This status is currently held by only four types of psychotherapy. Nevertheless, this true claim can be either belief-consistent or belief-inconsistent, depending on a participant’s position on the belief scale for this socioscientific issue. For example, participants who agree with the statement “The foundations of psychoanalysis are unscientific” would experience a conflict between the factual accuracy of the statement and their prior beliefs. Based on the theoretical considerations outlined above, this should lead to a type of inconsistency effect in the validity judgment task for these participants, that is, to longer response times when providing the correct “yes” response (which, based on the belief-inconsistency of the item, would have been a “no” response). Hence, we expect an interaction of belief consistency and factual accuracy for the response latencies of correct responses in the validity judgment task (Hypothesis 1.3): Response latencies for correct responses to true claims should be faster for belief-consistent claims compared to belief-inconsistent claims. In contrast, response latencies for correct responses to false claims should be faster for belief-inconsistent claims compared to belief-consistent claims.

## Method

## Participants

We determined that a sample size of 76 participants would be necessary to detect a small effect size of f =.15 (based on Abendroth et al. ( 2022 ), given α =.05, power (1 – β) =.90 and assuming a factor level intercorrelation of ρ =.5 using the core experimental design of the study specifying an ANOVA-repeated measures-Design (computed with G*Power; Faul et al., 2007 ). To compensate for missing data due to potential exclusions, we aimed at a sample size of 80 participants. The total sample for Experiment 1 was n = 88 university students. The data of 15 participants were excluded because of multiple or incomplete participation or because participants failed to respond correctly to control questions (e.g., “This is a control item. Please choose response category 3”).

The remaining sample consisted of 73 university students (67 women, six men) in their first to sixth semester ( M = 2.55, SD = 1.08). On average, participants were 20 years old ( M = 20.48 SD = 2.90). The highest level of education of 71 participants was a high school diploma (German Abitur, i.e., a higher-education entrance qualification) and two participants had a university of applied sciences degree or university degree as their highest level of education. Seventy-one participants were native speakers of German. Sixty-four participants were right-handed, seven left-handed, and two were ambidextrous. Despite excluding the data of 15 participants, the effective sample size was still sufficient to detect a small effect size of f =.16 (given α =.05, power (1 – β) =.90, and assuming a factor-level intercorrelation of.5, computed with G*Power; Faul et al., 2007 ).

## Material

All text materials (translated into English) of all experiments are available in the repository of the Open Science Framework (osf.io/46ug2).

## Belief assessment

To assess participants’ prior beliefs, participants indicated for each socioscientific issue the position they tend to agree with on a rating scale ranging from 1 = position B text to 5 = position A text . Response category 3 of the rating scale was labeled as neither . For example, the response “1” text for the belief item on the topic of biomass was “Biomass is not an environmentally friendly alternative for the future,” and the response “5” text was “Biomass is the environmentally friendly alternative to natural gas and petroleum.” Responses on the prior belief items were used to code the belief consistency of the claims for each socioscientific issue for each participant individually. To this end, the argumentative position of the claims (Position A vs. position B) was recorded so that for participants favoring position B, claims pro Position B were considered belief-consistent and claims pro Position A were considered belief-inconsistent. Similarly, for participants favoring position A, claims pro Position A were considered belief-consistent and claims pro Position B were considered belief-inconsistent. For example, for the topic of biomass participants favoring the argumentative stance “Biomass is the environmentally friendly alternative to natural gas and petroleum,” the two experimental claims “Substituting fossil fuels with biomass can reduce CO2 emissions” and “The current demand for fuel in Germany could already be completely covered by German biomass” would be belief-consistent, whereas the two experimental claims “For mass production of biomass, agriculture would need to be expanded at the expense of rainforests, among other things” and “Considering the transport distances in biofuel production, they have a similarly poor ecological balance as fossil fuels” would be belief-inconsistent. The opposite would be true for participants favoring the argumentative stance “Biomass is not an environmentally friendly alternative for the future.” If participants indicated no preference for one argumentative position on a socioscientific issue (response category 3 = neither ), the claims for this socioscientific issue were coded as neutral.

## Self-assessed prior knowledge

We asked participants for each socioscientific issue relevant to the experimental material how much they already know about this topic. Participants provided their subjective responses on a rating scale from 1 = no prior knowledge to 5 = high prior knowledge .

## Norming study

A norming study was conducted to ensure that the experimental items (a) were perceived as comprehensible, (b) showed clear differences in terms of perceived factual accuracy between false and true claims, and (c) showed clear differences in terms of argumentative position between claims of different positions on the socioscientific issues. Four claims were constructed for 15 socioscientific issues (e.g., global warming, cannabis legalization, vegan diet for children, alternative medicine) varying in argumentative stance (argumentative pro stance A vs. argumentative con stance B) and factual accuracy (true vs. false). Items were constructed to mimic news postings providing claims on socioscientific controversies available on the Internet. Table 1 2  provides examples of claims per argumentative stance and factual accuracy for the biomass topic (translated into English). We took care to construct items of similar structure and length (number of words: M = 14.22, SD = 3.20). To ensure that the claims were perceived as intended, 55 university students (43 women and 12 men) with an average age of 22.44 years ( SD = 6.51), most of them psychology majors ( n = 46) with an average number of 3.43 semesters ( SD = 2.34, range = 1 – 8) rated all items with regard to argumentative position, comprehensibility, credibility, and perceived validity on a 5-point rating scale (e.g., 1 = not at all comprehensible to 5 = very comprehensible ). Results of the norming study (see Table 2 ) suggested that one topic was not suitable (Bermuda-Triangle) because 60% of participants had no clear stance on this issue. For the 14 remaining topics, the number of non-neutral participants (i.e., participants preferring one argumentative stance in the issue) ranged from 62% to 95%. For each of these topics, some participants favored argumentative position A in the controversy (range = 9 – 51%), and some participants favored argumentative position B in the controversy (range = 1 – 33%). Two-factor analysis of variance for ranks according to Friedman with paired samples were conducted. All claims were rated as comprehensible with no significant differences in judgments between claims of different stance or factual accuracy ( M = 4.59, SD = 0.73, range = 3.87 – 4.93, for all comparisons, χ 2 < 1.00, p >.458). For credibility, we found significant differences between true and false claims (for all comparisons, χ 2 > 1.67, p <.001), and no differences between contra and pro claims (for all comparisons, χ 2 < 1.00, p >.780). Similarly, for perceived validity, we found significant differences between true and false claims (for all comparisons, χ 2 > 1.83, p <.001) and no difference between contra and pro claims (for all comparisons, χ 2 < 1.00, p = 1.00). Finally, for the argumentative position of the claims, we found significant differences between pro and contra claims (for all comparisons, χ 2 > 1.77, p <.001), and no difference between true and false claims (for all comparisons, χ 2 < 1.00, p = 1.00). Thus, the claims were judged by participants in line with our expectations and showed clear differences in terms of factual accuracy between false and true claims and in terms of argumentative position between claims of different positions on the socioscientific issues. Hence, based on the pilot study, four claims on 14 of 15 socioscientific issues (56 claims in total) were used as experimental materials in Experiment 1 .

## Validity judgment task

In the validity judgment task, participants were instructed to judge whether each of the 56 claims were true or false. The validity judgment task only contained claims on the socioscientific issues for which beliefs were assessed with the belief assessment. Participants were instructed “Please decide as quickly as possible whether the statement is true or not,” and needed to press the “K” key when evaluating a claim as true or press the “D” key when evaluating a claim as false. Participants were asked to rest the index fingers of their right and left hand loosely on the two response keys during the entire experiment to react as quickly as possible. The claims were presented one by one on a computer screen in black font (Arial, approximate height 1 cm) against a white background and in random order. The experiment was presented with Inquisit Web (Version 6.6.1). Response latencies and the accuracy (correct vs. incorrect) of participants’ responses to the claims were recorded.

## Procedure

Experiment 1 was an online experiment with an average duration of 30 min. After informed consent was obtained from participants (see Ethics statement section), participants indicated their prior beliefs and rated their prior knowledge on each of the socioscientific issues. Participants then worked on the validity judgment task. 1 To ensure that participants were reading the claims carefully, they were provided with a comprehension question after approximately eight claims during the validity judgment task. The comprehension question asked participants to indicate the topic of the claim. For this purpose, the name of a (randomly chosen) socioscientific topic was presented on the screen and participants were asked to provide a binary judgment by pressing the corresponding key (“K” = claim statement corresponds to the socioscientific topic, “D” = claim statement does not correspond to the socioscientific topic). After finishing the validity judgment task, participants provided demographic data (see Participants section) and answered three control items that assessed whether participants had read the questions and items thoroughly and had provided all answers seriously on a 5-point rating scale from 1 = totally disagree to 5 = totally agree . Participants were than thanked and debriefed.

## Ethics statement

At the beginning of the online experiment, informed consent of participants was obtained and only participants who gave their consent could start the experiment. Participants were provided with written information about ethical guidelines (e.g., data would be fully anonymized, participation was voluntary, they had the right to withdraw their consent to participate at any time). The experiment fulfilled all standards specified by German laws and ethical regulations for psychological research (see https://www.dgps.de/die-dgps/aufgaben-und-ziele/berufsethische-richtlinien/#c53 ) and no Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval had to be obtained per those regulations.

## Design

The design was a full 3 (belief consistency: belief-consistent, belief-inconsistent, neutral) × 2 (factual accuracy: true vs. false) within-subjects design. The order of the claims was randomized for each participant.

## Analyses

For Hypothesis 1.1 and Hypothesis 1.2, errors in the validity judgment task were analyzed with a generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) with the lme4 package with bobyqa as the optimizing function (Version 1.1–33, Bates et al., 2023 ) and lmerTest package (Version 3.1-3, Kuznetsova et al., 2020 ) in R (R version 4.5.2, R Core Team, 2025 ). Participants and items were included as random factors (random intercepts) and factual accuracy (true = 1, false = −1), belief consistency (Contrast 1: belief-consistent = 0.5, belief-inconsistent = 0.5, neutral = −1; Contrast 2: belief-consistent = 1, belief-inconsistent = −1, neutral = 0) and their interactions were included as fixed effects. For belief consistency, Contrast 1 incorporated the difference of the two belief-relevant conditions to the neutral condition and Contrast 2 incorporated the effect of belief consistency, that is, the difference between belief-consistent and belief-inconsistent claims. Hence, the main effect of factual accuracy on error rates was relevant for Hypothesis 1.1 and the interaction of belief consistency Contrast 2, and factual accuracy on error rates was relevant for Hypothesis 1.2.

To test Hypothesis 1.3, response latencies per word were analyzed for correct responses. Only correct answers were considered for the analysis of reaction times, as the inconsistency effect expected in Hypothesis 1.3 should occur here. An LMM was specified with participants and items included as random factors and factual accuracy (true = 1, false = −1), belief consistency (Contrast 1: 0.5 = belief-consistent, 0.5 = belief-inconsistent, −1 = neutral; Contrast 2: 1 = belief-consistent, −1 = belief-inconsistent, 0 = neutral), and their interactions as fixed effects. Relevant for Hypothesis 1.3 was the interaction of belief consistency Contrast 2, and factual accuracy for the response latencies of correct responses in the validity judgment task.

Effect sizes (Cohen’s f 2 ) for significant fixed effects were determined with a model-comparison approach comparing the variance explained by the model without the focal effect to the model with the focal effect using the R-package MuMIn (Version: 1.47.5, Bartón, 2023 ). For example, a (G)LMM was specified that included all main effects (Model 0) and a (G)LMM was specified that included in addition to the main effects the interactions (Model 1) to test the effect size for the interaction of belief consistency and factual accuracy. Effect sizes (Cohen’s f 2 ) were then determined by the formula: ( R 2 
 Model 1 - R 2 
 Model 0 )/(1- R 2 
 Model 1 ). For determining R 2 , we used the marginal R 2 value (theoretical method) as an estimation of the variance explained by the fixed effects in the model (see Nakagawa et al., 2017 ).

Hypothesis tests were based on Type-I error probability of.05 and were performed as two-tailed tests. However, we used one-tailed testing for directional hypotheses in follow-up tests of significant interactions when means were in the expected direction (e.g., Maxwell & Delaney, 2000 , p. 144).

## Experiment 2

It is important to note that the sample size of Experiment 2 was below our sample size estimation as we had not anticipated such a high level of data loss in Experiment 2 and were unable to collect further data due to budget constraints. Nevertheless, the sensitivity analysis showed that sample size was still sufficient to detect a small effect size.

Experiments 1 and 2 consistently suggest that when readers have to judge whether claims on socioscientific issues are true, their judgments are strongly influenced by their prior beliefs. Participants made more errors in the explicit validity judgment task for false belief-consistent claims. We assume this finding to be caused by readers’ natural tendency to judge claims as true when they are consistent with their beliefs. The results of both experiments are consistent with the assumption that readers often rely on fast judgments of plausibility and intuitions when making validity judgments (Brashier & Marsh, 2020 ) and that beliefs are especially used for making these judgments. The results of the response times, specifically the longer response latencies for accurate responses to true but belief-inconsistent claims and to false but belief-consistent claims, hint at processing difficulties that might have increased the cognitive effort required to provide correct validity judgments for false but belief-consistent and true but belief-inconsistent information. Theory and research on validation suggests that readers are sensitive to information that is inconsistent with (re-)activated memory content such as prior knowledge and beliefs (e.g., Albrecht & O’ Brien, 1993 ; Cook & Myers, 2004 ; for an overview, see Isberner & Richter, 2014 ). In the context of the present research, this seems to be primarily a sensitivity to belief consistency because reaction times for correct responses to neutral and belief-consistent claims did not differ significantly depending on their truth status. In contrast, we found longer reaction times for correct responses to true claims for belief-inconsistent items. This finding resembles typical results obtained with the inconsistency paradigm (e.g., Albrecht & O’ Brien, 1993 ; for an overview, see Cook & O’Brien, 2023 ). In these studies, prolonged reaction times are also found when information contradicts readers’ current mental model of the situation described in a text. Applied to the present findings, belief consistency seems to be the decisive factor in determining whether new information contradicts readers’ current mental model of socioscientific issues. The data from the norming study suggest that participants likely possessed the relevant knowledge to determine which information is true or false. In addition, the reaction time differences suggest that participants were able to provide the correct response in their validity judgments, but that it took them longer to make such correct judgments when belief consistency and factual accuracy of the claims diverged. However, it is still important to keep in mind that prior knowledge was neither manipulated nor objectively measured in Experiments 1 and 2 .

In sum, the prolonged reaction times for true but belief-inconsistent judgments may be understood as the result of belief-based validation. However, in Experiments 1 and 2 , participants were explicitly asked to judge the validity of the information, which invited participants to make strategic judgments about the validity of information. Hence, determining the unique role that nonstrategic validation might have played in causing the effects found in Experiments 1 and 2 is not possible. To provide a more informative investigation of whether validation in the case of belief-relevant claims that provide true and false information on socioscientific issues occurs without readers’ strategic intent and control, we conducted Experiment 3 .

## Experiment 3

In Experiment 3 , we used the epistemic Stroop task to investigate whether factual accuracy and belief consistency of information play a role for validation. The epistemic Stroop task can reveal whether reading and comprehension entails the involuntary rejection of false or belief-inconsistent information, even when the focal task requires no explicit judgment of validity or belief consistency. Specifically, the epistemic Stroop task is nonepistemic and nonsemantic in nature, as participants are simply tasked to judge whether a word is written in blue or black. This task is therefore suitable to indicate whether and how belief consistency and/or factual accuracy of information are processed by readers during reading without intention and control. Based on earlier research, we hypothesized that belief consistency (e.g., Gilead et al., 2019 ) and factual accuracy of information (e.g., Richter et al., 2009 ) can be sources for validation, and thus readers should be sensitive to false as well as belief-inconsistent information. In the epistemic Stroop task, a nonstrategic validation of information should evoke a negative response tendency for false and for belief-inconsistent information. Such an effect should result in longer responses after false or belief-inconsistent information when the required response in an unrelated task is “yes.” Consequently, we expected that participants would need more time to provide affirmative responses (“yes”) in the epistemic Stroop task for belief-inconsistent compared to belief-consistent claims (Hypothesis 3.1). In addition, we expected that participants would need more time to provide affirmative responses for false compared to true claims (Hypothesis 3.2). Moreover, in line with the results of Experiments 1 and 2 and our theoretical considerations, we expected prior beliefs to be the most important source of validation. Therefore, the slow-down in affirmative responses after belief-inconsistent compared to belief-consistent claims should be more pronounced than the slow-down in false compared to true statements (Hypothesis 3.3). How belief consistency and factual accuracy interactively influence affirmative judgments in the epistemic Stroop task was treated as an open research question. Experiment 3 and the hypotheses tested in this experiment have been preregistered ( https://aspredicted.org/NP1_1WZ ).

## Method

## Participants

The required sample size estimated with G*Power (Faul et al., 2007 ) was 76 participants, with α =.05, power (1 – β) =.90, assuming an effect size of f =.17 (based on Abendroth et al., 2022 ), and a factor level intercorrelation of ρ =.5. To account for potential exclusions, we aimed for a sample size of 95 participants. Participants were recruited via Clickworker and received a reimbursement equivalent to the German minimum wage for participating (6 Euro). A total of 93 participants completed the Experiment. However, nine participants were excluded because they failed to correctly respond to the control questions (e.g., “This is a control item. Please choose response category 3”) or completed the experiment more than once. The data from 84 participants (27 woman, 57 men) with an average age of 40 years ( M = 38.94, SD = 10.58) were analyzed. All participants were native German speakers. Two participants had a high school diploma as their highest level of education, seven had a secondary school certificate, five had an advanced technical college certificate, 20 had a high school degree, and 50 had a university of applied sciences degree or a university degree. Seventy-six participants were right-handed and eight were left-handed.

## Material

The set of items used in Experiments 1 and 2 was also used for the experimental trials in Experiment 3 . In addition, 56 filler claim sentences were constructed for 14 socioscientific issues which were different from those used for the experimental trials.

## Procedure

Participants worked individually in the online experiment provided with Inquisit Web (Version 6.6.1). After participants provided informed consent (see the Ethic statement section in Experiment 1 ), participants’ prior beliefs and their self-assessed prior knowledge for the socioscientific issues of the experimental trials were measured. Then, participants worked on the epistemic Stroop task. They first received written instructions and worked on a practice trial to familiarize themselves with the task. Participants were informed that they would be presented with sentences word by word in rapid sequence and that the presentation would stop at some words. For these words, participants were asked to decide as quickly as possible whether the word was written in blue or not. To allow fast responses, participants were instructed to rest their index fingers loosely on the two answer keys (“K” and “D”) throughout the experiment. If the word was written in blue, participants were required to press the key “K” (= yes). If the word was not written in blue (that is, written in black), they had to press the key “D” (= no) (color judgment task, Isberner & Richter, 2013 ). All claims were presented word by word on a computer screen using Rapid Serial Visual Presentation with a fixed rate of 450 ms per word.

In all experimental trials, the target word (i.e., the word participants were required to judge) was the final word of the claim. For these trials, factual accuracy and argumentative position of each claim were varied orthogonally to the required response (“yes” vs. “no”). Moreover, the final word of half of the experimental trials was presented in blue (requiring a “yes” response) and the final word of the other half of the experimental trials was presented in black (requiring a “no” response). The target word remained on-screen until participants provided a response.

In all filler trials, participants’ responses for the color judgment task were required after the second to ninth word of the sentence (seven trials each), to make the occurrence of the color judgment task less predictable. The target word of the filler trials remained black in half of the filler trials (requiring a “no” response) and was presented in blue in the other half of the filler trials (requiring a “yes” response).

All words except the target words were presented in black font (Arial, approximate height 1 cm) against a white background. Target words were either presented in black or blue font (Arial, approximate height 1 cm) against a white background. Participants were further informed that they would be asked comprehension questions after a random number of trials. For these comprehension questions, participants were presented with the name of a topic and were asked to decide as quickly as possible whether this topic was related to the content of the previously read sentence and to indicate their decision with the response keys “K” (= yes) and “D” (= no).

Claims were presented in two blocks, each with half of the claims and with a 3-min break between the two blocks. After the epistemic Stroop task, participants provided demographic data and answered three control items inquiring whether they had read the questions and items thoroughly and had responded attentively (responses were assessed on a rating scale from 1 = totally disagree to 5 = totally agree ). Participants were then thanked and debriefed.

## Design

A full within-subjects design with 3 (belief consistency: belief-consistent, belief-inconsistent, neutral) × 2 (factual accuracy: true vs. false) × 2 (required response: yes vs. no) was used in Experiment 3 . The order of the claims, as well the assignment of the required responses (“yes” vs. “no”) to claims, was counterbalanced across participants. Response latencies were recorded as the dependent variable. Condition (i.e., which half of the experimental trials was presented with a blue target word) was included as a control factor.

## Analyses

The LMM used to analyze response latencies in the epistemic Stroop task included participants and items as random factors (random intercepts) and the three predictors:

Factual accuracy (true = 1, false = −1),

Belief consistency included as two contrasts (Contrast 1: belief-consistent = 0.5, belief-inconsistent = 0.5, neutral = −1; Contrast 2: belief-consistent = 1, belief-inconsistent = −1, neutral = 0), and

Required response (yes = 1, no = −1) as well as their interactions as fixed effects. Condition was included as a contrast-coded fixed effect.

Relevant for the hypothesis testing of Experiment 3 was the significant three-way interaction of factual accuracy, belief consistency Contrast 2, and required response. Moreover, a simple main effect for affirmative responses of belief consistency Contrast 2 was relevant for testing Hypothesis 3.1, and a simple main effect for affirmative responses of factual accuracy was relevant for testing Hypothesis 3.2. In addition, the average estimated reaction time differences between false and true claims and between belief-consistent and belief-inconsistent claims were investigated for Hypothesis 3.3.

As predicted by Hypothesis 3.2 and replicating earlier results (e.g., Isberner & Richter, 2013 ; Richter et al., 2009 ), participants also needed more time to provide affirmative responses for false claims ( M = 523, SD = 32) compared to true claims ( M = 473, SD = 32). The average estimated reaction time difference between false and true claims was 50 ms, whereas the reaction time difference between belief-consistent and belief-inconsistent claims was estimated at 26 ms. However, the differences in response latencies for factual accuracy (response latencies to false claims minus response latencies to true claims) and the differences in response latencies for belief consistency (response latencies to belief-inconsistent claims minus response latencies to belief-consistent claims) were not significant, t( 83) = −0.54, p =.593. This result suggests that, contrary to Hypothesis 3.3, we found no evidence for a difference in the general slowing in affirmative responses as a function of factual accuracy and belief consistency.

Response latencies (in ms) for correct responses in the epistemic Stroop task of Experiment 3 as a function of belief consistency, factual accuracy, and required response . Error bars represent the standard error of the mean. Significant differences are marked with an asterisk

In the follow-up tests of the significant three-way interaction, we further found a significant interaction of belief consistency Contrast 2, and factual accuracy within affirmative responses, β = 13.81, t (4,219) = 1.85, p =.032 (one-tailed). Follow-up tests revealed that participants’ affirmative responses to belief-consistent claims were not significantly influenced by factual accuracy, β = −4.03, t (472) = −0.35, p =.725. In contrast, participants’ affirmative responses were longer for false compared to true belief-inconsistent claims, β = −31.55, t (472) = −2.77, p =.006. Similarly, for neutral claims, participants’ affirmative responses were longer for false compared to true neutral claims, β = −38.48, t (910) = −2.78, p =.006. Finally, the simple main-effects analysis further revealed no main effect of belief consistency Contrast 2 for affirmative responses to true claims, β = 1.24, t (4,201) = 0.12, p =.906. For false claims, however, participants’ affirmative responses were longer for belief-inconsistent than for belief-consistent claims, β = −26.38, t (4,236) = −2.49, p =.013.

For negative responses (i.e., trials that required a “no” response in the color judgment task), the simple main-effects analysis revealed significant main effects of belief consistency Contrast 2 (β = −28.12, t (3,919) = −3.81, p <.001), and factual accuracy, β = −18.62, t (127) = −2.37, p =.019. However, we found no significant interaction of belief consistency Contrast 2, and factual accuracy, β = −6.94, t (3,919) = −0.94, p =.348.

In sum, the results of Experiment 3 indicate that task performance was strongly influenced by belief consistency and the factual accuracy of the claims, even though these factors were irrelevant to the actual task – identifying whether the last word of a sentence was written in blue or black. The main effect of belief consistency in the affirmative responses demonstrates that belief-inconsistent information interferes with processing the information in a manner similar to false information. This result replicates earlier results on the epistemic Stroop task (e.g., Abendroth et al., 2022 ; Gilead et al., 2019 ; Isberner & Richter, 2013 ), but uses a new stimulus type, namely claims on socioscientific issues. In addition, our response time differences are similar to earlier studies using the epistemic Stroop task with a color judgment task, where response time differences of 31 ms (Isberner & Richter, 2013 ) and 24 ms (Abendroth et al., 2022 ) were found for affirmative responses to plausible/true and implausible/false items.

Moreover, the interaction of belief consistency and factual accuracy for affirmative responses suggests that our cognitive system might not always differentiate correctly between true and false information that is belief-consistent. Affirmative responses were especially slowed down for neutral false information, indicating interference in affirmative responses, which is in accordance with earlier research showing an interference in affirmative responses for false or implausible information (e.g., Isberner & Richter, 2013 ; Richter et al., 2009 ). Thus, in line with prior research, validation for belief-irrelevant neutral information was primarily based on the factual accuracy of claims. Affirmative responses were also particularly slowed down for belief-inconsistent false information, indicating that factual accuracy affects the processing of belief-inconsistent content without strategic control. However, there was no evidence for an effect of factual accuracy on response times for affirmative responses to belief-consistent information. These findings suggest that belief-consistent information may be quickly and involuntarily associated with truthfulness due to validation and the strong perceived link between belief consistency and plausibility (for the latter, see also Abendroth & Richter, 2021a ). This result also indicates that validation – which generally can be viewed as an effective means to prevent the cognitive system from processing false and inaccurate information – might lead to inaccurate outcomes when factual accuracy and belief consistency are dissociated. However, as for Experiments 1 and 2 , it is important to note that participants were only asked for their subjective prior knowledge of each socioscientific issue and no objective measure was included.

## Experiment 4

Experiment 4 aimed to replicate the findings from Experiments 1 – 3 in a larger sample. Moreover, Experiment 4 included a multiple-choice, single-answer task designed to assess whether participants knew the facts relevant to evaluating each claim’s factual accuracy. For example, to judge the factual accuracy of the statement “Human greenhouse gases accumulate heat in the atmosphere and warm the earth’s climate,” it is essential to understand the role of greenhouse gases in climate change. Similarly, recognizing that the claim “A vegan diet in childhood is healthy because the calcium in animal products is detrimental to children’s health” is factually incorrect requires basic knowledge of the importance of calcium in children’s diets.

In Experiment 4 , we employed both the validity judgment task and the epistemic Stroop task. As in Experiment 3 , we expected affirmative judgments (“yes”) in the epistemic Stroop task to be longer for belief-inconsistent claims compared to belief-consistent claims (Hypothesis 4.1). In addition, we expected affirmative judgments to be longer for false claims compared to true claims (Hypothesis 4.2). Given the results of Experiment 3 , we also expected an interaction of belief consistency and factual accuracy for affirmative responses (Hypothesis 4.3). Specifically, affirmative responses to belief-inconsistent claims were expected to be longer for false compared to true claims. In contrast, for belief-consistent claims, affirmative responses should be unaffected – or only minimally affected – by factual accuracy.

In the validity judgment task, we expected more errors for false claims compared to true claims (Hypothesis 4.4). In addition, we expected an interaction of belief consistency and factual accuracy on the error rates (Hypothesis 4.5), with more errors made for belief-consistent false claims (compared to belief-consistent true claims) but a smaller (or non-existent) difference for belief-inconsistent true or false claims. For the response latencies of correct responses, we expected response latencies for correct responses to true claims to be faster for belief-consistent claims compared to belief-inconsistent claims and the reverse effect for correct responses to false claims (Hypothesis 4.6). Experiment 4 and the hypotheses tested in this experiment have been preregistered ( https://aspredicted.org/tftt-r7pg.pdf ).

## Method

## Participants

The required sample size estimated with G*Power (Faul et al., 2007 ) was 216 participants, with α =.05, power (1 – β) =.90, assuming an effect size of f =.10 (based on the minimum observed effects for the focal hypotheses in Experiments 1 – 3 ), and a factor-level intercorrelation of ρ =.5. To account for potential exclusions, we aimed for a sample size of 250 participants.

We recruited participants via Clickworker and reimbursed them at a rate equivalent to the German minimum wage for participating (6.41 Euro). Participants with incomplete participation were excluded. A total of 249 participants completed Experiment 4 . In line with the preregistered exclusion criteria, three participants who (a) were not able to follow the instructions, (b) were not serious in their responses, and (c) reported that they had not read the instructions and claims carefully were excluded. In addition, participants who failed to respond correctly to control questions ( n = 10) were also excluded. To align the sample of Experiment 4 with that of Experiments 1 – 3 , we further excluded data from non-native German speakers ( n = 10) from the dataset.

The final dataset of Experiment 4 consisted of 226 participants (74 women, 150 men, one non-binary, one no response). Participants’ mean age was 36.92 years ( SD = 10.53, range = 18–60). The highest level of education of four participants was a high school diploma, 43 reported having a secondary school certificate as their highest degree, 11 reported having an advanced technical college certificate, 57 reported having a high school degree, and 111 reported having a university of applied sciences degree or university degree. Most of the participants were right-handed ( n = 196), whereas only 25 were left-handed and five were ambidextrous.

## Material

## Epistemic Stroop task and validity judgment task

The instructions for the validity judgment task were the same as in Experiments 1 and 2 and the instructions for the epistemic Stroop task were the same as in Experiment 3 . The set of items used in Experiments 1 – 3 served as the basis for the experimental items of Experiment 4 . However, since our goal was to create individual prior knowledge items that assessed specific facts relevant to each experimental item, four topics had to be excluded because they did not meet this criterion. The final item set comprised 80 items covering 20 socioscientific issues: 40 experimental claims addressing ten socioscientific issues and 40 filler claims addressing ten different socioscientific issues. The complete set of items is available in the Open Science Framework repository (osf.io/46ug2).

## Belief assessment

We assessed prior beliefs on the ten socioscientific issues relevant to the experimental items following the procedure in Experiment 3 .

## Prior knowledge assessment

For each experimental claim, one single-choice item assessing prior knowledge was constructed. For each prior knowledge question, one correct answer and two distractors were provided as response options. For example, for the experimental item on calcium in children’s diet (“a vegan diet in childhood is healthy because the calcium contained in animal products is detrimental to children’s health”), the prior knowledge question asked, “What is calcium needed for in children’s diets?” Response options were:

“Calcium is needed for healthy growth and bone development”;

“Calcium is needed to improve concentration and memory”; and

“Calcium is important for increasing energy production in the body.”

Each prior knowledge item was scored as correctly or incorrectly answered. Although the presence of prior knowledge was included as an item-level predictor in the LMMs, we also examined whether the prior knowledge scale reliably assessed participants’ knowledge. Cronbach’s alpha indicated good internal consistency (α =.81).

## Procedure

Experiment 4 was implemented as an online experiment via Inquisit Web (Version 6.6.1) and consisted of four parts. First, after participants provided informed consent (see the Ethics statement section in Experiment 1 ), participants’ prior beliefs for the socioscientific issues of the experimental trials were assessed. Second, the prior knowledge relevant to judging the factual accuracy of each experimental item was assessed using single-choice questions. Third, participants worked on the epistemic Stroop task (see Experiment 3 ). Fourth, participants worked on the validity judgment task (see Experiments 1 and 2 ). At the end of the experiment, participants provided demographic data and answered the control items. They were then thanked and debriefed.

## Design

Experiment 4 was based on a full 3 (belief consistency: belief-consistent, belief-inconsistent, neutral) × 2 (factual accuracy: true vs. false) × 2 (required response: yes vs. no) within-subjects design for the epistemic Stroop task. It was based on a 3 (belief consistency: belief-consistent, belief-inconsistent, neutral) × 2 (factual accuracy: true vs. false) within-subjects design for the validity judgment task. The order of claims and the pairing of required responses (“yes” vs. “no”) with each claim in the epistemic Stroop task were counterbalanced across participants. For both the epistemic Stroop task and the validity judgment task, the availability of prior knowledge (yes vs. no) was included as an additional fixed effect for each claim. Response latencies in the epistemic Stroop task, as well as response latencies and errors in the validity judgment task, were recorded as dependent variables.

## Results and discussion

## Control items and comprehension questions

As in Experiments 1 – 3 , participants reported being able to follow the instructions ( M = 4.76, SD = 0.52, range = 3–5), to have provided answers seriously ( M = 4.93, SD = 0.29, range = 3–5), and to have read the instructions and claims carefully ( M = 4.90, SD = 0.34, range = 3–5). Participants answered 88% of the comprehension questions correctly ( SD =.33).

## Prior beliefs

There was no socioscientific issue for which all participants agreed exclusively with one argumentative position, and the number of participants favoring Position A and Position B was approximately balanced (e.g., pre-implantation diagnostics: 43% agreement with Position A vs. 37% with Position B; plant-based fuels: 72% agreement with Position A vs. 15% with Position B; see Table 11 ).

## Prior knowledge

On average, participants answered 30 of the 40 prior knowledge questions correctly ( SD = 4.93, range = 11–38).

## Response latencies epistemic Stroop task

From 9,040 observations (226 participants × 40 items) recorded for the experimental trials in the epistemic Stroop task, 7,978 response latencies for correct responses (88% of all responses) were analyzed. As preregistered, response latencies deviating more than three standard deviations from either the subject or item mean were excluded as outliers (176 observations; 2.21% of all correct latencies). An investigation of Cook’s D and for DFBETAS ij revealed that no participant exceeded the cutoff values. Table 12 presents the results of the LMM for response latencies in the epistemic Stroop task. The model specifications followed Experiment 3 (see Analyses section in Experiment 3 ), but the availability of prior knowledge was further added as a fixed effect.

Participants were faster in providing an affirmative response ( M = 530.32, SD = 21.52) compared to a negative response ( M = 786.90, SD = 18.85), β = −122.54, t (7,103) = −32.15, p <.001, Cohen’s f 2 =.091. We also found a significant three-way interaction of factual accuracy, belief consistency Contrast 2, and required response, β = 8.58, t (7,565) = 2.12, p =.034, Cohen’s f 2 =.002 (Fig. 6 ). In contrast to Hypothesis 4.1, follow-up tests of simple main effects for affirmative responses revealed no effect of belief consistency Contrast 2, β = 4.26, t (7,386) = 0.69, p =.247 (one-tailed). However, in line with Hypothesis 4.2, participants’ affirmative responses to false claims ( M = 523, SD = 32) were slower than to true claims ( M = 473, SD = 32), β = −22.38, t (59) = −2.27, p =.013 (one-tailed). In the follow-up tests of the significant three-way interaction, we further found the expected interaction of belief consistency Contrast 2, and factual accuracy within affirmative responses, β = 12.23, t (7,388) = 1.97, p =.025 (one-tailed). In line with Hypothesis 4.3 and replicating the results of Experiment 3 , participants’ affirmative responses took longer for false compared to true belief-inconsistent claims, β = −36.80, t (119) = −3.12, p =.001 (one-tailed). No significant difference was found for affirmative responses to belief-consistent true versus false claims (β = −12.60, t (128) = −1.05, p =.295) or neutral true versus false claims, β = −17.80, t (235) = −1.28, p =.204. In addition, no effects of belief consistency or factual accuracy were found for negative responses (all | t | < 1.00).

Taken together, the results for reaction times in the epistemic Stroop task in Experiment 4 are in line with the predictions of Hypothesis 4.3 and replicate the main result from Experiment 3 : Participants needed more time to provide affirmative responses to belief-inconsistent false claims compared to belief-inconsistent true claims, whereas no such moderating effect of factual accuracy occurred for belief-consistent claims.

## Error rates validity judgment task

No data exceeded the cut-off values for Cook’s D and for DFBETAS ij . Table 13 presents the percentage of yes responses as a function of belief consistency and factual accuracy of the validity judgment task from Experiment 4 , and Table 14 presents the results of the GLMM for error rates and of the LMM for response latencies in the validity judgment task. In line with Hypothesis 4.4, we found that participants made more errors in the validity judgment task for false claims ( M =.40, SE =.04) compared to true claims ( M =.28, SE =.03), β = −0.27, z = −2.39, p =.017, Cohen’s f 2 =.013. In addition, and in line with Hypothesis 4.5, we also found an interaction of belief consistency Contrast 2, and factual accuracy on error rates, β = −0.47, z = −15.43, p <.001, Cohen’s f 2 =.043 (Fig. 7 ). In addition, simple main effects of belief consistency Contrast 2 were found in opposite directions for true, β = −0.48, z = −10.99, p <.001, and false claims, β = 0.45, z = 10.88, p <.001. Hence, results for error rates in the validity judgment task are fully in line with Hypothesis 4.5 and replicate the earlier results of Experiments 1 and 2 .

## Response latencies validity judgment task

Response latencies per word were analyzed for 5,919 correct responses (65% of all responses). Following the procedure of Experiments 1 and 2 , 114 response latencies deviating more than three standard deviations from either the subject or the item mean were excluded as outliers (1.92% of all correct latencies).

As predicted by Hypothesis 4.6, we found a significant interaction of factual accuracy and belief consistency Contrast 2, β = −10.00, t (5,586) = −4.27, p <.001, Cohen’s f 2 =.002 (Fig. 8 ). In line with our expectations, participants needed more time to provide correct responses to true belief-inconsistent claims compared to true belief-consistent claims, β = −9.26, t (5,589) = −2.94, p =.002 (one-tailed). In contrast, participants needed more time to correctly respond to false belief-consistent claims compared to false belief-inconsistent claims, β = 10.74, t (5,578) = 3.10, p =.001 (one-tailed). Moreover, participants’ correct responses to true belief-inconsistent claims were longer compared to false belief-inconsistent claims (β = 22.25, t (43) = 2.20, p =.033), whereas we found no significant simple main effect of factual accuracy for neutral claims, β = 11.99, t (48) = 1.14, p =.259, and belief-consistent claims, β = 2.97, t (44) = 0.29, p =.772. No other effects were significant (see Table 14 ). These results are fully in line with Hypothesis 4.6 and fully replicate the results from Experiments 1 and 2 .

## General discussion

In the present study, four experiments were conducted to examine whether and how readers’ prior beliefs influence the nonstrategic and strategic processing of (mis)information related to socioscientific issues. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first investigation to explore whether and how readers’ prior beliefs, distinct from their (subjective) factual knowledge on a topic, are employed in the strategic and nonstrategic evaluation of claims on socioscientific issues. Experiments 1 and 2 demonstrated that readers’ explicit validity judgments of socioscientific claims were strongly shaped by their prior beliefs. Participants tended to judge false claims as being true when these items were consistent with their beliefs and needed more time to correctly respond to true but belief-inconsistent claims and to false but belief-consistent claims. These results suggest that explicit validity judgments are strongly affected by belief consistency and not only by the claims’ factual accuracy. Moreover, the results support the assumption that belief consistency of information is a major source for validation (Richter & Maier, 2017 ). Participants needed more time to provide the correct answer to false belief-consistent and true belief-inconsistent information in the validity judgment task. Apparently, such judgments are not made easily by participants but might require greater cognitive effort to resolve the inconsistency, as reflected in the increased response time (e.g., Cook & Myers, 2004 ; Cook & O’Brien, 2023 ). However, since participants in Experiments 1 and 2 were explicitly instructed to judge the validity of the information, the nature of the validity judgment task in these experiments does not allow us to differentiate between the effects of nonstrategic and strategic processing.

In Experiments 3 and 4 , we found that affirmative responses in an unrelated non-semantic and non-epistemic task were also strongly impacted by the belief consistency and factual accuracy of the claims. The interaction of belief consistency and the claims’ factual accuracy for affirmative responses in Experiments 3 and 4 especially suggests that our cognitive system might not always differentiate correctly between true and false belief-consistent information. Prior beliefs seem to influence validation during comprehension, which leads to processing differences between belief-consistent and belief-inconsistent information without readers’ intention or control, affecting even unrelated tasks. Consistent with the concept of validation, our findings in the epistemic Stroop task suggest that readers are sensitive to false as well as belief-inconsistent information, even if they are not motivated to focus on belief consistency or truth during comprehension (e.g., Isberner & Richter, 2013 ; Gilead et al., 2019 ; Richter et al., 2009 ). In line with earlier research, this sensitivity was visible as cognitive disruptions in providing affirmative responses in the epistemic Stroop task to such information. However, our experiments also went one step further by investigating the combined influence of (subjective) prior knowledge and prior beliefs during validation. As suggested by van Moort and colleagues ( 2021 ), what type of information is primarily used during validation in specific situations is of theoretical interest. The results from Experiment 4 in particular suggest that, for the validation of claims on socioscientific issues, factual accuracy may be less important than belief consistency.

The results of Experiment 4 further replicated the findings from both the validity judgment task (Experiments 1 and 2 ) and the epistemic Stroop task (Experiment 3 ) in a larger and more diverse sample. Additionally, this experiment incorporated a measure to assess participants’ prior knowledge relevant to the experimental claims. On average, participants answered 75% of the knowledge questions correctly, indicating that they likely possessed sufficient knowledge to accurately evaluate the factual accuracy of the claims.

In sum, the results support the assumptions of the Two-Step Model of Validation (Richter & Maier, 2017 ). The Two-Step Model of Validation not only makes predictions about the influence of prior beliefs on validation but also provides a theoretical basis for understanding how and when beliefs can distort the comprehension of information. In the present study, the comprehension of information was not directly examined. However, based on the assumptions of the Two-Step Model of Validation and the findings reported here, comprehension of belief-consistent information may be partly decoupled from its objective factual accuracy when readers hold strong and accessible beliefs. In this context, the results suggest that prior beliefs on current socioscientific issues might be more readily accessible from long-term memory, might be preferentially reactivated during resonance processes, and thus might be predominantly used for validation. In contrast, individual facts stored in long-term memory as part of readers’ prior knowledge do not appear to be retrieved reliably. Rather, the findings indicate that such facts are less frequently employed for validation, especially when validation based on prior beliefs is positive, as is the case for belief-consistent information. Conversely, for belief-inconsistent information, facts from readers’ prior knowledge are more likely to be retrieved and used in validation. Nonetheless, future research is needed to directly investigate the cognitive mechanisms involved in comprehending belief-relevant true and false information and to further corroborate the theoretical interpretation proposed here.

The item materials used in the four experiments were designed to simulate news postings presenting claims about socioscientific controversies found on the Internet. This approach was chosen because it reflects a common scenario of information search and processing. However, many readers today encounter socioscientific information primarily through social media as part of their daily routines. Therefore, the present research should be extended to this context, and future studies should develop materials that resemble social media posts with their distinctive features, as well as longer texts commonly found online. Generally, we expect that prior beliefs and prior knowledge similarly influence validation during the processing of social media content and extended online texts, just as they do for the claims used in the current experiments.

Moreover, in constructing the material and conducting the experiments, we have made every effort to minimize the impact of skewed belief distributions in the samples. Nevertheless, and unfortunately, belief distributions are often skewed for socioscientific issues, as neutral positions are rarely taken and one argumentative position tends to be preferred. Future studies using, ideally, socioscientific topics with a more even distribution of beliefs in the sample seem important for a stronger generalization of the results.

The processing differences between belief-consistent and belief-inconsistent information in the epistemic Stroop task of Experiments 3 and 4 indicate a source of bias that is difficult for readers to control. In addition, when the readers’ cognitive system more readily treats belief-consistent information as true during early processing, regardless of its objective factual accuracy, this early processing might lead to biased or even false reasoning and decision making. Even if the process of validation cannot be stopped or controlled by readers, future studies should investigate possible means that might be effective in reducing the biasing influence of prior beliefs in downstream cognitive processes. In this regard, metacognitive trainings, which especially highlight the role of prior beliefs in validation, have been shown to be successful (e.g., Abendroth & Richter,  2021b ). Such approaches augment other methods that reduce the impact of misinformation (for an overview, see Pennycook & Rand, 2021 ). Here, interventions that not only educate people about the influence of prior beliefs during comprehension, but that also emphasize the importance of the factual accuracy of information, seem promising, as positive effects of such interventions have already been demonstrated, for example, in relation to the sharing of misinformation (e.g., Pennycook et al., 2021 ). Another promising avenue to reduce the effects of misinformation is to strengthen people’s analytic state of mind through metacognitive prompts (Salovich & Rapp, 2021 ).

In the present study, the two tasks used allowed us to investigate what type of information influences validation. However, we could not investigate the role of immediate versus delayed validation processes as described in the RI-Val model (O’Brien & Cook, 2016 ). Future studies could investigate the time course of validation based on beliefs versus prior knowledge.

One-sided, conflicting, and partly false information on socioscientific issues is widely accessible. Although readers and public discourse would benefit from thorough processing and comprehension of all credible and relevant information, the results of the four experiments reported here clearly demonstrate that readers’ prior beliefs not only exert a strong influence on the explicit evaluation of socioscientific information but also on its processing during comprehension. The acceptance of belief-consistent false information without readers’ intent or control poses a particular threat to opinion-formation processes that extend beyond motivated reasoning. Specifically, motivated reasoning theories predict that prior beliefs affect reasoning when readers are motivated to defend their views, typically influencing reasoning rather than basic comprehension. However, when prior beliefs impact basic comprehension processes without strategic control, they more fundamentally impede informed participation in the digital information society and increase the risk of belief biases during misinformation processing. This may lead to the incorporation of belief-consistent misinformation into readers’ mental representations of socioscientific issues.
