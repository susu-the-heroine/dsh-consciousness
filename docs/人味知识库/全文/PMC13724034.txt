## Abstract

Language impairment is a core clinical manifestation of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) that profoundly affects autonomy, decision-making, and social participation. Building upon the 2015 review by Kuca et al., this narrative review synthesizes empirical evidence from 2016 through 2026. A structured Web of Science Core Collection search retrieved 154 records. Following screening, 66 records were excluded and 88 full-text reports were appraised, of which 13 were consolidated, yielding 75 cited core empirical studies alongside foundational diagnostic, neuropathological, and trial benchmarks. Four main areas are synthesized. First, progressive linguistic decline across clinical stages is characterized, delineating typical amnestic AD dementia, mild cognitive impairment due to AD, and the logopenic variant of primary progressive aphasia (lvPPA), highlighting the necessity of biological amyloid and tau biomarker confirmation. Second, automated speech and natural language processing biomarkers have emerged as scalable tools predicting progression to dementia and detecting biological amyloid-beta positivity; however, real-world translation remains constrained by demographic bias, cross-linguistic variations, acoustic sensitivity, and privacy issues. Third, non-pharmacological interventions, including structured language stimulation and naming therapy, demonstrate measurable task-specific improvements; nevertheless, gains are largely restricted to trained items with minimal transfer to everyday discourse. Caregiver communication training programs reliably improve dyadic interactions and mitigate caregiver distress. Fourth, approved symptomatic drugs do not directly reverse language decline, and add-on memantine in the randomized ROMEO-AD trial produced no statistically significant language benefit over donepezil alone. Disease-modifying anti-amyloid antibodies offer biological intervention for biomarker-positive lvPPA, but pivotal trials lacked primary speech endpoints, establishing eligibility rather than demonstrated language recovery. Non-invasive neuromodulation remains preliminary and requires sham-controlled trials. Communication should be recognized as a distinct clinical outcome in trial designs, prioritizing standardized speech endpoints to assess disease-modifying therapies.

## Introduction

Effective communication is indispensable for human agency, social connectedness, healthcare decision-making, informed consent, and the maintenance of reciprocal relationships. In Alzheimer’s disease (AD), linguistic deterioration is not an inconsequential secondary manifestation but a core clinical symptom that profoundly degrades quality of life for both affected individuals and their care partners. The breakdown of communicative competence impairs the ability of patients to express physical discomfort, describe emerging symptoms, articulate personal preferences, participate in shared care decisions, and preserve their personal identity. In a previous narrative review published in 2015, Kuca et al surveyed the clinical trajectory of language deficits across the mild, moderate, and severe stages of AD, summarizing early behavioral recommendations and symptomatic pharmacotherapy. 1 Over the past decade (2016–2026), the clinical and research landscape has evolved markedly, driven by the emergence of computational linguistics, digital voice biomarkers, structured non-pharmacological trial designs, biological biomarker definitions of neurodegenerative pathology, and the approval of disease-modifying monoclonal antibodies targeting amyloid-beta.

Historically, clinical diagnosis relied entirely on syndromic criteria established by consensus guidelines, such as the 1984 and 2011 National Institute on Aging-Alzheimer’s Association (NIA-AA) criteria. 2 Under these syndromic frameworks, probable AD dementia was diagnosed primarily in individuals presenting with an insidious amnestic syndrome characterized by prominent episodic memory retrieval failures. However, contemporary diagnostic consensus explicitly distinguishes syndromic clinical stages from biological disease definitions. The 2018 NIA-AA Research Framework defined AD biologically through in vivo evidence of amyloid-beta deposition (A), pathological tau phosphorylation (T), and neurodegeneration (N), regardless of clinical syndrome. 3 This biological framing has highlighted non-amnestic phenotypes of AD, most notably the logopenic variant of primary progressive aphasia (lvPPA). 4 While typical amnestic AD presents with early semantic search deficits alongside memory failure, lvPPA is characterized by early and prominent phonological short-term memory deficits, word-retrieval hesitations, and impaired sentence repetition, predominantly arising from underlying Alzheimer neuropathology. 4 Furthermore, linguistic features serve an essential role in differentiating AD from other neurodegenerative dementias, including dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB) and frontotemporal lobar degeneration (FTLD) syndromes such as behavioral-variant FTD, semantic-variant PPA, and nonfluent/agrammatic PPA. 4 

Despite significant progress across specialized disciplines, research findings often remain fragmented. Computational computer scientists develop speech-recognition algorithms in isolated acoustic corpora, speech-language pathologists investigate behavioral naming protocols in small cohorts, and clinical trialists evaluate disease-modifying therapies using composite cognitive batteries that lack dedicated language endpoints. Integrating these disparate domains within a coherent clinical perspective is essential. The rationale for unifying automated speech biomarkers, caregiver communication interventions, behavioral speech therapies, and pharmacological treatments within a single review is that language represents a continuous functional spectrum. Subtle linguistic alterations provide sensitive early screening opportunities, structured behavioral interventions maintain everyday communication capacity, and disease-modifying pharmacotherapies offer potential biological slowing.

To address these developments, this review addresses four explicit review questions:

Review Question 1 (RQ1): What are the distinct linguistic manifestations observed across the clinical spectrum of AD, and how do they differ between typical amnestic AD, lvPPA, and non-AD neurodegenerative conditions?

Review Question 2 (RQ2): What is the current performance, external validity, and clinical translation readiness of automated digital speech and language biomarkers for AD detection, staging, and biological screening?

Review Question 3 (RQ3): What empirical evidence supports behavioral, caregiver-directed, and speech-language therapy interventions, and to what degree do therapeutic benefits generalize to everyday functional communication?

Review Question 4 (RQ4): What are the measurable effects of approved symptomatic drugs, disease-modifying anti-amyloid therapies, and non-invasive neuromodulation on language outcomes, and what evidence distinguishes treatment eligibility from demonstrated clinical language recovery?

A narrative review methodology was selected because the scope of the inquiry spans heterogeneous methodological designs, ranging from computational machine-learning classification and open-label behavioral pilots to randomized pharmacological trials and neuropathological post-mortem evaluations. These divergent study designs, sample sizes, and outcome metrics preclude formal quantitative meta-analytic pooling but are suited to structured, critical narrative synthesis.

## Methods

## Search Strategy and Information Sources

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in the Web of Science Core Collection, covering four major citation indexes: Science Citation Index Expanded (SCI-EXPANDED), Social Sciences Citation Index (SSCI), Arts and Humanities Citation Index (A&HCI), and Emerging Sources Citation Index (ESCI). The search was executed on October 1, 2025, and updated through January 2026 to ensure full coverage of literature published between January 1, 2016, and January 2026. To capture studies where language and communication represented a primary focus of investigation rather than an incidental secondary test within an extensive cognitive battery, disease and linguistic terms were restricted to the title field, whereas intervention and treatment terms were searched broadly across the topic field (title, abstract, and keywords).

The exact search syntax executed was:

TI=(Alzheimer* AND (“language*” OR “speech*” OR “communication*” OR anomia OR aphasia OR “word finding” OR “fluency” OR naming OR discourse)) AND TS=((“social intervention*” OR “psychosocial intervention*” OR “communication training” OR “communication strateg*” OR “caregiver training” OR “care partner training” OR “speech-language therap*” OR “cognitive stimulation therap*” OR reminiscence) OR (treatment* OR therap* OR pharmacotherap* OR medication* OR drug* OR “clinical trial*” OR “disease-modifying*” OR “anti-amyloid” OR “anti-tau” OR immunotherap* OR vaccine* OR peptide* OR nutraceutical* OR supplement* OR “combination*”)) AND PY=(2016–2026).

## Study Selection and Screening Process

The literature search returned 154 records. All records were evaluated against predefined eligibility criteria. Inclusion criteria comprised: (1) peer-reviewed journal articles published in English between 2016 and 2026; (2) human study populations diagnosed with Alzheimer’s disease dementia, mild cognitive impairment (MCI) due to AD, or primary progressive aphasia associated with AD pathology; and (3) empirical evaluation of language or communication characteristics, automated digital speech assessment, behavioral or caregiver communication interventions, or medical treatments with reported linguistic outcomes. Exclusion criteria comprised: (1) non-peer-reviewed materials, editorials, commentaries, errata, conference meeting abstracts, and study protocols without empirical results; (2) non-human or in vitro basic science animal models lacking speech or communication evaluation; and (3) general dementia studies wherein language was mentioned only incidentally without dedicated measurement. Record screening was performed independently by the author and cross-checked by a second academic researcher to verify selection consistency. Reference tracking and backward citation snowballing were applied to identify foundational diagnostic frameworks, neuropathological landmarks, and pivotal Phase 3 clinical trials cited within the synthesized literature.

## Language Impairment Across the Phases of Alzheimer’s Disease

Linguistic breakdown in typical amnestic AD follows an insidious, progressive course that mirrors the spreading pattern of neurofibrillary tau pathology from transentorhinal and medial temporal structures to temporal-parietal and frontal association cortices. 3 In the prodromal and mild stages, linguistic impairment most frequently manifests as lexical retrieval difficulty (anomia). Patients experience tip-of-the-tongue phenomena, prolonged word-finding pauses, vague circumlocutions, and semantic substitutions (for example, referring to a watch as “a clock” or “the thing for time”). While articulatory agility and syntactic grammar remain largely intact during initial stages, semantic fluency is disproportionately impaired relative to phonemic letter fluency. Saranpää and colleagues conducted an item-level analysis of semantic fluency across prodromal and very early AD cohorts, demonstrating that individuals with AD travel through semantic conceptual space less efficiently than cognitively unimpaired peers, producing smaller semantic clusters, fewer transitions, and restricted lexical diversity. 9 In a prospective longitudinal investigation tracking individuals across the continuum from healthy aging to MCI and mild AD, Masumi and colleagues observed that expressive language, receptive comprehension, and executive-linguistic coordination all decline over time, with expressive lexical access demonstrating the earliest and steepest deterioration. 36 

As neuropathological burden progresses into the moderate disease phase, linguistic impairments become widespread across multiple receptive and expressive subsystems. In detailed evaluations of moderate-stage AD, Ortiz and colleagues documented that sentence comprehension, confrontational naming, verbal repetition, and syntactic complexity decline concurrently rather than in isolation. 37 , 38 Semantic paraphasias become prominent, and comprehension of complex multi-clause sentences diminishes. Cross-linguistic investigations indicate that this core deficit in lexical retrieval and semantic processing is universal across typologically diverse languages. Weekes examined linguistic breakdown in Chinese-speaking individuals with AD, confirming identical lexical retrieval failures alongside language-specific disruptions in tonal processing and ideographic character reading. 39 To address language-specific variations, international initiatives, such as the protocol outlined by Bose and colleagues, are establishing standardized methodologies to evaluate connected speech features in non-English speakers living with AD. 40 

Analysis of connected spontaneous discourse reveals communicative breakdowns that standardized single-word naming batteries fail to capture. In connected speech, narrative informativeness declines, cohesion between utterances deteriorates, and thematic maintenance is lost. Clarke and colleagues applied computational natural language processing (NLP) to conversational samples, demonstrating that automated lexical and syntactic discourse parameters quantify the loss of semantic content density and grammatical cohesion at scale. 19 In early AD discourse, individuals increasingly rely on stereotypical and formulaic speech patterns. Zimmerer and colleagues demonstrated that individuals with probable AD produce a significantly higher proportion of high-frequency formulaic expressions (such as “you know” or “at the end of the day”) as compensatory scaffolding when creative lexical retrieval falters. 41 Furthermore, qualitative discourse patterns contribute to differential diagnosis; Bouvet and colleagues demonstrated that narrative discourse metrics differentiate AD from DLB, where DLB patients exhibit distinct visual-perceptual descriptive deficits distinct from the lexical-semantic retrieval failures characteristic of AD. 42 Automated evaluation of pragmatic discourse features, including conversational topic management and communicative turn-taking, provides robust discrimination between AD and neurotypical aging. 43 Although primary motor speech impairments are uncommon in early typical AD, phonetic and phonological alterations can be identified. Cera and colleagues documented subtle acoustic changes, including prolonged voice onset times, articulation rate slowing, and occasional phonemic paraphasias, which distinguish motor speech planning deficits from primary language storage degradation. 22 

Receptive language is compromised when the auditory signal is acoustically degraded by background ambient noise, a common challenge in domestic and institutional settings. Jiang and colleagues demonstrated that individuals with AD and primary progressive aphasia exhibit marked deficits in comprehending speech presented in noisy environments, reflecting impaired auditory scene analysis and central auditory processing. 15 Hardy and colleagues established that individuals with AD and PPA retain a measurable capacity for perceptual learning of degraded speech through structured repetition, indicating that central auditory plasticity is partially preserved in neurodegenerative disease. 16 While AD typically manifests in older adulthood, atypical early-onset presentations occur; Borges and colleagues reported a case of early-onset AD presenting primarily with fluent aphasia, highlighting the diagnostic complexity of language-led presentations. 44 In the advanced and late stages of AD, expressive verbal output is reduced to isolated monosyllables, perseverations, echolalia, or complete mutism. At this stage, communication relies on non-verbal channels, where emotional prosody, therapeutic touch, and expressive modalities such as art therapy can facilitate meaningful engagement. 28 

The syndromic and biological distinction between typical amnestic AD and primary progressive aphasia variants is clinically critical. Primary progressive aphasia encompasses three consensus clinical syndromes: the nonfluent/agrammatic variant (nfvPPA), the semantic variant (svPPA), and the logopenic variant (lvPPA). 4 While nfvPPA and svPPA are typically underpinned by frontotemporal lobar degeneration tau or TDP-43 neuropathology, lvPPA is most commonly caused by underlying Alzheimer’s disease neuropathology. In a comprehensive multicenter cohort of 110 PPA patients, Kang and colleagues reported that 81% of individuals presenting with the lvPPA clinical phenotype demonstrated biological amyloid positivity on positron emission tomography (PET), and greater than 90% of amyloid-positive cases also harbored elevated phosphorylated tau biomarkers. 45 Similarly, Norise and colleagues confirmed in a cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarker cohort that 91% of PPA cases with an AD biomarker profile presented with the lvPPA clinical phenotype. 46 Clinically, lvPPA is characterized by impaired single-word retrieval and impaired sentence repetition due to phonological working memory decay, with relative sparing of single-word comprehension and motor grammar. In early stages, distinguishing lvPPA from typical early AD or non-AD aphasias can be challenging using standard pen-and-paper batteries alone; Da Cunha and colleagues demonstrated that automated acoustic analysis of sentence repetition tasks provides non-invasive discrimination between lvPPA and typical AD, offering diagnostic utility when lumbar puncture or PET imaging is inaccessible. 24 

## Assessment and Digital Biomarkers

## Automated Acoustic and Linguistic Processing

The most substantial methodological advance since 2015 is the evolution of speech analysis from qualitative perceptual descriptions to automated digital biomarkers. Spoken language represents a non-invasive, rich behavioral signal that reflects distributed neural networks governing auditory processing, executive search, semantic memory, and motor speech coordination. Using machine-learning algorithms and natural language processing, researchers extract hundreds of acoustic, temporal, and linguistic features from brief voice recordings. In a landmark study using audio recordings from 166 participants in the Framingham Heart Study, Amini and colleagues developed a language-model framework that combined acoustic and linguistic features with basic demographic variables (age, sex, education) to predict cognitive progression from MCI to AD dementia within 6 years with 78% accuracy and 81% sensitivity. 10 In an investigation of 200 participants, Fristed and colleagues demonstrated that an automated voice-based digital assessment platform not only distinguished individuals with cognitive impairment from healthy controls but also successfully detected biological amyloid-beta positivity confirmed by PET or CSF analysis. 31 Garcia-Gutiérrez and colleagues trained machine-learning classifiers on spontaneous speech recordings to grade cognitive impairment across the entire AD spectrum, achieving high classification performance. 47 

Longitudinal monitoring of progressive speech changes has been demonstrated by Robin and colleagues, who showed that automated acoustic metrics detect decline in early AD over 12 months before standard cognitive batteries register significant deterioration. 48 A systematic review by Vigo and colleagues synthesized speech- and language-based classification models, confirming that combining temporal acoustic features (such as pause durations, speech-to-pause ratios, and fundamental frequency variability) with lexical-semantic metrics achieves diagnostic accuracy ranging between 80% and 94% across experimental cohorts. 49 Automated speech metrics can also assist in differential diagnosis; Yamada and colleagues demonstrated that speech and language characteristics reliably differentiate AD from DLB based on syntactic complexity and acoustic pause structures. 50 Deep-learning architectures, including convolutional neural networks and recurrent models, have been developed to classify AD directly from raw speech waveforms without manual feature engineering. 51 Gosztolya and colleagues demonstrated that combining automatic speech recognition (ASR) with acoustic and linguistic feature extraction accurately identifies MCI and mild AD in spontaneous Hungarian speech. 52 Pan and colleagues introduced a time-frequency causal hidden Markov model that tracks longitudinal speech degradation in AD with high temporal sensitivity. 53 Martinc and colleagues demonstrated that temporal integration of text transcripts and acoustic features enhances classification robustness. 26 Deep language space neural networks, as demonstrated by Orimaye and colleagues, identify semantic and syntactic markers of mild cognitive impairment. 54 Laguarta and Subirana developed longitudinal speech models for automated detection, 55 while Shah and colleagues integrated eye-tracking kinematics with speech processing in a multimodal fusion network to optimize early detection. 56 Machine-learning pipelines developed by Tripathi and Kumar, 57 as well as paralinguistic acoustic models evaluated by Haider and colleagues, 58 confirm that acoustic pause frequency, duration of silent intervals, and jitter/shimmer perturbation parameters represent stable markers of cognitive-linguistic compromise.

## Remote Decentralized Assessment and Clinical Implementation

Digital speech assessment offers substantial advantages for decentralized clinical trials and remote community screening. The SIDE-AD longitudinal observational study protocol established by Saunders and colleagues is collecting spontaneous speech via remote web platforms and clinic visits to develop standardized digital progression markers. 59 Gregory and colleagues evaluated the feasibility and acceptability of remote speech data collection in individuals at elevated risk for AD, reporting high adherence, patient comfort, and data completion rates. 60 Addressing privacy concerns in continuous acoustic monitoring, Garrard and colleagues developed speech motif discovery algorithms that detect recurrent linguistic perseverations and pauses while masking identifiable phonetic content, preserving participant privacy. 20 Complementing conversational acoustic analysis, digital tablet-based cognitive instruments have modernized traditional confrontational naming tests. Seidman and colleagues evaluated a tablet-based picture-naming assessment in prodromal AD, showing that millisecond-level reaction times and naming latencies accurately differentiate individuals with MCI from cognitively intact older adults. 5 Wu and colleagues explored rapid automatized naming (RAN) and visual scanning tests as rapid prodromal screening instruments, demonstrating high diagnostic sensitivity. 61 Genomic investigations have begun elucidating the biological underpinnings of linguistic phenotypes; Deters and colleagues conducted a genome-wide association study (GWAS) of language performance in the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) cohort (n=1560), identifying novel genetic loci associated with composite language scores. 6 Qualitative communication behaviors, such as “saving appearance” conversational strategies wherein patients utilize evasive humor or superficial polite phrases to mask memory failures, have been characterized by Matsushita and colleagues. 62 Advanced mathematical signal processing, including higher-order spectral estimation of spontaneous speech signals developed by Nasrolahzadeh and colleagues, provides sensitive feature extraction in early disease. 63 

## Critical Appraisal of Digital Speech Biomarkers: Validity, Bias, and Ethics

Although digital speech biomarkers demonstrate high diagnostic performance in experimental studies, several methodological, demographic, and ethical limitations must be critically addressed before clinical deployment:

External validity and dataset representativeness: A substantial proportion of published machine-learning models rely on benchmark academic datasets, such as the Pitt Corpus within DementiaBank and the ADReSS (Alzheimer’s Dementia Recognition through Spontaneous Speech) challenge datasets. These datasets feature modest sample sizes, homogeneous socio-demographic backgrounds, and highly curated recordings, which introduces risks of algorithmic overfitting. When models are tested on unselected, real-world clinical cohorts, diagnostic accuracy often declines substantially.

Acoustic recording conditions: Controlled acoustic environments in sound-attenuated laboratory booths differ fundamentally from domestic home environments, where ambient background noise, reverberation, microphone hardware discrepancies, and telephone bandwidth compression (8 kHz vs 44.1 kHz) degrade acoustic feature extraction.

Speech elicitation task disparities: Performance varies substantially depending on the elicitation paradigm employed. Highly constrained tasks, such as the Cookie Theft picture description, restrict lexical variability and facilitate automated scoring, but they may fail to capture dynamic pragmatic discourse breakdown observed during unstructured conversational interactions.

Risk of data leakage: In automated acoustic analysis, segmenting audio files into brief audio chunks without strict speaker-level partitioning between training and test sets can lead to substantial data leakage, resulting in inflated performance estimates that do not generalize to unseen patients.

Cross-linguistic, educational, and demographic generalizability: Most speech biomarkers have been developed in English-speaking, highly educated cohorts. Speech rate, pause duration, lexical diversity, and syntactic complexity vary across languages, dialects, accents, cultural conversational norms, and baseline educational attainment. A model trained on standard English risks generating high false-positive rates when applied to non-native speakers or individuals with low literacy.

Cognitive impairment vs etiology detection: Differentiating generalized cognitive slowing from Alzheimer-specific pathology remains challenging. Slurred speech, prolonged pauses, and word-finding difficulty are present in depression, vascular dementia, frontotemporal lobar degeneration, and normal aging. Predicting biological amyloid or tau positivity from speech alone remains exploratory and cannot replace biofluid or PET confirmation.

Ethical and privacy considerations: Continuous passive voice monitoring in domestic settings raises complex ethical challenges concerning participant surveillance, data ownership, commercial monetization of biometric data, and informed consent in populations with progressive cognitive decline. Furthermore, communicating automated screening results risks psychological distress, particularly when false positives or uninterpretable risk probabilities are delivered without adequate clinical counseling.

## Social and Non-Pharmacological Interventions

## Structured Behavioral Speech-Language Therapy

In the 2015 review, non-pharmacological recommendations were largely restricted to general clinical advice. Over the past decade, structured behavioral interventions have been evaluated in small controlled and prospective clinical trials. Direct speech-language therapy aims to maintain communicative functioning, stimulate lexical retrieval pathways, and provide compensatory mechanisms. Wasik and colleagues evaluated a structured language-stimulation program in 88 individuals with mild and moderate AD (54 intervention participants and 34 controls) reassessed at 3-month intervals over one year. 12 Participants receiving structured language stimulation demonstrated statistically significant maintenance of verbal fluency scores relative to the control group, which exhibited progressive decline. In an independent controlled investigation, Potemkowski and colleagues confirmed that a systematic language stimulation program supported functional language maintenance in mild-to-moderate Alzheimer-type dementia. 13 Social engagement in adult day services has also been associated with functional maintenance; Honjo and colleagues observed that regular day-service utilization over 6 months was associated with significant improvements in verbal fluency and attention scores in community-dwelling AD patients. 14 Furthermore, addressing concurrent psychiatric symptoms is essential, as Yoon and colleagues demonstrated that active depressive symptoms exacerbate language impairment in AD, whereas treating depression partially improves linguistic performance. 64 

Confrontational naming therapy represents the most widely investigated behavioral language intervention. Semantic Feature Analysis (SFA) is a structured restorative technique in which patients systematically generate semantic attributes of target objects (eg, category, physical appearance, function, location) to strengthen semantic networks and facilitate lexical retrieval. Flanagan and colleagues conducted an investigation of SFA in individuals with mild AD, demonstrating that systematic semantic feature training improved naming accuracy for treated items. 7 To overcome geographic and mobility barriers, Zannino and colleagues evaluated a self-administered, computerized home-based anomia training program in mild AD, showing that autonomous digital practice yielded significant naming gains for trained vocabulary. 8 Computational network models have been applied to optimize item selection; Semiz and colleagues compared network model-based lexical selection against embodied cognition approaches, demonstrating that selecting target words based on semantic network centrality maximizes training efficiency. 65 

Despite positive findings, critical evaluation reveals a central limitation of restorative anomia therapy: therapeutic improvements are predominantly restricted to trained lexical items, with little evidence of spontaneous generalization to untrained words or everyday conversational discourse. Meyer and colleagues investigated naming interventions in PPA and AD, demonstrating that baseline conceptual-semantic impairment strongly predicts longitudinal treatment response; individuals with severe semantic loss exhibited minimal benefit, whereas individuals with preserved semantic representations showed sustained item recovery. 66 In comprehensive reviews of lexical retrieval interventions, Beales and colleagues emphasized that while item-specific re-learning is robust, generalization to untrained items and functional communicative participation is limited. 67 , 68 When apraxia of speech or motor programming deficits compound linguistic impairment, multimodal compensatory systems can support communication; Shin and colleagues demonstrated that unaided augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) utilizing structured gesture training improved communicative success in individuals with AD presenting with ideomotor apraxia. 25 

## Caregiver-Directed Communication Training

Because the majority of individuals with AD reside in community settings, family care partners represent the primary communicative environment. Communication breakdowns contribute substantially to caregiver burden, behavioral symptoms, and institutionalization. Structured caregiver communication training programs have transitioned from informal counseling to manualized, evidence-based interventions. Chesneau and colleagues evaluated the AID-COM program, a structured, communication-focused training intervention for family caregivers of individuals with early-stage AD. 17 Following three structured group sessions, caregivers exhibited statistically significant increases in their use of facilitative communication strategies, with corresponding reductions in perceived communication barriers. Vachon and colleagues investigated spontaneous communication strategies developed organically by family caregivers and nursing staff, documenting that patience, conversational flexibility, and non-confrontational validation are essential for preserving interactional harmony. 21 Caregiver acoustic prosody represents a powerful communicative tool; Rodero and colleagues evaluated prosodic modifications in caregiver-patient interactions, finding that caregivers identify modulated pitch, warm intonation, and reduced speech tempo as effective strategies to facilitate comprehension. 18 

Synthesizing evidence across clinical trials and observational studies, evidence-based caregiver communication recommendations include:

Simplify syntactic structure: Deliver short, grammatically direct sentences conveying one concept at a time; utilize closed or forced-choice questions (“Would you like tea or water?”) rather than open-ended queries (“What would you like to drink?”).

Modulate acoustic pace and prosody: Speak slowly with clear articulation and warm, reassuring vocal intonation, allowing an extended response latency (10–15 seconds) before repeating or rephrasing.

Optimize auditory environment: Minimize background acoustic noise (turn off televisions, radios, and household appliances) and maintain direct face-to-face visual contact to support comprehension.

Facilitate lexical retrieval non-punitively: Provide subtle phonetic or semantic prompts, point directly to target objects, or supply the forgotten word smoothly without quizzing, interrupting, or correcting.

Implement visual and external memory supports: Use written reminder cards, visual schedules, memory books, and labeled domestic items to support lexical retrieval and conversational orientation.

Leverage preserved emotional and social scripts: Utilize familiar social pleasantries, formulaic phrases, humor, and non-verbal touch, as automated social scripts and emotional comprehension remain preserved long after propositional syntax declines.

Digital resources provide accessible psychoeducation for care partners, although content quality varies; Jones and Parks analyzed 332 online caregiver resources, observing that while practical guidance is widely available, evidence-based communication training remains inconsistently integrated. 69 Targeted psychological interventions support caregiver resilience; Ghezelsefloo and colleagues demonstrated that resilience training for primary caregivers significantly reduced perceived caregiving stress and improved dyadic communication. 27 Warren highlighted the clinical importance of caregiver awareness of preserved consciousness and emotional receptivity, noting that understanding retained emotional processing fosters compassionate communication. 70 However, implementation gaps persist in clinical practice; Lanzi and colleagues surveyed speech-language pathologists, finding that clinical services for individuals with MCI and early-stage dementia remain underutilized and inconsistent. 71 A systematic review by Dimitriou and colleagues concluded that structured behavioral and dyadic communication interventions provide modest but statistically significant benefits for communication efficiency and psychological well-being. 72 Expressive creative arts provide valuable adjunctive modalities: López-Méndez and colleagues demonstrated that graphic art activities foster communication in early dementia, 29 Karam and colleagues showed that music-based interventions improve speech-related behavioral outcomes, 30 and Parlak and colleagues developed mobile software tailored for cognitive-communication rehabilitation in AD. 73 

## Medical Treatment

## Overview and Current Clinical Context

A critical appraisal of pharmacological and medical interventions requires clarity regarding regulatory indications and therapeutic targets. To date, no pharmacological agent is approved specifically for the symptomatic treatment or reversal of language impairment in Alzheimer’s disease. Approved medications target global cognitive decline or underlying neuropathological cascades, with language evaluated as an exploratory, secondary, or composite cognitive outcome. What has evolved substantially since 2015 is the planned incorporation of dedicated linguistic outcome measures into clinical trials, neuropathological delineation of cholinergic loss in language networks, the introduction of disease-modifying anti-amyloid monoclonal antibodies, and the application of non-invasive brain stimulation.

## Cholinesterase Inhibitors: Donepezil

Acetylcholinesterase inhibitors (donepezil, galantamine, rivastigmine) represent the standard symptomatic pharmacotherapy for mild to moderate AD, enhancing cholinergic neurotransmission across neocortical circuits. Standard clinical dosing is established: donepezil is initiated at 5 mg daily and titrated to 10 mg daily after 4–6 weeks. While large pivotal trials established modest improvements on global cognitive batteries (such as the Alzheimer’s Disease Assessment Scale-Cognitive Subscale [ADAS-Cog]), language subitems were rarely examined independently. Hegedus and colleagues conducted a 24-week naturalistic, open-label, self-controlled pilot study evaluating acoustic speech parameters in 20 patients with mild AD receiving donepezil (10 mg daily). 23 Using automatic speech recognition and the Speech-Gap Test, fifteen acoustic temporal parameters were tracked longitudinally. At 12 weeks, the filled-pause duration ratio improved significantly, whereas global ADAS-Cog scores demonstrated only a minor, non-significant change. These findings must be interpreted with caution: this small, uncontrolled pilot study does not demonstrate that donepezil restores language function. Rather, it indicates that automated temporal speech parameters may offer greater sensitivity to pharmacodynamic fluctuations than conventional composite neuropsychological scales.

## N-Methyl-D-Aspartate Receptor Antagonists: Memantine and the ROMEO-AD Trial

Memantine, an uncompetitive N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor antagonist, is indicated for moderate to severe AD (maintenance dosing 20 mg daily, titrated in 5 mg weekly increments). The most rigorous trial directly investigating language outcomes retrieved in our search is the ROMEO-AD trial conducted by Kim and colleagues. 32 ROMEO-AD was a multicenter, randomized, open-label, prospective 24-week clinical trial designed to estimate the add-on effects of memantine oral solution on language function in patients with moderate to severe AD already maintained on stable donepezil monotherapy. A total of 188 patients were randomized, and 164 completed the study. The primary efficacy endpoint was change on the Korean version of the Western Aphasia Battery (K-WAB) Aphasia Quotient. Over 24 weeks, both the add-on memantine group and the donepezil monotherapy group demonstrated slight improvements on K-WAB language scores; crucially, however, the between-group difference was not statistically significant. Add-on memantine did not provide statistically significant language benefit over donepezil alone. The significance of the ROMEO-AD trial lies not in demonstrating efficacy, but in establishing a precedent for designating standardized aphasia batteries as primary efficacy endpoints in advanced dementia trials.

## Neuropathology of Cholinergic Innervation in Language-Predominant AD

Post-mortem neuropathological investigations provide an anatomical and neurochemical rationale for cholinergic pharmacotherapy in language-predominant AD phenotypes. In an analysis of 36 autopsied brains from patients with primary progressive aphasia, Mesulam and colleagues demonstrated that cases with underlying Alzheimer neuropathology (PPA-AD, predominantly lvPPA) exhibited severe loss of basal forebrain cholinergic neurons and profound cortical cholinergic axonal denervation. 74 Crucially, cholinergic denervation was asymmetrical, being significantly more severe in the language-dominant left hemisphere and localized to language-affiliated perisylvian temporal and parietal cortices, whereas non-AD PPA variants (FTLD-tau or FTLD-TDP) showed intact cholinergic innervation. In a quantitative stereological analysis of the nucleus basalis of Meynert across 47 autopsied cases, Schaeverbeke and colleagues confirmed that cholinergic neuronal degeneration tracked directly with the severity of Alzheimer neuropathological changes (Braak neurofibrillary stages and amyloid plaque burden) rather than with the clinical aphasic phenotype per se. 75 These neuropathological findings demonstrate that cholinergic loss directly underlies language network degeneration in AD-associated aphasia, providing a biological rationale for cholinesterase inhibitor therapy in biomarker-positive lvPPA, though clinical trial evidence of functional efficacy in lvPPA remains lacking.

## Disease-Modifying Anti-Amyloid Monoclonal Antibodies: Eligibility vs Efficacy

The regulatory approval of monoclonal antibodies directed against amyloid-beta represents a major development in Alzheimer’s therapeutics. Because lvPPA is primarily driven by Alzheimer neuropathology, patients presenting with language-predominant symptoms are biologically eligible candidates for these disease-modifying therapies. Inagawa and colleagues evaluated eligibility for anti-amyloid therapies in a prospective cohort of 13 PPA patients in Japan using MRI, SPECT, CSF analytes, and amyloid-PET. 33 Among 7 patients diagnosed with lvPPA, 6 (86%) were amyloid-positive, and 5 (71%) satisfied all Japanese appropriate use criteria for lecanemab or donanemab; the single biomarker-negative patient was rediagnosed as frontotemporal lobar degeneration. These findings align with broader multicenter biomarker cohorts: Kang and colleagues observed 81% amyloid positivity in lvPPA, 45 and Norise and colleagues reported 91% AD-biomarker concordance in lvPPA. 46 Monoclonal antibodies targeting amyloid-beta, including lecanemab (which targets soluble protofibrils in the Clarity AD trial, N=1795) 76 and donanemab (which targets plaque amyloid in the TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2 trial, N=1736), 77 have demonstrated statistically significant slowing of clinical decline across early symptomatic Alzheimer cohorts.

However, a crucial clinical distinction must be emphasized between treatment eligibility and demonstrated therapeutic efficacy. In the pivotal Phase 3 trials of lecanemab and donanemab, 76 , 77 primary and secondary outcomes were global cognitive-functional composites, specifically the Clinical Dementia Rating-Sum of Boxes (CDR-SB), the Alzheimer’s Disease Assessment Scale-Cognitive Subscale (ADAS-Cog14), and the integrated Alzheimer’s Disease Rating Scale (iADRS). Neither trial incorporated dedicated primary or secondary speech-language outcome measures (such as confrontational naming, discourse cohesion, or auditory sentence comprehension). Consequently, there is currently no direct empirical evidence establishing that lecanemab or donanemab improves, restores, or specifically slows the decline of language impairment in AD or lvPPA. Patients with lvPPA who meet biomarker criteria are eligible to receive anti-amyloid therapy under standard indications, but therapeutic expectations must be carefully managed. Furthermore, clinical implementation requires biomarker confirmation (PET or lumbar puncture), routine MRI surveillance for amyloid-related imaging abnormalities (ARIA), and consideration of healthcare access and equity.

## Non-Invasive Brain Stimulation and Acoustic Neuromodulation

Non-invasive brain stimulation (NIBS) techniques, including repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS), transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS), transcranial alternating current stimulation (tACS), transcranial random noise stimulation (tRNS), and non-invasive vagus nerve stimulation (nVNS), have been investigated to modulate neuroplasticity in language and cognitive networks. LoBue and colleagues published a critical review evaluating these five NIBS technologies in Alzheimer’s dementia and PPA, observing that while numerous small trials of rTMS and tDCS report transient improvements in cognitive or naming performance, the clinical meaningfulness of these changes remains uncertain due to methodological limitations. 78 Georgopoulou and colleagues conducted a systematic review pooling 9 studies and 290 participants (190 active, 100 control) evaluating rTMS combined with cognitive and language training, reporting improvements in global cognition and neuropsychiatric symptoms while noting that protocols rarely included dedicated language measures. 79 Chen and colleagues reviewed neuromodulation interventions for language deficits in AD, concluding that although cortical stimulation targeting the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) or perisylvian language cortex is biologically plausible, a substantial translational gap remains. 80 

Investigating neurofunctional mechanisms, Zhao and colleagues conducted a randomized, double-blind, sham-controlled study combining 4 weeks of bilateral rTMS with functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) during a verbal fluency task in 33 patients with AD and depression (17 active, 16 sham). 34 Active rTMS significantly improved depression scores and reduced hyperactivation in the right prefrontal cortex during the verbal fluency task, suggesting more efficient neural recruitment, although Mini-Mental State Examination scores were unchanged. Beyond electrical and magnetic stimulation, acoustic neuromodulation using transcranial pulse stimulation (TPS), which delivers focused acoustic shockwaves, has recently emerged. In an open-label, single-arm pilot study of 32 AD patients, Wiechmann and colleagues utilized connected-speech digital biomarkers to monitor TPS response. 35 A machine-learning Speech Composite Index demonstrated relative stabilization of speech trajectories over treatment, though individual patient responses varied widely. Crucially, neuromodulation studies in AD are largely constrained by small sample sizes, open-label designs, and lack of active sham controls. In unblinded single-arm studies, observed linguistic improvements may reflect practice effects, regression to the mean, or increased non-specific social interaction rather than true neurobiological neuromodulation. Rigorous, double-blind, sham-controlled randomized trials with standardized language outcomes are required before clinical implementation can be supported.

## Discussion

## Synthesis of Findings Across Review Questions

This narrative review synthesized peer-reviewed evidence published from 2016 through 2026 addressing language impairment in Alzheimer’s disease across digital assessment, behavioral therapy, and medical management, answering the four predefined review questions:

In response to RQ1 (linguistic manifestations and phenotypes), evidence demonstrates that language breakdown is characterized by early semantic access and discourse cohesion deficits in typical AD, 9 , 19 whereas lvPPA presents with prominent phonological short-term memory deficits and sentence repetition breakdown. 4 , 24 Biological biomarker studies confirm that over 80% to 90% of individuals with clinical lvPPA harbor underlying Alzheimer neuropathology (amyloid-beta and hyperphosphorylated tau), 45 , 46 reinforcing the necessity of biomarker verification to differentiate AD-driven aphasias from frontotemporal lobar degeneration variants.

In response to RQ2 (digital speech biomarkers), automated acoustic and natural language processing models demonstrate high diagnostic accuracy (75–94%) for detecting cognitive impairment and biological amyloid positivity in controlled corpora. 10 , 31 , 49 However, their clinical translation is constrained by dataset bias, acoustic sensitivity, lack of external validation across diverse dialects and literacy levels, and substantial ethical concerns regarding ambient voice surveillance and data privacy. 20 

In response to RQ3 (behavioral and caregiver interventions), structured language stimulation and semantic feature naming therapy measurably maintain verbal fluency and recover treated vocabulary. 7 , 8 , 12 Nevertheless, therapeutic gains are largely item-specific, with minimal transfer to untrained words or everyday conversational discourse. 67 , 68 In contrast, caregiver communication training (eg, the AID-COM protocol) reliably enhances dyadic interactions, reduces communication breakdowns, and mitigates caregiver distress. 17 , 18 

In response to RQ4 (medical treatments and neuromodulation), no currently approved drug restores language function. In the randomized ROMEO-AD trial, add-on memantine provided no statistically significant language benefit over donepezil alone. 32 Anti-amyloid monoclonal antibodies (lecanemab, donanemab) represent biologically targeted therapies for which lvPPA patients frequently meet eligibility criteria, 33 but pivotal Phase 3 trials lacked dedicated primary speech endpoints, meaning clinical efficacy in preserving or restoring language remains unproven. 76 , 77 Neuromodulation approaches remain exploratory and require rigorous sham-controlled evaluation. 34 , 78 

## Integration into a Clinical Pathway for Language Preservation

Rather than treating assessment, behavioral interventions, and medical therapies as isolated silos, clinical practice benefits from an integrated care pathway. In this model, automated digital speech tools could serve as sensitive, low-burden screening instruments in primary care to identify subtle conversational alterations. Detection of linguistic impairment should trigger formal multidisciplinary evaluation, including detailed neuropsychological and speech-language assessments alongside in vivo biomarker confirmation (CSF or PET) to establish biological etiology. When AD pathology is confirmed, a stage-matched management plan should be initiated: (1) baseline semantic profiling to guide targeted anomia therapy and language stimulation; (2) referral of family care partners to manualized communication training programs (such as AID-COM) to optimize conversational strategies and modify acoustic environments; (3) consideration of disease-modifying anti-amyloid therapy in eligible early-stage patients, with realistic counseling regarding expected global slowing rather than language restoration; and (4) structured longitudinal monitoring using standardized speech outcome measures.

## Standardized Language Endpoints in Disease-Modifying Clinical Trials

A critical priority for the next generation of Alzheimer’s clinical trials is the development and validation of standardized, psychometrically robust speech and language endpoints. In a comprehensive review of outcome measures in AD and Parkinson’s disease trials, Berthier and colleagues emphasized that conventional composite cognitive batteries (such as ADAS-Cog or MMSE) are insufficiently granular to detect subtle changes in communicative competence. 81 Similarly, Wright and colleagues emphasized that verbal fluency tasks reflect complex interactions between semantic storage, executive switching, and processing speed that require standardized scoring rubrics. 11 Establishing harmonized digital speech protocols, validated across multiple languages and clinical sites, will allow disease-modifying trials to determine whether biological clearance of amyloid-beta or tau tangles genuinely preserves everyday human communication.

## Strengths and Limitations of the Review

The primary strength of this review is its broad synthesis bridging computational linguistics, behavioral speech-language therapy, and clinical pharmacology across a 10-year literature window. However, several methodological limitations must be acknowledged. First, as a narrative review, studies were synthesized qualitatively rather than pooled statistically, reflecting the substantial heterogeneity of study designs and outcome measures. Second, the search strategy was conducted exclusively within the Web of Science Core Collection and restricted AD and linguistic terms to the title field. While this strategy successfully captured studies where language was a primary titular focus, it may have omitted broader dementia trials where language tests were reported only as secondary exploratory endpoints in the full text. Third, the review was restricted to peer-reviewed articles published in English, which may have excluded relevant investigations published in other languages.

## Conclusion and Future Directions

Substantial progress has occurred in the study of language impairment in Alzheimer’s disease between 2016 and 2026. Language and speech have evolved from descriptive clinical symptoms into objective, automated digital biomarkers capable of quantifying disease progression and biological amyloid status. Behavioral interventions have matured from general advice into structured, protocolized programs; however, clinical expectations must remain balanced: restorative naming therapies achieve task-specific improvements that rarely generalize to spontaneous conversation, while caregiver communication training provides robust support for dyadic communication and care partner well-being. In pharmacotherapy, approved symptomatic drugs and newly developed disease-modifying anti-amyloid antibodies do not directly restore language, and their efficacy in preserving communication remains to be demonstrated in trials featuring dedicated linguistic endpoints. Clinicians and researchers must differentiate early detection, task-specific skill maintenance, everyday functional communication, neuropathological modification, and patient quality of life. The clear next step for the field is the execution of adequately powered, sham-controlled neuromodulation trials and the systematic incorporation of standardized, cross-linguistically validated speech and language endpoints into disease-modifying clinical trials.
