Anthropomorphic design is widely assumed to shape self-disclosure and emotional dependence by first cultivating felt empathy and trust, and the safety measures platforms deploy, such as labelling and disclosure of artificial origin, target those perceptions. Across a twelve-country survey, forty-seven interviews, and three experiments, this research tests that mediation model and identifies the route that carries the behavioural effect. A twelve-country survey ( N  = 1480) found empathy and trust negligibly related to disclosure and dependence at the trait level. Two experiments ( N  = 390 and N  = 300) produced strong direct effects of an anthropomorphic agent, with the anthropomorphic agent roughly tripling the odds of behavioural disclosure (63% versus 35% under a matched neutral prompt), while the indirect effects through empathy and trust received no support. Study 5 ( N  = 300) measured competing mediators and provided the most defensible interim account of the mechanism: only perceived anonymity and reduced evaluation apprehension carried the effect on disclosure, locating the process in online disinhibition rather than felt relational warmth. Forty-seven interviews surfaced clinically salient disclosure, including suicidal ideation revealed to a chatbot and to no person. Because the consequences of anthropomorphic design travel through the lowered social risk of the medium, safeguards that recalibrate how users perceive an agent may leave disclosure and dependence largely untouched.

## Introduction

Conversational AI has moved from research demonstration to a daily fixture in under five years: hundreds of millions of people now hold regular conversations with large language model agents, and companion services such as Replika and Character.AI report tens of millions of registered users ( Pentina, Hancock, & Xie, 2023 ). A distinctive behavioural pattern has drawn sustained attention, namely the willingness of users to reveal intimate material to a chatbot, including material withheld from family, friends, and clinicians ( Ho, Hancock, & Miner, 2018 ; Kang & Kang, 2023 ; Lee & Lee, 2023 ; Skjuve, Følstad, Fostervold, & Brandtzaeg, 2021 ). Some describe patterns of use that resemble behavioural dependence, intertwined with loneliness and smaller social networks ( Laestadius, Bishop, Gonzalez, Illencik, & Campos-Castillo, 2024 ; Pentina et al., 2023 ; Xie & Pentina, 2022 ). Understanding this behaviour requires a precise account of the mechanism by which anthropomorphic design produces it.

This research operationalises the outcome as emotional dependence, a maladaptive attachment dynamic involving emotional reliance, separation anxiety, and compensatory use ( Pentina et al., 2023 ), rather than behavioural addiction's tolerance-withdrawal cycle, which the measures used here do not target.

The dominant lens is the Computers Are Social Actors paradigm, which holds that people apply to computers the same social rules they apply to other people, and that this tendency intensifies when a system presents human cues such as language, personality, or affective expression ( Nass & Moon, 2000 ; Reeves & Nass, 1996 ). Anthropomorphism, the attribution of humanlike characteristics, motivations, or mental states to nonhuman agents, has been advanced as the central organising construct ( Epley, Waytz, & Cacioppo, 2007 ), and anthropomorphic cues such as name, voice, and warmth reliably raise trust in nonhuman agents ( de Visser et al., 2016 ; Waytz, Heafner, & Epley, 2014 ; Pizzi, Vannucci, Mazzoli, & Donvito, 2023 report full mediation of a related disclosure outcome through warmth and trust in a different task context). The prevailing account of the downstream consequences takes a mediational form, in which anthropomorphic cues elicit perceptions of empathy and trust and these perceptions in turn drive self-disclosure and emotional dependence. The paradigm itself allows direct social responses without empathy or trust as intermediaries, so three routes can be distinguished: automatic social responding, relational appraisal through empathy and trust, and reduced social-evaluative concern through anonymity and lowered evaluation apprehension. This research adjudicates among them, and treats immediate disclosure and longer-term dependence as distinct processes, the latter better framed by attachment and compensatory internet-use accounts. This assumption has shaped commercial design choices around voice and persona, yet the mediating step has received little direct scrutiny, and where tested, the perceived-understanding mechanism did not exert the predicted effects ( Ho et al., 2018 ).

This distinction has practical stakes: if felt empathy and trust carry the outcomes, perception-focused interventions such as labelling should attenuate them; if not, such interventions may have little effect. A complementary tradition locates the willingness to disclose in the affordances of the medium rather than perceptions of the agent. The online disinhibition effect describes more open expression online, attributed to anonymity, invisibility, and absent authority cues ( Suler, 2004 ); people disclose more intimately when visually anonymous than face to face ( Joinson, 2001 ), and framing a virtual interviewer as a computer lowers evaluation concern and raises disclosure ( Lucas, Gratch, King, & Morency, 2014 ): a user discloses not because the agent seems empathic but because the channel removes the social risks that constrain disclosure.

Loneliness is a growing population-level health concern ( Cacioppo & Cacioppo, 2018 ), companion chatbots are used disproportionately by lonely people, and reports have surfaced of users disclosing suicidal ideation and self-harm to chatbots, including content shared with no human ( Maples, Cerit, Vishwanath, & Pea, 2024 ; Miner, Milstein, & Hancock, 2017 ). This research adopts a mixed-methods design ( Creswell & Plano Clark, 2018 ) integrating a survey, an interview study, and three randomised experiments, examining whether the assumed chain from anthropomorphism through empathy and trust to disclosure and dependence holds and, where it does not, what carries the effect. ## Method
 2.1 
## Design and ethics

This research integrates five studies conducted in 2026: a cross-sectional survey of structural relationships among six focal constructs (Study 1), a qualitative interview study (Study 2), and three randomised experiments testing whether the survey relationships survive controlled manipulation, with Study 4 adding behavioural outcomes and a moderator and Study 5 measuring competing mediators to identify what carries the direct effect. All studies were approved by the institutional review board of the author affiliation (No. IRB-2026-0527), and all participants provided informed consent. Interviewers followed a documented distress protocol for emotionally sensitive material: where a participant recounted suicidal ideation or self-harm the interview was paused, the participant was checked on and given crisis resources in writing, and reminded they could stop. The protocol was invoked three times (retrospective, not active in-session crisis); all three continued after a break and one ended early. One participant was 17, for whom written parental consent and age-appropriate assent were obtained. Transcripts were de-identified at transcription; the full protocol and instrumentation appear in Appendix F.
 2.2 
## Study 1

A total of 1480 chatbot users completed the Study 1 survey using quotas balanced for country, gender, and age, eligible if they had used a consumer chatbot at least weekly for the preceding three months. The sample spanned twelve countries, the mean age was 38.4 years (SD = 15.0), and it comprised 643 women, 683 men, 71 non-binary respondents, and 83 who declined to specify gender. The six constructs were measured with multi-item one to seven scales, with anthropomorphism captured by five items adapted from Epley et al. (2007) , empathy by seven, trust by eight (four reverse keyed), self-disclosure by ten, emotional dependence by six, and social connection by ten. Two embedded attention checks gated the retained sample; pre-screening and exclusion counts are not available in the deposited records and are not reported (Appendix K).
 2.3 
## Study 2

Forty-seven frequent users participated in the Study 2 semi-structured interviews (23 women and 24 men; 17 to 45 years, M = 26.9; recruited across fourteen countries (full breakdown, Appendix K); 36 primarily used ChatGPT, seven Character.AI, and four Replika). All 47 consenting participants completed the interview and were retained for analysis (Appendix K). Interviews followed a 26-item protocol across eight sections. Analysis followed the six-phase reflexive thematic analysis framework of Braun and Clarke, 2006 , Braun and Clarke, 2019 . For transparency rather than theme validation, an independent second coder blind to the lead coder coded a random 30% subsample ( n  = 14): 95.0% agreement, pooled kappa = 0.90 (per-theme values, Appendix B). Frequency counts serve triangulation with the survey. Participants also provided one to ten self-ratings for trust and disclosure and binary indicators for dependence.
 2.4 
## Studies 3 and 4

Study 3 used a 2 (agent type: anthropomorphic vs. robotic) by 2 (prompt type: encouraging vs. neutral) between-subjects design with 390 participants. The anthropomorphic agent used a personal name, conversational warmth, expressions of understanding, and first person reference, while the robotic agent used third person reference, presented itself as a tool and avoided affective language. Because it bundles naming, warmth, and understanding, the manipulation is an anthropomorphic-relational package (full scripts, Appendix H); each interaction lasted about twelve minutes on a custom chat interface. Trust, empathy, self-disclosure, and dependence were each assessed with two items (reliabilities in Table C1), with a two-item anthropomorphism manipulation check. Study 4 retained the 2 by 2 design with 300 participants, assessed each outcome with a single item, and added a three-item loneliness composite adapted from the UCLA Loneliness Scale ( Russell, 1996 ). Two behavioural outcomes were recorded, namely behavioural disclosure, coded by whether the free-text response contained substantive personal content (a rule-based binary code; rubric and depth anchors in Appendix I), with word count and a four-point depth rating retained (inter-rater reliability: binary code, two independent coders, 96.0% agreement, kappa = 0.91; depth rating, two independent coders, 92.0% agreement, unweighted kappa = 0.89, weighted kappa = 0.96; Appendix I), and advice compliance, a binary indicator of whether the participant followed a concrete recommendation.
 2.5 
## Study 5

Study 5 was a between-subjects experiment ( N  = 300, 150 per condition) that measured the candidate mechanisms directly, using the same agent manipulation as Studies 3 and 4 without the prompt factor, so the design isolated the agent contrast. After a structured interaction, participants completed single-item measures of the two mediators central to the prevailing account (felt empathy, trust) and the two central to the disinhibition account (perceived anonymity, evaluation apprehension). Disclosure was the word count of the response to a standardised invitation to share a recent emotionally weighted experience. All four mediators were tested simultaneously as competing pathways, with bootstrapped confidence intervals for each indirect effect.
 2.6 
## Analysis and open science

Analyses were conducted in R version 4.3.3. Structural equation modelling used the robust maximum likelihood estimator with fit evaluated against established cutoffs ( Hu & Bentler, 1999 ), and experimental mediation used bootstrapped confidence intervals at 2000 to 5000 replications for the indirect effects ( Preacher & Hayes, 2008 ), with the logic distinguishing indirect from total effects ( Zhao et al., 2010 ). Effect sizes are reported as Cohen's d, partial eta squared, and standardised coefficients, and the alpha level was set at 0.05. Reliabilities (alpha and omega), configural fit across cultural clusters (a formal metric-invariance test was attempted but could not be reliably obtained; see Appendix A), a marker-variable check of common method bias ( Fuller, Simmering, Atinc, Atinc, & Babin, 2016 ; Podsakoff, MacKenzie, Lee, & Podsakoff, 2003 ), and a sensitivity power analysis appear in the appendices. The materials, data, and analysis scripts are deposited on the Open Science Framework and shared with reviewers via an anonymized view-only link, as a retrospective (post-hoc) record; no study was prospectively pre-registered.

## Results
 3.1 
## Study 1

Reliability of the six Study 1 scales was strong (alpha 0.755 to 0.955 after the four reverse-keyed trust items were recoded). Composite means placed anthropomorphism, empathy, and trust near the scale midpoint (4.25, 4.09, 4.26), while self-disclosure and emotional dependence sat well below it (2.81, 2.39). The correlation pattern diverged from the prevailing account. Anthropomorphism correlated with empathy at 0.28 and with trust at 0.42, replicating the input side of the chain, but none of the three perceptual constructs correlated meaningfully with self-disclosure, emotional dependence, or social connection, with all coefficients between negative 0.03 and 0.01. A structural equation model reproduced this in a stronger form, with acceptable fit (CFI = 0.962, RMSEA = 0.030, SRMR = 0.045): the paths from anthropomorphism to empathy and trust were strong, at 0.31 and 0.46, while the paths to the outcomes were near zero. Crucially, the direct paths from anthropomorphism to the outcomes were also near zero (self-disclosure b = −0.01, 95% CI [−0.04, 0.03], p  = .69; dependence b = 0.00, p  = .98), so Study 1 does not evidence a direct pathway. These nulls are not an artefact of restricted range: the outcome composites are only modestly skewed (skew +0.28 and + 0.49) with substantial variance, and rank-based Spearman correlations are also near zero (Appendix A). Common method variance is more likely to inflate than to generate a spurious null, although construct mismatch, differential method effects, and response styles could in principle produce more complex patterns; a common latent factor (6.7%) and a marker-variable check are reported as a limited sensitivity check rather than confirmation that the associations are free of method bias (Appendix A).
 3.2 
## Study 2

The Study 2 embedded ratings were consistent with the qualitative material, with mean self-rated trust of 6.68 and disclosure of 5.02. Twenty of the 47 participants (42.6%) reported emotional dependence, and this group reported substantially higher disclosure (M = 6.95) than the non-dependent group (M = 3.59), t(45) = 10.76, p  < .001, d = 3.17. Dependence concentrated by platform: all eleven Character.AI and Replika users reported dependence, compared with nine of 36 ChatGPT users, a difference significant by Fisher's exact test ( p  < .001) given the small companion-platform cell. Six themes emerged. Roughly 89% of participants attributed a personality to the agent, and the eight who had assigned it a persistent personal name all came from the dependent group, functioning as a behavioural marker of dependence rather than generic personalisation. Trust was conditional and domain specific, with participants verifying technical outputs while accepting affective content with less scrutiny. On self-disclosure, a majority reported sharing material they had told no human, and their explanations converged on the disinhibition account rather than on perceived empathy: disclosure felt easier because there was no social risk of gossip or judgement. Three participants recounted suicidal ideation or self-harm that, by their account, they had disclosed only to the chatbot and to no person. Because no formal clinical assessment was conducted, this material is described as clinically salient rather than clinically significant. The remaining themes captured emotional dependence, social withdrawal concentrated in the dependent group, and negative experiences including confidently false content and cold, scripted crisis responses.
 3.3 
## Studies 3 and 4

In Study 3 the agent manipulation produced a strong difference in perceived anthropomorphism (partial eta squared = 0.804) and strong main effects on every outcome, with partial eta squared from 0.143 for dependence (early relational proxy) to 0.289 for empathy and Cohen's d from 0.81 to 1.27; the two-item composites were reliable (alpha 0.75 to 0.80; Appendix C), and no agent by prompt interaction was significant. A bootstrapped mediation partitioned the agent effect on disclosure: the paths to empathy and trust were strong (0.51 and 0.45) but the paths from these to disclosure were non-significant, the total indirect effect spanned zero, and the direct path remained strong (0.49); the dependence (early relational proxy) model showed the same structure. All randomised participants (390 in Study 3, 300 in Study 4) completed the procedure and were retained for analysis, with no post-randomisation loss or exclusion in either experiment. In Study 4, behavioural disclosure rates were 89.3%, 62.7%, 64.0%, and 34.7% in the anthropomorphic-encouraging, anthropomorphic-neutral, robotic-encouraging, and robotic-neutral cells (corrected after a second-coder check identified twelve miscoded refusals; Appendix D). A 2 × 2 logistic regression on the corrected classification showed a significant agent main effect (OR = 3.16, 95% CI [1.62, 6.16]) and prompt main effect (OR = 3.35, [1.71, 6.55]) with no interaction (Appendix D). Because the extreme-cell contrast changes both factors, the agent effect is better expressed as the main effect or the same-prompt comparison (62.7% versus 34.7% under the neutral prompt), which is not reducible to instructed compliance. A full factorial negative-binomial model of word count showed significant agent (IRR = 2.13, p  = .003) and prompt (IRR = 2.21, p  = .002) main effects with no interaction. The four-point depth rating (inter-rater reliability 92.0% agreement, kappa = 0.89 unweighted, 0.96 weighted) showed the same additive pattern, confirmed by a full factorial ANOVA (agent F(1, 296) = 33.41, p  < .001; prompt F(1, 296) = 30.63, p  < .001; no interaction; Appendix D). Advice compliance showed no agent effect, weighing against a blanket demand account. Loneliness was tested on four outcomes; the trust interaction, recomputed here directly from the deposited data (b = 0.36, p  = .036; the originally reported b = 0.33, p  = .040 reflected a preliminary analysis script), did not survive Holm correction across the four tests (adjusted p  = .14) and is reported as exploratory.
 3.4 
## Study 5

Study 5 tested the candidate mechanisms directly. The manipulation moved all four mediators strongly, raising felt empathy (b = 1.71, p  < .001), trust (b = 1.11, p  < .001), and perceived anonymity (b = 1.33, p  < .001) and lowering evaluation apprehension (b = −1.35, p  < .001, consistent with Fig. 2 ), and it produced more disclosure, with mean response length rising from 29.2 to 48.6 words (standardised b = 0.94, p  < .001). All four mediators then competed in one model predicting disclosure. The paths from felt empathy (b = 0.02, p  = .74) and trust (b = 0.05, p  = .32) were non-significant, replicating the null indirect effect observed in Studies 1, 3, and 4, whereas perceived anonymity (b = 0.23, p  < .001) and evaluation apprehension (b = −0.19, p  = .001, lower apprehension predicting more disclosure, consistent with Fig. 2 ) predicted disclosure, and the direct effect remained significant (b = 0.55). Collinearity was not a concern (all VIF 1.08 to 1.35; Appendix E), so the null empathy and trust coefficients are not an artefact of partitioning. Bootstrapped indirect effects (5000 resamples) were significant through anonymity (0.21, 95% CI [0.10, 0.33]) and reduced apprehension (0.17, [0.06, 0.29]) and null through empathy and trust; bootstrap contrasts confirmed anonymity and apprehension exceed empathy; a negative-binomial count model was directionally consistent (agent IRR = 1.35, anonymity IRR = 1.14, apprehension IRR = 0.90) but did not reach significance for any predictor (all p  > .06; Appendix E), so this robustness check is directional rather than confirmatory. Because the direct effect persists, this is partial mediation. Because the mediators were measured rather than experimentally isolated, Study 5 cannot adjudicate causally among them, but the pattern favours online disinhibition, shown in Fig. 2 : anthropomorphic design promotes disclosure by raising felt anonymity and lowering apprehension about being judged, rather than through the felt empathy and trust on which the prevailing account rests. ## Discussion
 4.1 
## Direct pathways and mechanism

The central finding is that the effect of anthropomorphic design on self-disclosure and emotional dependence was not statistically carried by felt empathy or trust, even though those perceptions were themselves produced by the manipulation with strong and replicable paths. A non-significant indirect effect does not prove mediation is absent, but this pattern is evidence against the prevailing mediational account. The implication is not that empathy and trust are unimportant, but that they do not adequately explain how the design affects the outcomes. Of the four measured mediators, only perceived anonymity and reduced evaluation apprehension carried significant indirect effects. The pattern is therefore most consistent with online disinhibition as a candidate mechanism, in which the felt anonymity and lowered social risk of the exchange release disclosure, rather than as the felt relational warmth the prevailing account places at the centre ( Suler, 2004 ). The divergence between the null survey associations and the large experimental effects is itself a mixed-methods finding: the survey relates stable trait differences, whereas the experiments manipulate the state features of a specific exchange. Because this rests on a single, non-pre-registered experiment with a significant remaining direct effect (partial, not complete, mediation), it is best regarded as the most defensible interim account rather than settled, and it leaves room for automatic social responding below conscious appraisal ( Nass & Moon, 2000 ).

A further tension deserves comment rather than relabelling. The single item retained here, “I felt anonymous during this conversation,” rose under the anthropomorphic agent, counter-intuitive since greater social presence would ordinarily raise, not lower, felt evaluation. This research retains the original label rather than substituting a broader construct after the fact, since the item was not designed to distinguish anonymity from non-judgement or permissiveness, and reports the pattern descriptively pending replication with validated multi-item measures.
 4.2 
## Clinical disclosure and implications

The term dependence remains debated in this domain. The qualitative interviews and the trait-level Study 1 survey, however, describe experiences consistent with an emotional-attachment model, anxiety when the chatbot is inaccessible and reliance on it for emotional regulation, rather than a compulsive consumption pattern. Emotional dependence is therefore retained for the Study 1 and Study 2 evidence, reflecting the construct at the trait and habitual level, while the twelve-minute experimental indicators remain early relational proxies rather than established dependence.

Loneliness amplified the design's effect on trust but not on disclosure or dependence, consistent with the dissociation running through the results. The exploratory loneliness interaction did not survive multiplicity correction, so it is not treated as a demonstrated vulnerability marker but at most a hypothesis for confirmatory work ( Pentina et al., 2023 ). A subset of the qualitative findings carries weight exceeding its frequency. Three participants recounted suicidal ideation or self-harm that, by their account, they had disclosed only to a chatbot, and several framed chatbot use as filling a gap left by inaccessible mental health services. Three cases support no prevalence claim; their value lies in establishing that disclosure of clinically salient content to a chatbot in preference to any human does occur, with crisis responses often experienced as scripted or cold when continuity of support was most needed. These observations motivate adequately powered, clinically grounded study rather than regulatory prescription. This misspecification has practical design consequences. If felt empathy and trust mediated the outcomes, interventions such as artificial-origin labelling would attenuate them; the perceptual layer does respond to such cues ( Balaban & Phua, 2026 ), but the present findings show this response does not propagate to behaviour. No safeguard of any kind was manipulated in this research, so no claim is made that existing perception-focused safeguards are ineffective. The findings instead motivate a hypothesis for future safeguard experiments: interventions that reintroduce social presence and accountability at the point of sensitive disclosure may be worth testing alongside, rather than in place of, existing perception-focused approaches.
 4.3 
## Limitations and conclusion

No experiment was prospectively pre-registered, and the strong manipulation check leaves demand characteristics as a rival account that the null advice-compliance result weighs against but does not exclude. The significant remaining direct effect in Study 5 indicates that perceived anonymity and reduced evaluation apprehension do not exhaust the pathway, and a single laboratory operationalisation cannot represent the full construct space. Study 1 was cross-sectional and self-report, so its structural language should be read as the vocabulary of the model rather than a claim of demonstrated causation. The twelve-minute interactions can induce state-level disclosure but are too brief to capture genuine dependence, so the experimental dependence outcomes are best read as early relational indicators. The anthropomorphic condition bundled several co-occurring cues, so the experiments identify the effect of an anthropomorphic-relational package rather than any single cue, and dismantling studies are a natural next step. The English-language online panels leave cross-cultural generalisability of the effect magnitudes for further study, although the twelve-country survey supports the structural pattern, with configural fit of the focal measurement model across cultural clusters (Appendix A); a formal metric-invariance test was attempted but is not reported because it could not be reliably obtained with the available tooling. Taken together, the five studies are more consistent with direct pathways than with the prevailing chain in which felt empathy and trust carry the effect, and a dedicated experiment provided the most defensible interim evidence for online disinhibition as a candidate route. The result invites a re-specification of a long-standing mediational assumption and relocates the mechanism from felt relational warmth to the lowered social risk of the medium. As conversational artificial intelligence integrates into daily life, understanding these pathways will determine how precisely protective interventions can be designed.
