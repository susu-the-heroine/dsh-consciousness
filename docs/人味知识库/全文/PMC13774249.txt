Narrative time is essential for understanding and remembering stories. Reconstructive memory theory posits that retrieving past events is not a mere reactivation of the original memory trace but involves a reorganization process informed by a combination of stored memories, general knowledge, and interpretative elements. Recent studies have shown that humans are remarkably accurate in judging the time-of-occurrence of fragments from a previously encoded narrative, but also that expectations and assumptions can compromise their performance. Here, we investigated the mechanism underlying the ability to infer the time-of-occurrence of videoclips extracted from a previously unencoded movie to elucidate the gradual integration of episodic and semantic information during the construction of narrative time. Across four experiments performed by different groups of human participants, we progressively manipulated the amount of available episodic information for the time-estimation task. Compared with the high precision observed for a known (i.e., previously encoded) movie, a robust decrease in performance was observed in the absence of prior encoding, irrespective of task repetitions. Exposing participants to additional episodic information (movie fragments) between task repetitions produced a gradual enhancement in task performance, especially when episodic cues were presented in chronological order. These results suggest that the temporal information provided by episodic cues can be exploited to gradually form a temporal scaffolding of the narrative, filling in the gaps between encoded pieces of information. This temporal representation, in turn, enables the dating of movie fragments, almost as if the movie had been encoded in its entirety.

## Introduction

Temporal information is a fundamental organizing principle of both episodic memory retrieval and event construction (Tulving, 1983 ). The perception of sequential unfolding can reliably support the formation of structural knowledge (i.e., a general temporal structure) and the subsequent retrieval of event time (i.e., the time of occurrence of specific events; Bellmund et al., 2020 ). Since episodic memories are intrinsically formed from sequences of events, retrieving specific temporal aspects of an event is thought to be associated with the reactivation of specific temporally adjacent information, but also of a more general temporal structure. According to a prominent memory model (Eichenbaum & Cohen, 1988 ; Eichenbaum et al., 1999 ), event sequences are thought to be stored in a relational network of interconnected memory representations. In this network, nodal representations contain information that has been progressively abstracted from the specific episode (Eichenbaum et al., 1999 ). Within this view, therefore, the temporal unfolding of episodic experience is intrinsically associated with an overarching mechanism of across-episode integration working (e.g., through the extraction of statistical regularities across different events). This putative mechanism supports the formation of a “memory space” and the progressive development of schematic/semantic knowledge of events. As is widely recognized in semantic memory research and formally acknowledged in the semantic scaffolding hypothesis (Greenberg & Verfaellie, 2010 ; Irish et al., 2012 ), stored semantic knowledge about prototypical events (scripts and schemas) is, in turn, systematically employed as a scaffold to facilitate both memory encoding and retrieval.

The field of research on memory for complex, life-like materials, such as TV series and movies, has provided crucial insights into the relationship between episodic and semantic components of memory for time. In particular, since the study material is based on chronological arrangements between contextually rich events sharing different degrees of perceptual and semantic proximity (e.g., perceptual details, causal connections, social relationships), the recollection of specific event times entails not only the reinstatement of contextual information associated with the experience but also of semantic representations and conceptual processing that occurred at encoding. The idea that memory for complex life-like events is not a straightforward representation of the original experience but rather a combination of episode-specific information and stored semantic knowledge is central to reconstructive accounts of episodic memory retrieval (Addis & Schacter, 2008 ; Bartlett, 1932 ; Schacter, 1999 ; Yassa & Reagh, 2013 ). Since the seminal work of Bartlett ( 1932 ), a large body of cognitive studies has demonstrated that episodic memories may contain missing elements or erroneous intrusions of schema-relevant information (Mandler & Goodman, 1982 ; Alba & Hasher, 1983 ). The complex interaction between episodic and semantic components of memory representations has been further explored by the research field on event cognition. In particular, according to the event horizon model (Radvansky & Zacks, 2017 ), ongoing experience is segmented into sequences of discrete events by automatically placing event boundaries, and event models are stored in long-term memory based on the causal connections between events. Accordingly, event boundaries have been shown to exert a strong influence on sequence memory, including memory for the order of events, as well as estimates of temporal distance and duration (reviewed in Clewett et al., 2019 ). Therefore, when probing memory for complex events, performance reflects both the activation of event models associated with the specific encoding experience and event schemas associated with general properties of stored similar experiences (Xue, 2022 ).

Consistent with the reconstructive nature of episodic memory, we have recently shown that memory for time of video clips extracted from a previously encoded movie is modulated by event boundaries and script-based prior knowledge about conventional movie scripts, such as the beginning-middle-end template (Frisoni et al., 2021 ). In particular, using a sensitive measure of temporal positioning on a visual analog scale (VAS), we have shown that humans are remarkably accurate in judging the time of occurrence of video clips extracted from a previously encoded movie (Frisoni et al., 2021 ). Following the prediction of the event horizon model (Radvansky & Zacks, 2017 ) and location theories of memory for time (Friedman, 1993 ), we observed more precise temporal judgments for clips extracted in proximity to event boundaries and from the beginning and the end (vs. the middle) movie part. Notably, consistent with the automatic activation of narrative schemas and script-based prior knowledge about conventional movie scripts, we further observed that removing the final part of the encoded movie was associated with a systematic underestimation of the clips’ position. More generally, evidence that memories are moved in time to make room for missing information provides an empirical demonstration that incoming information has been transformed and adapted to fit a standard template of narrative time.

Further behavioral evidence indicates that schema activation appears to be automatic and unintentional (Frisoni et al., 2022 ). Along the same line, by temporally scrambling the movie at encoding, we also showed that positioning errors are consistent with a tendency for a recombination of the movie parts to fit the “story time,” despite the request to indicate the time of occurrence in “viewing time” (Frisoni et al., 2024 ).

In general, experimental evidence about schema-based distortions during memory retrieval and schema-based transformation of memories is widespread and supports the notion that detailed episodic information about complex events is retrieved with reference to semantic/structural knowledge. But how do episodic details combine with semantic knowledge during the construction of narrative time? And what are the features of episodic experience underlying the construction of event models and event schemas? Although schema-based retrieval of episodic memory information has been extensively demonstrated in episodic memory research (e.g., Abbott et al., 1985 ; Migueles & García-Bajos, 2012 ), the progressive integration of episodic details with semantic knowledge during event construction is less known. As discussed above, relational networks are thought to be the semantic scaffolding that guides both the memory reconstruction of past episodes and the inferential reasoning about temporal relations that were not directly encoded. According to influential theoretical perspectives, such a mirroring mechanism of event recollection and event construction would be based on a common simulation system, which operates an integration of elements of sensory experience to create abstract knowledge and structural representations of events (Schacter et al., 2007 , 2008 ). Within this view, therefore, mental representations of past events and future scenarios rely on a common “simulation system” (Addis, 2020 ), based on the same information and governed by the same integration mechanisms. In contrast, “situation models” focus on the representation of the current moment as it unfolds (Zwaan & Radvansky, 1998 ). They selectively encode features relevant to the ongoing event and its direct causal antecedents, forming a localized and continuously updated representation of narrative structure (Barnett & Bellana, 2025 ). Consistent with these models, recent studies have shown that the mnemonic construction of event time closely relies on a mental representation of event temporal structure, which can be generalized across events from different sequences (Bellmund et al., 2022 ).

In the specific case of the construction of narrative time, the chronological unfolding of narrative events and the associated causality chain might be thought of as the building blocks for the creation of a semantic representation of the narrative temporal structure (event models/schemas), which, in turn, can be exploited to infer the story time. On this basis, we predict that a crucial requisite for the successful construction of narrative time is the encoding of episodic information within a coherent and continuous spatiotemporal context. More specifically, following the notion of the generalization of experience in a “memory space” and the semantic scaffolding of episodic experience, we predict that the mnemonic construction of narrative time strictly depends on the representation of episodes as the consequential and causal flow of events across time.

To investigate the gradual process of mnemonic construction of narrative time, in the present study, we tested the effect of manipulating the amount of semantic and episodic information on the participants’ estimates of the time of occurrence (i.e., in story time coordinates) of video clips extracted from a conventional movie (an episode of the TV series 24 ) across multiple task repetitions. The first group of participants performed the time-estimation task after encoding the full movie, thereby exploiting both semantic and episodic information as in previous studies. Then, we minimized the episodic component by asking a different group of participants to date the same video clips with no prior exposure to the movie, which was therefore unknown to them. We predicted this manipulation to negatively affect task performance. In a third experiment, we partially reintroduced fragments of episodic information between task repetitions, in the form of additional video clips, extracted from the same movie (episodic cues) and presented in chronological order. Finally, we tested whether participants could use the same cues, this time presented in random order, to gradually increase the precision of temporal positioning across task repetitions. However, we expect episodic cues to be ineffective due to the lack of a temporal structure.

## Experiment 1: Time estimation after full movie encoding

The first experiment aimed to define a reference measure of task performance that can be achieved in the clip-positioning task, allowing participants to watch the entire movie beforehand. Like our previous studies on memory for time (Frisoni et al., 2021 , 2022 ), participants could therefore exploit both episodic and semantic memory to indicate the time of occurrence of video clips extracted from the encoded movie on a horizontal timeline representing the movie duration. We further assessed whether the temporal precision increased as a function of task repetition by asking participants to perform the same time-estimation task, using the same video clips, always presented in the same random order, three times in a row, with a few minutes break between repetitions. This feature of the paradigm served to establish whether the temporal representation of the movie was stable or still subject to improvements across repetitions.

## Methods

## Participants

Twenty-eight participants (10 men; mean age = 23.89 years) participated in the study. All participants were recruited from the university campus and were either undergraduate or university graduates, indicating a relatively homogeneous educational background. Participants stated that they were not previously exposed to any episode of the TV series used in the present study. Participants were unaware of the purpose of the experiment, reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision, and provided informed consent before the experiment, following guidelines set by the Human Studies Committee of University G. d’Annunzio of Chieti-Pescara, Italy. A sample size of 28 participants per condition was determined a priori based on a power analysis conducted using G*Power, which indicated that this number would achieve 80% of statistical power in the case of a medium effect (effect size of 0.25; alpha level of 0.05; Faul et al., 2007 ).

## Stimuli and procedure

All the audiovisual stimuli used in the present study were obtained from an episode of the commercial TV series 24 (Season 6, Episode 1, Duration: ~40 min) used in previous studies (Kwok et al., 2012 ; Tang et al., 2016 ). This political thriller follows the deeds of an American counterterrorist federal agent in his attempts to prevent terrorist attacks and bring down those responsible. The episode starts with the agent returning to the US after being released from a prison in a prisoner exchange with a terrorist. The episode establishes a new threat (a series of devastating bombings) and introduces new characters and political tension as the American president deals with national security and a looming nuclear threat, while the agent’s return is complicated by his past and the ongoing crisis. Most importantly in the present context, this particular TV show uses the real-time method of narration, according to which each episode, which lasts approximately 40 min, refers to 60 min of real (story) time (and each season, made of 24 episodes, covers 24 consecutive hours). The real-time approach to narration provides a particularly useful naturalistic context for investigating the formation and estimation of temporal representations. Of note, the series also does not use flashbacks, which would have complicated the time-estimation task.

The experimental paradigm included an encoding and a retrieval session conducted one after another in a darkened testing room (Fig.  1 A). Stimuli were presented on a 17-in. LCD computer monitor (1,024 × 768 pixels, 60-Hz refresh rate) positioned at a distance of ~60 cm. Participants wore headphones and listened to the audio track dubbed in Italian at a comfortable level (~60 dB). In the encoding session, the full 24 episode was presented using the software VLC media player, version 2.20. Importantly, this particular TV series is narrated in chronological order, and each episode represents one hour of happenings in narrative time. Participants were instructed to pay attention to the movie but were not informed about the nature of the following task. Fig.1 
Experimental design. The figure illustrates the structure of the paradigm for the four experiments. In Experiment 1 ( A ), participants performed the time estimation task after encoding the whole episode, as in previous studies, with 5-minute breaks between repetitions. In Experiment 2 ( B ), participants performed the time estimation task with no prior exposure to the movie, thereby simply inferring the time of occurrence based on semantic information. In Experiment 3 ( C ), two passive viewing tasks, with different clips, were introduced between repetitions of the time estimation task as cues to help the time estimation task. Crucially, clips were presented in chronological order during the passive viewing task. Experiment 4 ( D ) had the same structure, but this time, cues were not presented in chronological order.

In the retrieval session, 80 video clips of 2-s duration, sampled uniformly every ∼30 s from the encoded episode, were presented using E-Prime 2.0 software (Psychology Software Tools). The retrieval session included three repetitions of the same block of 80 trials, separated by a 5-min pause, in which participants rested quietly in the testing room. In each block, clips were presented in the same random order with respect to the chronological order of the story. Each trial began with the presentation of a video clip at the center of the screen, followed by a centrally presented horizontal grey line (size: ~1° × ~20° visual angle) representing the full movie duration. The timeline was made of 1,000 consecutive segments, each corresponding to 2.37 s of the movie. The task was to indicate the time of occurrence, in story time coordinates, of the presented video clip by clicking with the mouse on the corresponding point of the timeline. A cursor (arrow pointer) was visible and moved in synchrony with the mouse, but the participant was given no numerical feedback while interacting with the visual analogue scale. The timeline remained on the screen until a judgment was given for a maximum of 5 s, followed by an intertrial interval (ITI) of ∼3 s. No feedback about task performance was provided. Participants were instructed to consider the timeline as representing the entire movie duration, where the extreme left corresponded to the beginning, and the extreme right corresponded to the end of the movie. The task was repeated three times, with video clips presented in the same random order. An experimenter made sure that participants understood the instructions during the practice session.

For the practice session, participants were presented with a series of numbers (31 stimuli) at the center of the screen and asked to indicate their corresponding position on a continuous timeline, where the leftmost represented 0, and the rightmost represented 1,000. This task was designed to test the participant’s ability to use the VAS while minimizing additional information about the movie that could influence performance in the subsequent time-estimation task.

## Data analysis

We analyzed time estimation judgments after the exclusion of trials associated with mouse clicks outside the timeline. Time estimation precision was calculated as the temporal distance, quantified in seconds, between the line segment selected by the subject and the correct segment for each trial. Such a measure, hereafter defined as positioning error, was calculated with the center of each video clip as a reference. Two parameters were considered: absolute and relative error. Whereas absolute error refers to general temporal precision, relative error considers the direction of error (forward or backward) and provides an estimate of a systematic overestimation or underestimation bias across participants.

We examined whether task repetition was associated with a significant improvement in time-estimation performance by comparing the mean absolute error in each retrieval block through a one-way repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by post hoc tests with Bonferroni correction. To determine the presence of a systematic bias in the positioning task across subjects, we compared the mean relative error in each repetition condition against zero using one-sample t tests (two-tailed) with Bonferroni correction. In addition to assessing whether time estimation performance varies across task repetitions, we implemented a permutation-based analysis to determine whether temporal estimates in each repetition were above chance. Mean absolute error (MAE) was first averaged across participants to obtain a group-mean error. To generate a null distribution of group-level performance, the clip labels associated with each of a given participant's time estimates were randomly shuffled 5,000 times. For each of the 5,000 permutations, MAE was computed separately for each participant and then averaged across participants to obtain a group-mean error. This procedure resulted in a null distribution of group-mean MAEs expected by chance. In parallel, the same permutation procedure was applied at the single-participant level, whereby each participant’s observed MAE was compared against an individual null distribution generated from 5,000 within-participant label permutations using a one-tailed test. The observed group-mean MAE was then compared with this null distribution, and a p value was computed as the proportion of permuted means that were lower than the true mean error. The permutation analysis was performed in MATLAB R2021a (The MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA) using custom scripts. As for the absolute error, we next assessed the effect of task repetition by comparing the mean relative error associated with each repetition through a one-way repeated-measures ANOVA, followed by post hoc tests with Bonferroni correction.

We also derived an index of memory for the temporal order (i.e., the degree to which the video clips were positioned in the correct order) by computing a trial-by-trial Spearman rank-order correlation test between the estimated (group-level) and the actual order of the movie clips. The correlation was conducted within each block. To perform statistical comparisons across blocks, we calculated single-subject estimates of the same correlation, normalized using Fisher transformation, and performed a one-way repeated-measures ANOVA, followed by post hoc tests with Bonferroni correction. To investigate whether temporal precision was associated with temporal order memory, we computed a trial-by-trial Spearman correlation test between the MAE and temporal order performance within each block.

Additional analyses were conducted after excluding outliers, defined as participants exceeding ±2 standard deviations from the mean in either of the two error measures (absolute and relative). These results are reported in the Supplementary Materials .

All statistical analyses were performed with Jamovi version 2.6.44.

## Results

The mean level of absolute error across the three repetitions was 325 ± 101 s; 322 ± 101 s, and 319 ± 101 s ( M ± SD ), with the error in the last block corresponding to ~5 m (i.e., 14% of the whole movie duration). The ANOVA with repetition as a factor yielded no significant effect, F (2,54) = 0.38, p = 0.68, n 2 = 0.001, indicating a remarkably consistent level of memory precision across repetitions (Fig.  2 A). The permutation-based analysis further confirmed that the group’s temporal estimates were significantly better than chance across all three repetitions ( p <.05; Fig.  2 B). The same permutation analysis at the individual level showed that all participants exhibited above-chance performance in each repetition (100%), suggesting that their performance reflected a reliable ability to construct narrative time. The mean level of relative error across the three repetitions (Fig.  2 C) was −6 ± 144 s; −4 ± 162 s and −22 s ± 146 s ( M ± SD ). The one-sample t tests revealed no significant underestimation or overestimation across subjects in each task repetition (all corrected p values = n.s., Cohen’s d = −0.044, −0.023, −0.149), and the one-way ANOVA with repetition as factor showed no significant differences across blocks, F (2,54) = 1.37, p = 0.264, n 2 = 0.003.

The group-level analysis of order memory conducted in each block indicated a consistent correlation between the estimated and the actual order of clips ( r = 0.81, 0.81, 0.81, all p values < 0.01). Consistent with other indices of temporal precision, the ANOVA with repetition as a factor, performed on the Fisher-transformed correlation values calculated in each individual (Fig.  2 D), indicated no significant modulation of order memory performance across the three repetitions, F (2,54) = 0.392, p = 0.678, n 2 = 0.001. Further analysis conducted in each block revealed a significant negative correlations between absolute error and temporal order performance in the first ( r = −0.44, p = 0.018) and second ( r = −0.42, p = 0.024) repetitions, indicating that greater temporal precision was associated with better order memory, whereas the third repetition showed a weaker, nonsignificant correlation ( r = −0.28, p = 0.15), suggesting a potential stabilization across repetitions.

## Discussion

The results of Experiment 1 closely fit with evidence from previous studies that used the method of the horizontal timeline to calculate temporal memory precision with complex audiovisual material. The percentage of absolute error relative to the movie length (13%, about 5 min) was similar to the one observed with longer material (Frisoni et al., 2021 , 2022 ), suggesting the efficient use of a temporal representation of the movie. Crucially, task performance, as measured in terms of absolute error, relative error, or clip ordering, was unaffected by task repetition, suggesting that the temporal representation that guided the temporal judgments was relatively stable. However, it is also worth noting that greater temporal precision actively supports the reconstruction of event sequences in memory, suggesting that a temporally precise temporal representation appears to sustain the ordered encoding of events in a narrative (Montchal et al., 2019 ). The next experiment aimed at more directly testing the specific contribution of semantic memory for the time of movie scenes.

## Experiment 2: Time estimation of fragments from an unknown movie

As discussed in our previous studies, precise time estimation benefits from the integration between episodic information about encoded information, semantic representations associated with event models (e.g., the segmentation of the narrative in sequences of events) and, finally, script-based prior knowledge about conventional narrative time. The goal of the second experiment was to disentangle the specific contribution of these factors by selectively manipulating the amount of semantic and episodic information during event construction. In particular, while Experiment 1 was based on the time estimation of clips extracted from a previously encoded movie, Experiment 2 investigated the ability of participants to infer the time of occurrence of clips extracted from an unknown movie, under the rationale that the deprivation of episodic memory information allows for the quantification of the specific contribution of semantic representations to the positioning task. The assumption is that partial narrative understanding and semantic schemas may emerge during the task, allowing some temporal predictions based on general event structure and genre conventions (Van Kesteren et al., 2012 ; Zacks & Tversky, 2001 ). To this aim, the same method of the horizontal timeline was employed to assess the accuracy of temporal positioning of the same video clips, this time with no previous exposure to the movie content (Fig.  1 B). As in Experiment 1 , the modulation of task repetition, and the potentially associated consolidation effects were assessed by comparing the accuracy of the time estimation across three repetitions of the positioning task.

## Methods

Twenty-eight new volunteers (17 women; mean age = 25.24 years) participated in the study, with the same inclusion criteria as Experiment 1 . Experiment 2 was a replication of Experiment 1 with the exception that participants did not perform the encoding session. Thus, participants were now asked to infer the time of occurrence of the video clips, in story time coordinates, by selecting a segment of a horizontal timeline representing the duration of the whole movie. Clips were presented in the same random order compared with Experiment 1 (the same for the three task blocks). We performed the same analyses as in the previous experiment.

## Results

The absolute error for each task repetition was 670 ± 79 s, 673 ± 109 s, and 673 ± 108 s (Fig. 3 A), with a positioning error in the last block of about ~10 min (i.e., 28% of the entire movie duration). While the absence of the encoding session resulted in an evident decrease in temporal precision, the one-way ANOVA with repetition as a factor revealed no significant effect, F (2,54) = 0.021, p = 0.980, n 2 = 0.0. While the permutation analysis revealed that participants were still able to perform above chance at the group level ( p <.05) in each repetition, a substantial proportion of individuals showed empirical errors overlapping the permutation-derived null distribution, with 54%, 46%, and 54% of the sample performing at chance in repetitions 1–3, respectively (Fig. 3 B). Fig. 3 
Absolute error ( A ), mean absolute error (MAE) for each participant, with solid bars indicating performance significantly better than chance ( B ), relative error ( C ), and correlation between the estimated and actual order of the video clips ( D ) for the three task repetitions in the “no movie” condition

The severe drop in temporal precision was paralleled by an increase in relative error, which was −87 ± 104 s, −101 ± 129 s, and −81 ± 138 s in the three repetitions, suggesting the presence of an underestimation bias (Fig. 3 C). This impression was statistically confirmed by the one-sample t tests, which indicated a consistent bias in each task repetition (all corrected p values < 0.01, Cohen’s d = −0.833, −0.783, −0.585). The bias was stable across task repetitions, as demonstrated by the lack of a significant effect in the one-way ANOVA, F (2,54) = 0.480, p = 0.622, n 2 = 0.005.

A robust decrease in the correlation between the estimated and the actual position of the clips compared to Experiment 1 was observed in the group-level analysis performed for each task repetition, even if the positive relationship remained significant ( r = 0.19, 0.20, 0.20; all p values < 0.01). The ANOVA conducted on the Fisher-transformed correlation values revealed no significant effect of task repetition, F (2,54) = 0.216, p = 0.807, n 2 = 0.001. The correlation between the absolute error and the order in each block showed no significant effect in any of the three blocks ( r = −0, −0.115, −0.354, all p values > 0.05; Fig. 3 D).

## Discussion

The results from Experiment 2 indicate that the inference about the time of occurrence of the video clips based on semantic information alone results in suboptimal task performance compared with the movie condition. Specifically, the amount of positioning error doubled, a significant underestimation bias appeared, and the correlation between the estimated and the actual position of the clips showed an almost four-fold reduction. Concerning theoretical models of semantic–episodic integration, the present findings indicate that semantic knowledge alone provides limited support when used as a scaffold for a temporal representation of an unknown episode. As a matter of fact, although group-level performance was above chance, the fact that nearly half of the participants still performed at chance level after multiple task repetition suggests that this knowledge does not provide an effective basis for temporal memory. Indeed, script-based prior knowledge about the temporal structure of standard movies and the semantic information extracted from the randomly presented video clips of the unseen movie were not sufficient to support the formation of an efficient temporal structure of the narrative.

Moreover, the lack of a significant improvement in task performance as a function of repetition reinforces the notion that repeated exposition to temporally disorganized information about movie clips is useless for consolidation processes. This interpretation is further supported by the lack of a coherent temporal representation of the narrative, as evidenced by our results. The next experiment aimed to test the effect of reintroducing partial episodic information to promote the interplay between semantic and episodic representations (script-based prior knowledge and semantic extraction from episodic experience) in the time estimation process.

## Experiment 3. Time inference with temporally organized episodic cueing

Following the rationale that accurate temporal memory performance relies on the integration between episodic information about sequences of events and script-based prior knowledge about movie temporal structure, we investigated whether sparse chronological cueing can reliably support performance about temporal positioning of previously unseen movie clips. To this aim, we implemented the same experimental design employed in Experiment 2 , but participants were now provided with additional information (i.e., different video clips) from the same movie between the three task repetitions. Crucially, the additional video clips presented during the passive viewing blocks were arranged in the correct chronological order (Fig.  1 C). We tested whether this cueing procedure is enough to produce a gradual increase in task performance. More specifically, the chronological unfolding of episodic information is expected to support the representation of the causal structure of events, inferential reasoning, and integration with script-based prior knowledge about movie narrative. Such an ongoing integrative mechanism between episodic and semantic information would support the formation of a mental representation of the movie's temporal structure, which can be used for inferring the temporal position of unknown clips.

## Methods

Twenty-eight new volunteers (11 men; mean age = 25.93 years) participated in the study with the same previous inclusion criteria. Like in Experiments 1 and 2 , the clips of the time-estimation task were presented in random order (the same for the three task blocks). However, in contrast to Experiments 1 and 2 , here the rest periods between task blocks were replaced by two 5-min passive viewing blocks, in which a different set of movie clips, belonging to the same unseen TV episode, were displayed, this time in chronological order. The additional clips ( N = 158, ∼2 s duration, N = 79 for each block) were sampled ∼10 se before/after the ones used for the time-estimation task. Specifically, for each clip used in the time-estimation task, a corresponding passive-viewing clip was selected exactly 10 s after (passive viewing #1) and before (passive viewing #2) its occurrence in the episode timeline. Minor deviations from the intended ±10-second offset occurred only in a small number of cases, when the clip did not contain sufficiently coherent or meaningful content. In these instances, the clip was shifted minimally while preserving the closest possible temporal correspondence. The first set of clips differed by an average of 9.99 s ( SD = 0.98 s), whereas the second set differed by an average of 10.18 s ( SD = 0.99 s). During the passive viewing task, participants were instructed to sustain their attention for the entire duration of the block. Data analysis was the same as in the previous experiment.

## Results

As depicted in Fig. 4 A, the mean level of absolute error associated with the three repetitions was 636 ± 61 s, 491 ± 84 s, 439 ± 94 s, with the error in the last block representing 20% of the entire movie duration (~8 min). Crucially, a progressive decrease of the MAE was observed across the three repetitions, suggesting a gradual construction of an efficient narrative temporal structure (Fig. 4 A). This impression was quantified by a significant effect of task repetition in the one-way ANOVA, F (2,54) = 70.9, p < 0.001, n 2 = 0.521, and by the post hoc tests, which indicated a significant difference between the three repetitions (all corrected p values < 0.001). The permutation analysis revealed that participants’ temporal estimates exceeded chance performance ( p < 0.05) in each repetition. The analysis conducted on each participant further showed that, across repetitions, only a small subset of participants performed at chance level. Specifically, 25% of participants fell within the permutation-derived chance distribution in the first repetition, whereas this proportion dropped to 0% in the second repetition and remained as low as 3.6% in the third (Fig. 4 B). Estimates of the mean relative error associated with the three repetitions were −96 ± 164 s, −40 ± 173 s, and 6± 183 s (Fig .4 C). The one-sample t test against zero indicated a significant underestimation bias in the first block (corrected p < 0.05, Cohen’s d = −0.586). which was no longer observed in the second and third block (corrected p > 0.05, Cohen’s d = −0.231, 0.033). Consistently, a one-way ANOVA revealed a significant effect of repetition on relative error, F (2,54) = 3.78, p = 0.029, η 2 = 0.057, although the post hoc analyses revealed that no comparison across repetitions survived Bonferroni correction.

The correlation between the actual and the estimated order of the clips at the group level suggests an increase across task repetitions ( r = 0.28, 0.53, 0.62, all p values < 0.001). Indeed, when the individual Fisher-transformed correlation values in the three repetitions were directly contrasted in the ANOVA (Fig.  4 D), we observed a significant effect, F (2,54) = 65.5, p < 0.001, n 2 = 0.488, with post hoc tests showing a significant difference between all the blocks (all corrected p values < 0.001). Significant negative correlations between absolute error and temporal order performance were observed in the first repetition ( r = −0.90, p < 0.001), the second repetition ( r = −0.92, p < 0.001), and the third repetition ( r = −0.93, p < 0.001), indicating that lower absolute errors were strongly associated with better temporal order memory throughout the task.

## Discussion

In this Experiment, we examined whether episodic information, conveyed through cues presented in chronological order, supports the gradual formation of a temporal representation of the story that guides time estimation judgments. The results provided compelling evidence that the chronological presentation of sparse information about an unseen movie allows the formation of an accurate temporal representation of the movie events. We remark that the temporal precision observed in the final block of Experiment 3 was only ~35% lower than the one observed in Experiment 1 , compared with the ~111% decrease in precision observed for Experiments 2 . This difference highlights that temporal coherence can emerge even when participants are exposed to limited and fragmented narrative material. Furthermore, participants’ estimates exceeded chance expectations, and the proportion of individuals performing at the chance level declined across task repetitions. This pattern suggests that the cueing procedure progressively sharpened the representation of narrative time. In other words, episodic fragments presented in correct order likely provided sufficient structure to integrate semantic knowledge with episodic cues, thereby sustaining above-chance performance for the majority of the sample.

We also note that the total duration of the clips delivered in the passive viewing blocks (316 s) corresponds to a small fraction (13%, about 5 min) of the entire movie duration, suggesting that the cognitive process of forming a temporal representation does not necessarily require access to the full sequence of events. Rather, participants appeared capable of constructing a coherent and relatively accurate representation even from sparse inputs. This ability can be explained by inferential or "completion" mechanisms, based on the integration between fragments of episodic information and preexisting cognitive schemas (Mandler & Goodman, 1982 ). Research on event segmentation has demonstrated that observers spontaneously organize continuous experience into structured event models that preserve causal and temporal relations (Radvansky & Zacks, 2011 ). Similarly, studies of narrative comprehension and situation models have shown that partial cues are sufficient to activate a coherent temporal framework guiding predictions about subsequent events (Magliano & Zacks, 2011 ; Ranganath & Ritchey, 2012 ). The ability to interpolate missing temporal information thus reflects the operation of a hierarchical inferential system, in which episodic fragments are continuously aligned with higher-level narrative templates. This schema-consistent integration not only supports efficient temporal reasoning but may also underpin the subjective sense of continuity in episodic memory (Ezzyat & Davachi, 2011 ).

## Experiment 4. Time inference with temporally scrambled episodic cueing

The goal of Experiment 3 was to explore the dynamic interplay between episodic and semantic information during the construction of narrative time by specifically modulating the temporal properties of the episodic information provided in support of the positioning task. Building on this evidence, Experiment 4 tested whether this effect persists when the temporal coherence of the episodic input is disrupted. Specifically, we implemented the same experimental design employed in Experiment 3 , with the difference that the additional video clips presented during the passive viewing blocks were now presented in random order (i.e., temporally scrambled; Fig.  1 D). This manipulation allowed us to assess whether participants could still use temporally disordered cues to refine their temporal representation of the story and improve time-estimation performance. In other words, we tested whether temporally scrambled episodic information is still useful for semantic integration and scaffolding, as causal inference from prior knowledge, or, otherwise, the lack of a chronological arrangement of the clips disperses the behavioral improvement observed in Experiment 3 .

## Methods

Twenty-eight volunteers (17 women; mean age = 25.21 years) participated in the study following the same inclusion criteria as in previous experiments. Experiment 4 was a replication of Experiment 3 , with the only exception that, during the passive viewing condition, clips were now presented in random order. Data analysis was the same as in previous experiments.

## Results

As depicted in Fig. 5 A, the absolute error for each task repetition was 651 ± 64 s, 647 ± 110 s, and 597 ± 136 s, with a level of precision in the final block representing 24% of the entire movie (about 9 min), close to that observed in Experiment 2 . The one-way ANOVA revealed a significant effect of task repetition, F (2,54) = 5.11, p = 0.009; n 2 = 0.052, with post hoc tests indicating a significant decrease of absolute error between the second and third repetition (corrected p = 0.047). The permutation analysis indicated that participants’ estimates were significantly more accurate than chance ( p <.05) in each repetition. However, the proportion of participants performing at chance level remained relatively stable across repetitions, with 32% of the sample falling within the permutation-derived null distribution in the first repetition, 32% in the second, and 29% in the third (Fig. 5 B). Absolute error ( A ), mean absolute error (MAE) for each participant, with solid bars indicating performance significantly better than chance ( B ), relative error ( C ), and correlation between the estimated and actual order of the video clips ( D ) for the three task repetitions in the “temporally scrambled cueing” condition

The relative error in the three repetition blocks was −83 ± 129 s, −164 ± 152 s, −172 ± 133 s, suggesting the presence of an underestimation bias (Fig. 5 C), as observed in Experiment 2 . This qualitative impression was statistically supported by the one-sample t tests, which revealed a significant bias in all task repetitions (all corrected p values <.01, Cohen’s d = −0.646, −1.083, −1.287). Interestingly, a significant effect of repetition, F(2,54) = 7.39, p = 0.001, n 2 = 0.080, was observed in the one-way ANOVA, with post hoc tests indicating a significant increase of the underestimation bias from the first to the third task block (corrected p < 0.05).

Similar to Experiment 3 , the group-level analysis of order memory conducted in each block indicated a correlation between the estimated and the actual order of clips ( r = 0.24, 0.29, 0.37, all p values < 0.05). The ANOVA conducted on the Fisher-transformed correlation values revealed a significant effect of task repetition, F (2,54) = 8.22, p < 0.001, n 2 = 0.100, with the post hoc tests indicating a significant increase in the correlation values between the first and the last repetition (corrected p = 0.05). A correlation between the absolute error and the order illustrated a significant negative correlation across all three repetitions ( r = −0.71, r = −0.87, r = −0.93, all p values < 0.001; Fig. 5 D).

## Discussion

A notable result of Experiment 4 is that the additional information provided in random order during the passive viewing phases had a positive impact on the participants’ ability to infer the time of occurrence of the video clips, both in terms of temporal precision and order memory. Consistent with the prediction, therefore, our results suggest that semantic scaffolding can provide a compensatory mechanism also in the presence of fragmented and scrambled episodic information about narrative time. One possible interpretation for this effect is that participants’ temporal judgments reflected a broader narrative representation shaped by prior knowledge and schematic structure, rather than a direct reliance on the presented clip. However, the size of the behavioral improvement was weaker compared to that of Experiment 3 . Furthermore, although participants as a group demonstrated greater accuracy than random responding, approximately one-third of the sample consistently produced chance-like estimates, also in the last task repetition. Overall, the results suggest that scrambled episodic cues are only partially helpful for time estimation, as they can be reorganized into a coherent schema by some, but not all, participants. Furthermore, the results of Experiment 4 further suggest that the cueing procedure, if administered in the absence of a coherent temporal structure, can also distort the temporal representation, as the underestimation bias increased across task repetitions.

## Comparison between experiments

To directly compare the time estimation performance across the four experiments, mixed-design ANOVAs were conducted with repetitions (the three task blocks) as the within-subject factor and condition (full movie, no movie, temporally scrambled, temporally ordered) as the between-subject factor for the measures of absolute error, relative error, and correlation between estimated and correct order of the clip, followed by Bonferroni post hoc tests. In these analyses, we expected the full-movie condition (green boxes) to be the easiest, since participants were exposed to the maximum amount of episodic information, whereas the “no movie” condition (yellow boxes) was expected to be the most difficult, since no additional episodic information was provided. Note that, to help the interpretation of the associated figures, the pattern of the chronological cueing condition was now displayed as the last one (rightward in the figure).

For the absolute error (Fig. 6 A), the results showed a significant main effect of repetition, F (2,216) = 35.9, p < 0.001, η 2 = 0.023, and condition, F (3,108) = 92.3, p < 0.001, η 2 = 0.624, and a significant interaction, F (6,216) = 21.2, p < 0.001, η 2 = 0.041. The post hoc tests on the interaction effect revealed that all the conditions with no prior exposure to the movie ( Experiments 2 – 4 ) showed the same disruption of performance compared to the full-movie condition in the first task repetition (all p values < 0.001). However, the temporally ordered cueing condition showed a significant increase in performance compared to both the no-movie and the temporally scrambled conditions in the following task repetitions (all p values < 0.001). For the relative error (Fig. 6 B), the ANOVA revealed no significant main effect of repetition, F (2,216) = 0.390, p = 0.678, η 2 = 0.001, a significant main effect of condition, F (3,108) = 5.62, p = 0.001, η 2 = 0.097, and a significant interaction effect, F (6,216) = 5.154, p < 0.001, η 2 = 0.035. Post hoc analyses on the interaction effect indicated no significant difference across conditions in the first task repetition (all p values = n.s.). However, the temporally scrambled condition started to show an increase of temporal distortion compared to the full-movie condition in the second repetition ( p < 0.01), and the difference was present also in the final repetition ( p < 0.05). Crucially, in the third repetition, a significant difference was also observed between the temporally scrambled and the temporally ordered conditions ( p < 0.005), which was always comparable with the full-movie condition across repetitions (all p values = n.s.).

Regarding the correlation between the estimated and the actual order of the clips (Fig. 6 C), the ANOVA showed a significant main effect of repetition, F (2,216) = 43.00, p < 0.001, η 2 = 0.022, and condition, F (3,108) = 139.00, p < 0.001, η 2 = 0.709, and a significant interaction effect, F (6,216) = 20.60, p < 0.001, η 2 = 0.031. As for the absolute error, the post hoc tests revealed that all the conditions with no prior exposure to the movie differed from the full-movie condition in the first task repetition (all p values < 0.001). However, the temporally ordered condition showed a significant increase in performance compared with both the no-movie and the temporally scrambled conditions in the following task repetitions (all p values < 0.001), which never differed from each other (all p values = n.s.).

Finally, to better characterize the pattern of time estimation performance across conditions and task repetitions, we conducted two additional analyses. Firstly, as detailed in the Supplementary Materials , we examined the consistency of the placement of the same clip across task repetitions (1 vs. 2 and 2 vs. 3), regardless of the clip’s true position. As shown in Supplementary Fig.  6 , the results showed that, in cases of no or incomplete movie encoding ( Experiments 2 – 4 ), a large re-adjustment was observed from the first to the second task repetition, and was followed by a relative stabilization of the temporal judgments from the second to the third repetition.

Secondly, in absence of a direct measure of clip’s centrality to assess potential difference in time estimation performance between clips with different degree of centrality in the story (see related work on narrative centrality; e.g., Lee & Chen, 2022 ; Upadhyayula et al., 2025 ), we derived a proxy of centrality based on the performance in the benchmark movie condition ( Experiment 1 ). As detailed in Supplementary Fig.  7 , the results of this analysis indicated that clips placed more accurately and consistently across participants in the benchmark movie condition were also better positioned in Experiment 2 – 4 , suggesting higher saliency or centrality to the story, also in absence of a movie encoding session.

## Discussion

The between-subject ANOVA allowed us to directly compare the effect of the experimental manipulation across multiple time points. The analysis revealed that the availability and type of episodic information had a strong impact on the ability to reconstruct narrative time. Across the three dependent variables, the main effect of condition was highly significant, characterized by large effect sizes, and was always greater than the mere effect of task repetition. In particular, while the full-movie condition outperformed all the other conditions in the first task repetition, as expected, the temporally ordered cueing condition began to approximate the full-movie condition more closely with each repetition, likely because this condition allowed participants to gradually reconstruct the story timeline, which, in turn, could be exploited to better position the target stimuli on the visual analogue scale (VAS). In contrast, the temporally scrambled cueing condition showed less, or no, improvement compared with the no-movie condition, which represented the negative benchmark in this study, bolstering the idea that exposure to episodic fragments lacking temporal structure, even if repeated in time, is insufficient, or even disruptive (see the analysis of relative error), for efficiently constructing narrative time. Thus, it is not just the amount of episodic information available that counts, but how well the available information supports the coherence of the underlying temporal structure.

## General discussion

The present study explored the cognitive mechanisms underlying the progressive construction of narrative time. Using a time-estimation task in which judgments were reported using a VAS, we investigated how semantic and episodic information are progressively integrated to construct the temporal structure of a narrative movie. Across four experiments with different groups of participants, we examined how time estimation was influenced by the quantity and quality of encoded narrative information (full-movie encoding vs. exposure to sparse clips extracted from the movie, temporally ordered vs. scrambled presentation). Consistent with previous studies, high precision was observed following full-movie encoding ( Experiment 1 ). Performance declined in the absence of prior encoding ( Experiment 2 ) or when only temporally disorganized cues were provided ( Experiment 4 ). In contrast, the exposure to temporally ordered cues ( Experiment 3 ) allowed a progressive improvement in time estimation as a function of task repetition, suggesting the gradual construction of narrative time.

## Reconstructive mechanisms for episodic memory

A long-standing notion in memory research is the time-dependent consolidation of memory representations. Episodic memory recall has been traditionally explained by the reactivation of neural representations from the original encoding experience (Renoult et al., 2019 ; Rugg et al., 2015 ). However, a wealth of experimental evidence on false memories and memory distortions, as well as the recent advances in memory research using complex life-like events, has progressively transformed our conceptualization of memory consolidation. Indeed, a recent paradigm shift, as reviewed by Xue ( 2022 ), has moved from emphasizing the strengthening of memory traces with time and memory reactivation (the so-called reinstatement) to a transformative view of memory consolidation. This “reconstructive” account posits that memories evolve from episodic and context specific to more semantic or schematic forms, a process called “semantization” or “schematization.” In this framework, episodic memory retrieval is viewed as a transformative process towards a “situational model” that combines the contextual detail of the original encoded event with semantic knowledge from similar past events (scripts or schemas). Accordingly, research in rodents has shown that consolidation is accelerated when new information is integrated into these preexisting schemas (Tse et al., 2007 ). Similarly, evidence from human memory studies indicates that prior knowledge influences memory acquisition, consolidation, and retrieval (Gilboa & Marlatte, 2017 ).

Since episodic memory is typically organized around event sequences rather than isolated items, time plays a central role in structuring memory. According to the reconstructive model of memory for time (Friedman, 1993 ), memory for time is not based on distinct temporal codes or a chronologically organized memory store but it emerges from the integration of episodic memory with general knowledge about time patterns and conventional time locations, reflecting a process of reconstruction that gradually incorporates various types of semantic and episodic information. This gradual progression enables the situation model to be updated incrementally with minimal reorganization, thereby supporting more accurate encoding of temporal relations and more precise estimates of temporal distance (Rinck & Bower, 2000 ; Zacks et al., 2009 ; Zwaan, 1996 ; Zwaan & Radvansky, 1998 ). In contrast, when events are presented in a nonlinear order, the cognitive system must internally reconstruct the underlying chronology, which increases processing demands and reduces the precision of temporal judgments. Under such conditions, individuals must infer missing temporal relations and realign events within the causal–temporal structure of the narrative (Rinck & Bower, 2000 ; Zwaan, 1996 ), resulting in impaired accuracy of temporal–distance representations, as showed in several studies (Claus & Kelter, 2006 ; Kelter et al., 2004 ). Although these studies do not directly examine time estimation judgments, they emphasize the construction and accessibility of narrative time, underscoring the importance of the way the material is presented across events. For instance, greater accessibility of narrative time during chronological cueing ( Experiment 3 ) facilitates more precise temporal judgments compared with nonchronological cueing ( Experiment 4 ). Indeed, as demonstrated above, participants who were progressively exposed to coherent temporal information showed greater accuracy in the time-estimation task compared with those who lacked exposure to temporally coherent episodic information, particularly during the last task repetition. Furthermore, the present results suggest that the presence of a structured event sequence and narrative context facilitates the integration of episodic details into existing temporal schemas (Friedman, 1993 ).

Similarly, using the method of the horizontal timeline, in previous work, we found that time-estimation judgments are influenced by event segmentation and script-based knowledge of narrative structure. Behaviorally, precision was higher for events near boundaries, and removing a movie segment introduced a bias reflecting the intrusion of the missing part (Frisoni et al., 2021 , 2022 ). Additionally, disrupting the temporal order of the narrative by presenting participants with a scrambled/nonlinear movie impaired memory and caused a positioning bias towards the original sequence, supporting the notion of automatic reshaping and chronological reconstruction of temporal memory (Frisoni et al., 2024 ). Analogously, here we showed that the temporally scrambled cueing condition showed growing bias over time, potentially reflecting interference or misattribution effects in the absence of coherent scaffolding.

Overall, research supports the idea that prior knowledge actively shapes memory encoding and retrieval by providing a framework for new information. Depending on the alignment between the encoded sensory information and the semantic representation, this semantic scaffolding can either enhance or bias memory performance, depending on the circumstances.

## Semantic scaffolding and temporal memory

The findings from Experiment 2 support the idea that semantic knowledge facilitates temporal judgments only when it can be used as a scaffolding for episodic information at encoding. This suggests that semantic schemas require appropriate cues for activation, and when these cues are insufficient or too complex, schematic knowledge may lose its value. Moreover, the chronological order of information affects its integration into preexisting schemas. Neuroimaging research (Van Kesteren et al., 2010 ) showed that disrupting a narrative’s natural sequence (via a scrambled film) increased hippocampal–vmPFC connectivity during later encoding, suggesting a compensatory assimilation mechanism. However, this effort correlated with poorer memory for the overarching storyline, underscoring the importance of temporal coherence in schema-based integration. Similarly, Baldassano and colleagues ( 2018 ) found that naturalistic perception activates structured event representations in regions of the so-called default mode network. Yet, when scripts were scrambled, the effects in mPFC were reduced, indicating sensitivity to global temporal structure. This supports the idea that prior knowledge about event sequences is integrated into a coherent “situation model” (Zadbood et al., 2017 ).

These results suggest that semantic support for temporal memory depends on structured encoding material. In our previous study (Frisoni et al., 2022 ), participants automatically reconstructed disordered movie sequences into a linear representation. However, the amount to which a severe disruption (e.g., denser scrambling) affects this process remains an open question. Moreover, the lack of significant improvement in task performance with repetition in Experiment 2 reinforces the idea that repeated exposure to temporally disorganized information is ineffective for consolidation, suggesting that a coherent temporal structure is essential for memory stabilization. Additionally, even when additional information is provided without a coherent temporal structure, it can be detrimental to task performance, further highlighting the critical role of temporal coherence in memory construction and retrieval. Representations need to be stable across moment-to-moment fluctuations in perceptual input but need to be updated when they are no longer appropriate (Zacks et al., 2007 ).

We also note that the total duration of the clips delivered in the passive viewing blocks (316 s) corresponds to a small fraction (13%) of the entire movie duration (~2,370 s; see Experiment 3 ), suggesting that forming a temporal representation does not require access to the full sequence of events. Participants were able to construct a coherent representation from sparse inputs, likely through inferential or "completion" mechanisms that integrate episodic fragments with preexisting cognitive schemas (Mandler & Goodman, 1982 ). The effectiveness of such a process relies on the use of structured cues—prototypical and essential elements—that serve as a minimal template for constructing a coherent event representation, emphasizing the importance of organized, schematic knowledge in temporal cognition.

## Working memory and online narrative assembly

There is evidence that events separated in time are remembered more accurately when they form a coherent narrative (Cohn-Sheehy et al., 2022 ). This suggests that people can establish meaningful connections between nonadjacent events at encoding, as proposed by the event horizon model (Radvansky & Zacks, 2014 , 2017 ). Prominent theories of text comprehension similarly emphasize that readers strive to integrate events into a cohesive causal chain by constructing and continually updating abstract mental models of the unfolding situation (Gernsbacher, 1990 ; Kintsch, 1998 ; Trabasso & Sperry, 1985 ; Zwaan & Radvansky, 1998 ; Zwaan et al., 1995 ). In the absence of a movie encoding condition (as in Experiments 2 – 4 ), when participants are presented with random clips that must be positioned on the timeline, they likely infer the time of occurrence by integrating incoming information into a working model of the movie, which is continually subjected to modification and updating. Thus, in the absence of episodic cues, this inference process strongly depends on working-memory capacity and executive functions (Baddeley, 2000 ; Miyake et al., 2000 ), especially in the first repetition of the task. Consequently, task performance not only reflects the ability to extract semantic information from sparse cues but also individual differences in working memory capacity and executive functions in general.

## Temporal memory error and duration proportionality

Across the present study ( Experiment 1 ) and previous studies employing longer materials (Frisoni et al., 2021 ), temporal precision, measured in terms of absolute error, appears to be proportional (about 10–15%) to the entire movie duration. This suggests that temporal memory errors scale with the length of encoded content, in line with the scalar timing theory (Gibbon et al., 1984 ). Such proportionality supports the efficient maintenance of temporal representations across different timescales, extending principles of temporal perception research beyond shorter durations (i.e., seconds). Crucially, task performance in Experiment 1 , whether measured by absolute error, relative error or clip ordering, remained stable across repetitions, suggesting that the temporal representation guiding judgments was robust, once formed. This stability may reflect the efficient integration of episodic and semantic elements into a cohesive temporal framework, which, once established, does not need further refinement. Rather than benefiting from consolidation or reconsolidation, as observed in other domains of memory, the temporal template appears to be immediately utilized at retrieval and to remain unchanged in the short term (see Frisoni et al., 2022 ). This finding, consistent with previous work, supports the idea that temporal representations are constructed and reused in a parsimonious manner, potentially constrained by the amount of available information.

## Conclusion

According to constructive memory theories, the recall of specific encoding contexts is based on constructive mechanisms that combine episodic and semantic information, yielding both detrimental and beneficial effects on memory performance. In the domain of temporal memory, this mechanism is exemplified by the ability to date when a past event occurred, even when the specific event time is not part of the original memory. Here we show that the construction of narrative time for an unfamiliar story relies on a specular process of generalization of episodic experience. In particular, we provide experimental support for the idea that the representation of the narrative temporal structure relies on the encoding of temporally organized experience about the chronological unfolding of events. These findings support the notion that mnemonic construction, like memory retrieval, relies on structural knowledge of narrative time that is acquired through the integration and recombination of episodic memory sequences. In summary, these findings suggest that structural knowledge of narrative time provides a scaffold not only for memory retrieval of event time, but also for the construction of event time.

## Study limitations

One limitation of the current study concerns the absence of subjective measures of participants’ engagement or narrative absorption. Although the experimental design allowed us to characterize how dynamic audiovisual events shape temporal estimation, the lack of validated experiential indices (such as immersion or transportation scales) prevents us from assessing whether individual differences in engagement contributed to the observed effects. It remains unclear whether the observed distortions reflect solely stimulus-driven dynamics or also depend on participants’ experiential involvement, an ambiguity that limits the interpretability of individual differences and constrains the generalizability of our findings to real-world narrative contexts. For example, participants may have varied substantially in the degree to which they attended to or became immersed in the unfolding narrative, and this unmeasured heterogeneity may have influenced their temporal judgments.

A second limitation concerns the lack of a direct measure of “centrality” and a test of its potential effect on time estimation performance. More specifically, whereas previous work has defined “centrality” in terms of the conceptual or causal importance of events within a story (e.g., Lee & Chen, 2022 ; Upadhyayula et al., 2025 ), here we adopted a data-driven proxy of “centrality” based on the precision level in the benchmark movie condition ( Experiment 1 ). Albeit the two measures are likely correlated, we acknowledge that this metric does not directly reflect the notion of “centrality” as discussed in prior work (Lee & Chen, 2022 ; Upadhyayula et al., 2025 ). Future work should therefore include a direct and independent assessment of clip centrality.
