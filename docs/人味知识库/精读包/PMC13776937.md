## Abstract

Testosterone is an essential endogenous hormone in women, exerting physiological actions in reproductive and non-reproductive tissues. Despite the increased demand for the use of testosterone for an array of menopausal symptoms beyond sexual functions, there is a lack of robust evidence on its impact on cardiovascular and musculoskeletal health and cognition. The aim of this review is to provide an overview of evidence-based indications of testosterone as part of menopausal hormone replacement therapy (HRT) in women and highlight the uncertainties, evidence gaps, and future research priorities. This narrative review synthesised current evidence on the physiological role of testosterone, indications for testosterone as part of HRT, clinical efficacy, formulation and prescribing, and safety profile. Testosterone declines across the adult female lifespan until the late sixth decade, and the menopausal transition itself has no meaningful independent impact on testosterone concentrations. Transdermal preparations maintaining testosterone levels in the physiological range are generally safe, with potential side effects of acne and hair growth. The existing high-quality evidence, including national and international guidelines, recommends that hypoactive sexual desire disorder (HSDD) in postmenopausal women is the only evidence-based indication for use; the evidence does not support use for any other symptom, nor for disease prevention.

## Discussion

While the majority of the research in the arena of hormone replacement therapy has primarily focused on oestrogen, a parallel line of evidence has explored the role of testosterone in sexual health, neuroprotection, cognitive performance, cardiometabolic protection, and bone health.

The postmenopausal stage with intact ovaries does not automatically translate into a state of androgen insufficiency. Testosterone declines across the adult female lifespan and starts increasing slightly at age 58–59 years, the menopausal transition having no meaningful impact on testosterone concentrations ( Wang et al. 2025 a ). In addition, there is no universally established definition of ‘androgen deficiency’, and as a result the decision on testosterone replacement therapy should be based on symptoms and signs, as recommended by evidence-based guidelines ( Wierman et al. 2014 , Davis et al. 2019 , Parish et al. 2021 ). Women on testosterone replacement should have baseline testosterone levels checked using an accurate and sensitive assay to ensure it is in the premenopausal physiological range and to avoid the risk of hyperandrogenism with overuse. The existing evidence, including national and international guidelines, does not support the use of testosterone for women for any symptom or condition other than postmenopausal HSDD or for disease prevention.

Although mechanistic and observational studies have found some positive associations between testosterone levels and improvements in cardiovascular and musculoskeletal health, as well as cognitive function, these associations should not be extrapolated to therapeutic benefit of testosterone.
