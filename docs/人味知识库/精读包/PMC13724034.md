## Abstract

Language impairment is a core clinical manifestation of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) that profoundly affects autonomy, decision-making, and social participation. Building upon the 2015 review by Kuca et al., this narrative review synthesizes empirical evidence from 2016 through 2026. A structured Web of Science Core Collection search retrieved 154 records. Following screening, 66 records were excluded and 88 full-text reports were appraised, of which 13 were consolidated, yielding 75 cited core empirical studies alongside foundational diagnostic, neuropathological, and trial benchmarks. Four main areas are synthesized. First, progressive linguistic decline across clinical stages is characterized, delineating typical amnestic AD dementia, mild cognitive impairment due to AD, and the logopenic variant of primary progressive aphasia (lvPPA), highlighting the necessity of biological amyloid and tau biomarker confirmation. Second, automated speech and natural language processing biomarkers have emerged as scalable tools predicting progression to dementia and detecting biological amyloid-beta positivity; however, real-world translation remains constrained by demographic bias, cross-linguistic variations, acoustic sensitivity, and privacy issues. Third, non-pharmacological interventions, including structured language stimulation and naming therapy, demonstrate measurable task-specific improvements; nevertheless, gains are largely restricted to trained items with minimal transfer to everyday discourse. Caregiver communication training programs reliably improve dyadic interactions and mitigate caregiver distress.
