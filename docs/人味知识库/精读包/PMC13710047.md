## Abstract

The transition from menstruation to endometrial regeneration is a dynamic biological interval involving inflammatory resolution, immune-cell regulation, epithelial repair, vascular remodeling, and endocrine-dependent tissue renewal. Although these processes are individually well established, it remains unclear whether measurable variation in the peri-menstrual endometrial immune transition predicts subsequent endometrial differentiation, receptivity, or implantation within the same cycle. This unresolved question defines the primary focus of the present framework. This article proposes the Immunological Purity Theory, a testable conceptual framework in which immunological purity refers to the transition from menstrual-associated inflammatory and reparative activity toward a regulated endometrial state compatible with subsequent receptivity. The term does not denote sterility, absence of immune activity, complete absence of inflammation, or an established clinical condition. The core hypothesis proposes that interindividual and intraindividual variation in the timing, coordination, or characteristics of this immune–repair transition may prospectively relate to the subsequent trajectory of endometrial regeneration, hormone-dependent differentiation, receptivity, and implantation. The framework does not assume that menstrual inflammation persists until implantation. Rather, it proposes a testable within-cycle relationship between an early endometrial immune–repair phenotype and the later receptive state of the same tissue. This distinction is important because menstruation occurs during the early follicular phase, whereas implantation occurs during the mid-secretory phase. ## Discussion

The Immunological Purity Theory proposes a testable framework for investigating whether measurable variation during the peri-menstrual endometrial immune transition has prospective relevance for subsequent receptivity and implantation. The principal contribution of the framework is therefore not the assertion of a new established causal pathway, but the identification of a specific and falsifiable question situated between menstrual immunobiology and later endometrial function.

The revised framework substantially narrows the central proposition. Menstruation and implantation are temporally separated, and the hypothesis does not assume persistence of menstrual inflammation until implantation. Instead, it proposes that measurable characteristics of the early immune–repair transition may reflect or predict the subsequent trajectory of endometrial regeneration, endocrine-dependent differentiation, and acquisition of the receptive phenotype. This distinction converts the proposed link from an assumed biological continuity into an empirical relationship that can be directly tested.

Preliminary human evidence supports the feasibility of investigating this relationship but does not validate it. Menstrual material can contain biologically informative molecular signals, and menstrual-blood-derived endometrial cells can retain functional characteristics relevant to endometrial differentiation and receptivity ( 11 , 12 ). These observations justify prospective investigation of an early-cycle phenotype but do not establish that such a phenotype predicts implantation or has developmental consequences.
