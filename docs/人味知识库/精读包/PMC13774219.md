## Abstract

Previous research shows that remembering repeated events differs from remembering single events, yet little research has examined suggestibility in adults’ memory for repeated events. In the present experiment, we investigated the effects of eyewitness misinformation and the role of a warning against misinformation in a repeated-event scenario. We examined how different types of misinformation – entirely new items versus items present in non-target instances – influence reporting. Three hundred and nine participants watched four surveillance-mission videos that shared a general story structure but differed in key details. Participants were later exposed to pieces of misinformation in a video narrative of someone else reporting the first mission. Reporting was measured using free recall, cued recall, and recognition. We replicated the standard misinformation effect in the repeated-event context. We found that the warning reduced misinformation reporting, but the effect was present only in the recognition task. Based on the source-monitoring framework, we expected it would be easier to resist external (entirely new) misinformation than misinformation consistent with what was present in non-target instances, and this was also true. The warning’s lack of efficacy may be accounted for in two ways. First, the inherent difficulty of attributing details to specific instances of repeated events may have overshadowed the potential effects of the warning. Second, ours was a general warning; it might not have been strong enough to restore an adequate understanding of a memory task distorted by misinformation.

## Discussion

We tested each hypothesis in three memory tasks: free recall, cued recall, and recognition. As we indicated above, the free-recall data were the least conclusive since many of the items could not be included in the analyses because participants did not report them. Therefore, from the perspective of evaluating the hypotheses, the cued-recall and recognition tests were more informative, with the cued-recall task providing a more ecologically valid measurement.

The present study successfully replicated the standard misinformation effect in the context of repeated events, with misled items yielding significantly fewer correct responses compared to non-misled items across all three memory tests, providing support for H1. Additionally, the repetition of original information enhanced memory accuracy relative to control items in each of the memory tasks (H5 supported). However, contrary to predictions, internal and external misinformation did not differ in their impact on correct responding (H2a not supported). We expected the internal misinformation presentation to result in higher rates of misinformation endorsement than external misinformation (H2b). This was supported only in cued recall; analyses of recognition were inconclusive. Below, we elaborate on why the effect occurred only in the recall task.

One of our main predictions concerned the positive effect of warning on responding to items targeted with misinformation. The only significant warning effect emerged in the recognition test, where warned participants showed reduced misinformation endorsement compared to not-warned participants (H3b supported).
