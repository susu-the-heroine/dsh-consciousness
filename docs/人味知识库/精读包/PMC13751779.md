## Abstract

Larry Jacoby was one of the most creative experimental psychologists of his generation. I describe four papers from early in his career that were influential to debates at the time and that are still well worth reading today. These are Jacoby and Bartz ( Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior, 11 [4], 561–565, 1972 ), Jacoby (( Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior, 17 [6], 649–667, 1978 ), Jacoby and Dallas ( Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 110 [3], 306–340, 1981 ), and Jacoby ( Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior, 22 [5], 485–508, 1983 ). The last two papers showed that dissociations among memory measures, analogous to those observed in amnesic patients (relative to controls), could be obtained in college students. These are just a selection of his famous findings, which include how people can become famous overnight (Jacoby et al., Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 56 [3], 326–338, 1989 ), how to deceive the elderly (Jacoby, Cognitive Neuropsychology, 16 (3/5), 417–436,  1999 ), and, of course, the process dissociation procedure (Jacoby, Journal of Memory and Language, 30 [5], 513–541, 1991 ). Jacoby’s influence on cognitive psychology and related fields will endure.

## Conclusion

Larry Jacoby was a master experimentalist. In this brief encomium, I have provided four examples from his early research. The contributions reported here are necessarily selective but highly influential; the last three have each been cited more than 1,000 times as of this writing, and the Jacoby and Dallas ( 1981 ) paper has been cited more than 4,000 times. These papers reported results that mirrored the dissociations observed in amnesic patients, as Jacoby and Witherspoon ( 1982 ) emphasized in another paper cited over a thousand times. I was tempted to extend my range to 1989, because he had three papers and a chapter, all published that year, each cited more than 1,000 times. Larry Jacoby was a great psychologist and an interesting and complex man. His contributions to cognitive psychology and other fields will be influential for many years to come.

My intellectual debt to Jacoby should be clear from this article, but it runs much deeper than is indicated here. I followed his work carefully from the early 1970s until his retirement in 2015. We were colleagues at Washington University from 2004 until his retirement, and I marveled at how he continued to design novel experiments that yielded important insights. He has trained a cadre of students who have followed in his footsteps, making their own important contributions. Larry and I had many delightful conversations at professional meetings in our early days and, much more often, after we became colleagues. We never managed to collaborate much. We are co-authors on two papers, and I also have one with his son, Derek, who was an undergraduate at Rice University while I taught there (Roediger et al., 1996 ). I feel confident that Larry Jacoby’s work and influence will long endure.
